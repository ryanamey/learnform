## Meter changes do formal jobs

Biamonte's central claim is that metric dissonance in rock isn't decoration. It does **formal work**, small-scale and large-scale, much like harmonic tension in Classical form <Source book="biamonte" />. She separates three levels: rhythm (inside the beat), meter (the bar), and hypermeter (groups of bars, usually 4). Then she sorts dissonances by what they *do*.

<KeyIdea>Put metric dissonance where a section begins, joins, ends or contrasts — not everywhere. Where it sits is what makes it form.</KeyIdea>

## Small-scale functions

| Function | What happens | Examples Biamonte cites |
|---|---|---|
| **Initiating** | Two types: an odd bar at the start (an extra beat, usually keeping the beat length), or a "metric fake-out" where the listener guesses the downbeat wrong and corrects it when the voice or harmony enters | The Beatles, "Martha My Dear" (odd opening bar); The Cars, "Just What I Needed" (fake-out) |
| **Linking (partial bar)** | A truncated bar at a section join, most often a 2/4 bar in 4/4. Either an arrival on beat 3 is reheard as the next section's downbeat (an elision), or a turnaround bar is simply cut short (a contraction) | Prince, "Purple Rain" (elision); Derek and the Dominos, "Layla" (contraction) |
| **Cadential (hemiola)** | Before a cadence the beat or bar value shifts for two to four bars. In Classical music triple usually turns duple; in rock, 4 usually turns into 3. Shorter groups accelerate; a longer beat works as a written-out slow-down | David Bowie, "Changes" (triple bars plus faster chord changes); Led Zeppelin, "Rock and Roll" (a 3-beat motive, then a 3+3+3+3+2+2 rhythm); The Beatles, "The End" (a true metric modulation into a slower 4/4) |

## Large-scale functions

| Function | What happens | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Loose verse / tight chorus** | Verses are irregular, choruses regular, so the chorus feels like an arrival | Alice in Chains, "Them Bones" (7/4 verse, 4/4 refrain); Rush, "Limelight" |
| **Dissonant middle** | The bridge or development is the most unstable section | Yes, "Roundabout"; Radiohead, "Paranoid Android" |
| **Dissonant frame** | Unstable outer sections around a stable centre | Rush, "YYZ"; Led Zeppelin, "Kashmir"; Pink Floyd, "Money" |

### Notes on the large-scale functions

- **Loose verse, tight chorus** (Temperley's term) is a whole package: the chorus is more regular in meter, harmony, texture and lyric, and louder. It works as both contrast and resolution, which is why it's often repeated at the end as a de facto coda. Biamonte compares it to sonata form with the order reversed: there the tight-knit theme comes first and the loose one second; here the loose verse comes first and the tight chorus second (see *Tight-knit and loose*).
- **Meter can carry meaning.** In Rush's "Limelight" the chorus is in 3/4 while the text is about appearances, then 4/4 when it turns to the reality underneath. In the last chorus the drummer plays in two against the band's three, a direct dissonance that intensifies the final statement.
- **The bridge as development.** Rock bridges share a list of features with sonata developments: middle position, maximum contrast, leaving the home key, more dissonance, wider register, sequence and fragmentation, a looser texture, and an open ending. Irregular or conflicting meter is one more way to make a middle unstable. In Radiohead's "Paranoid Android" the dissonant middle section comes back as the coda, so it's both the climax of the middle and the ending.
- **A stable middle in a dissonant frame** often holds a solo. A soloist wants a regular meter, and the loss of metric interest is made up for by harmony, texture or rhythmic density.

## What the corpus says about Tool

Biamonte tabulated metric dissonance across seven corpora: the Beatles, the Rolling Stones, Hendrix, Led Zeppelin, Radiohead, Tool and 200 songs from *Rolling Stone*'s greatest-songs list <Source book="biamonte" where="§8, Ex. 21" />. **Tool (39 songs from *Undertow* to *10,000 Days*) was the most metrically dissonant corpus.** The chart for Tool (her Example 21) gives the details:

- **Only 5 of 39 songs are metrically consonant** (4 in plain 4/4, 1 in another steady meter).
- **The biggest category is "dissonant throughout": 16 songs.** For Tool, dissonance is the normal state, not the exception.
- **Small-scale functions are everywhere:** initiating dissonances in 13 songs, partial-bar links in 13, cadential hemiolas in 9.
- **Large-scale placement:** a dissonant middle in 11 songs (plus 3 with a dissonant intro and middle, 4 with a dissonant middle and ending), a dissonant ending in 2.
- **Polarity:** tight verse / loose chorus in 6 songs, loose verse / tight chorus in only 1. Tool is the only corpus in the study where the chorus is more often the *less* stable section.
- **By album:** *Ænima* has the most large-scale dissonance (about 60% of its songs), *Lateralus* the most consonance (about a quarter of its songs), and *10,000 Days* has no consonant songs at all.

Read those numbers as a design profile rather than a recipe. When dissonance is the norm, contrast has to come from *degrees* of dissonance: which sections are more regular, where the meter changes predictably and where it doesn't, where the band realigns. In most rock the chorus is the arrival of stability; in Tool it's often the arrival of instability.

The only Tool song the article names is "Lateralus", where meter and syllable counts follow the Fibonacci sequence (Biamonte cites a 2001 interview with the band); she adds that the album as a whole is the least dissonant of the four.

### Dissonance at the level of the whole piece

Pieslak's reading of Meshuggah's 21-minute *I* takes the idea one level up <Source book="pieslak" where="pp. 240–241" />. Two clean-guitar sections divide the piece into three nearly equal parts, while the only two passages with a clear surface meter (a 7/4 opening and a 4/4 near the middle) divide it into two halves. The two-part and three-part readings don't agree, the way two rhythmic layers don't, so the whole form is "dissonant". For a long piece, you can plan the large divisions this way on purpose: one set of landmarks (texture) dividing the piece in three, another (meter, or a returning riff) dividing it in two.

### Who is in the band shapes the meter

Her other corpora suggest something for any band: the metric profile changes with the players. Trio guitarists who combine lead and rhythm roles (Hendrix, Alex Lifeson) tend toward more rhythmic and metric dissonance. Hendrix's metric dissonance dropped when a more straight-ahead drummer replaced a freer one. Radiohead is surprisingly consonant at the bar level because they put their dissonance into rhythm rather than meter. Across all the bands, metric dissonance increases over time.

## How to use it

<Steps>

- **Choose a polarity for each song.** Loose verse / tight chorus means the chorus arrives as relief. Tight verse / loose chorus (Tool) means the chorus arrives as intensification. Decide which one the song needs.
- **Put a partial bar at one join, not every join.** One extra or missing half-bar before the climax works like a pushed downbeat. Used everywhere it stops being noticed.
- **End a section with a hemiola.** Contract (4 → 3) to speed into the next section; expand (3 → 4) to slow into it.
- **Use a dissonant middle as the development.** If the outer sections are fairly regular, the bridge is where the meter can break, and the return feels like coming home.

</Steps>

<ListenerSide grade="moderate">

Biamonte also notes a production habit: when metric dissonance occurs, the consonant bass and drums are often mixed more prominently. She cites listening studies showing that listeners entrain to a regular meter quickly and notice disruptions of it. The listener gets the anchor at the front and the dissonance on top. This fits the "contradict without undermining" principle (Vuust & Witek). It's an observation from records, not an experiment.

</ListenerSide>
