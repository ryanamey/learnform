## The most useful small form you'll learn

Schoenberg describes two standard ways to build a complete theme, the **period** and the **sentence**, and calls the sentence the higher form, because it starts developing almost immediately <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. V, VIII" />. Its basic scheme (8 bars in the practice form) is:

1. **Presentation, bars 1–4.** A basic idea (2 bars), then its immediate repetition, exact or moved to another degree. Repetition makes the idea stick.
2. **Continuation, bars 5–6.** The idea is broken into fragments, units get shorter, sequences push upward. New energy, no new material.
3. **Liquidation and cadence, bars 7–8.** The characteristic features are gradually removed until only neutral residue remains, which no longer asks to be continued. Then the arrival.

Caplin's later terms, *presentation*, *continuation* and *cadential function*, describe the same stages, and he reads them as **beginning, middle and end** <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 2" />. Two refinements from his chapter on the sentence matter for writing:

- **The continuation has four signs, not one**: shorter units, faster chord changes, busier surface rhythm, sequence. They can stand in for each other. A continuation that keeps the two-bar units can still sound like a middle if the chords change twice as fast. See *Formal functions*.
- **Why the presentation doesn't end**: repeating a beginning strengthens the sense of beginning. Only a contrasting idea with closing support can end anything.

Caplin also contrasts the two types' character, quoting Erwin Ratz: the period has "repose in itself", while the sentence has a "forward-striving" character, which is why it suits openings of dynamic pieces <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 3, Ch. 10" />.

<KeyIdea>Present it, repeat it, break it, dissolve it, land. That one gesture drives most riff sections that feel inevitable.</KeyIdea>

## Liquidation: how to make something end

Schoenberg's definition: liquidation gradually eliminates the motive's characteristic features until only uncharacteristic ones remain, ones that no longer demand continuation. Its purpose is to stop the music from extending forever. It usually comes with **shortening**. Two-bar units become one bar, then half a bar.

Belkin links this to **neutral lines**: scale fragments, simple figures, clichés <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 1, Neutral lines" />. A cliché is familiar, so it releases attention. That's why it works at endings, and also why music needs occasional neutral passages to breathe. Wall-to-wall motivic density is exhausting.

In the demo, listen to bars 7–8. The stutter that defined M is gone, the tritone is gone, there's just a falling line, and then a power-chord hit. The *removal* of character is what makes the arrival feel final.

## Why this matters more in rock than in Classical music

In tonal music, cadences do much of the work of ending. Modal, riff-based music has weaker cadences, so the other tools have to carry the job:

- **Fragmentation** builds the energy that a harmonic progression would otherwise provide.
- **Liquidation** creates the sense of ending that a V–I would otherwise provide.
- **Arrival** can be texture, register, a held chord, a drop to silence, or the band entering together.

The scheme stretches to any scale. Caplin counts in **real** bars, not notated ones: in a slow piece one notated bar can hold two real bars, and in a fast one two notated bars can make one <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 2, Finer Points" />. For a band that switches between half-time and double-time feels, that's the right way to count a sentence: by the units the listener feels. The "basic idea" might be a 7/8 riff cycle of 10 seconds, the repetition a transposed or reharmonized cycle, and the continuation a section where the riff is chopped into its first few notes and sequenced up across a minute. It still works if the *functions* are clear: present, repeat, compress, dissolve, arrive.

## Beyond the scheme

Schoenberg is clear that real pieces rarely match the practice form exactly. Extensions come from:
- remote variants that need extra repetition to be understood
- units of unequal size
- insertions

His test is worth stealing: **try removing bars**. If the music still makes sense without them, they're an insertion or extension, and now you know what job they do.

<ListenerSide grade="moderate">

Liquidation works by draining distinctiveness, so attention is freed for what comes next. That matches the prediction view (Vuust & Witek, Module 5): as a pattern becomes fully predictable, it stops demanding attention. Belkin's point that clichés help endings is the same effect. Whether the sentence shape itself is perceived as a unit by rock listeners hasn't been tested; treat it as a strong compositional tool, not a perceptual law.

</ListenerSide>

## Practice

<Exercise title="Write three sentences from one motif">

1. Use a motif from your bank.
2. **Sentence A**, 8 bars in 4/4, following the scheme exactly, like the demo.
3. **Sentence B**, same functions, but each stage longer: a 2-bar idea repeated, then 4 bars of continuation that fragments further each bar.
4. **Sentence C**, same functions in an odd meter or with a displaced repetition.
5. For each, mark where liquidation begins. If you can't find the spot, your ending isn't ending yet.

</Exercise>
