## Form only exists in memory

A phrase can be heard all at once. Anything bigger can't. By the time a section ends, its beginning is already a memory, and the shape of a whole song is something the listener *reconstructs*. Snyder's chapter on form is about what that means for the composer: which changes create sections, what memory keeps, and why some long pieces can be followed and others can't <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 13 Form" />.

<KeyIdea>You don't write a form for the listener to perceive. You write a series of cues for the listener's memory to rebuild one.</KeyIdea>

## What makes a section

Snyder defines a section as a stretch of music held together by **constancy** (some parameters stay within stable limits) and separated from its neighbours by **multiparametric change**. His rule of thumb:

- **One parameter changes** (a transposition, everything louder, a faster tempo): an **articulation within the section**, not a new section.
- **Two parameters** may or may not be enough, depending on which ones and how much.
- **Three or more at once**: almost certainly a new section.
- The more parameters change at one point, the **higher the level** of the boundary.

Two qualifications matter for prog. First, context: in a piece where little changes, a single change counts for more. Second, **time**: the change has to *last* longer than short-term memory to establish a new section. A multiparametric change that lasts a few seconds and then goes back is heard as an **anomaly or interruption** inside the ongoing section. Snyder notes that ambiguity can be built on purpose here: very short sections whose interruptions aren't clearly new or old.

## Two kinds of parameter

This is the most practical idea in the chapter. Snyder splits musical parameters in two <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 13, Primary and secondary parameters" />:

| | Primary | Secondary |
|---|---|---|
| **What** | Pitch, harmony, rhythm | Loudness, tempo, density, timbre, register and range, number of instruments, articulation… |
| **How we hear them** | As identifiable *patterns* that can be recognized when they return | As *more of / less of* |
| **What they can do** | Motives, riffs, themes, syntax (cadences, recognizable goals) | Gradients (builds, fades) and big contrasts |
| **Weakness** | Need the listener to learn the pattern | Can't make a pattern you'll recognize later: nobody remembers "that exact loudness" |

“Secondary” doesn't mean less important. Two of his points make this a design tool for a band:

- **Large changes in secondary parameters stand out most in a listener's first memory of a piece**, especially for less experienced listeners. Your drummer's feel, your guitar sound, register and sheer loudness are what people remember first.
- Secondary parameters are an **open list**. Anything that can be heard as more or less can become one: phrase length, amount of repetition, noise content, how many players are going. That's a lot of untapped room in a band.

He also notes that **syntax** (learned patterns in primary parameters, like a cadence) is what lets closure go *up* — an ending can rise if the pattern says “end”. Without syntax, closure falls back on the natural signs: falling, slowing, softening.

## Too much, too little

Two limits pull against each other <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 13, Information and redundancy" />:

- **Habituation**: what doesn't change fades into the background. We need change to stay engaged.
- **Short-term memory**: we can only take in a little new information at a time. Too much change is noise.

So every piece balances **information** (novelty) and **redundancy** (repetition, constancy, syntax). Redundancy isn't the enemy: it's what lets the listener predict, chunk and remember, and it acts as automatic rehearsal. Snyder links the two to tension: returns of earlier material are stable and closural; new material (transitions, beginnings) is less stable and higher in tension.

## Remembered time isn't clock time

A section's length is judged by how much information it held, not by the clock <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 13, Duration" />:

- A stretch **full of novel events** feels fast while it happens, but is **remembered as long**.
- A stretch **full of repetition** feels long while it happens, but is **remembered as short**.
- Of two equal stretches, the **first** tends to be remembered as longer, because it was new.
- So section lengths planned as clock-time proportions (golden ratios, Fibonacci bar counts) aren't heard as those proportions. Two things help: **repeated listening**, and a **steady pulse and meter**, which acts as a clock for the listener.

This is the evidence behind Module 5's *What listeners actually hear*.

## Hierarchy, landmarks and lists

Long-term memory holds a long piece as a **hierarchy of chunks**: events inside groups, groups inside phrases, phrases inside sections, sections inside section groups <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 13, Memory and hierarchy" />. A few consequences:

- **Chunk boundaries are the entry points.** Listeners find their way around a piece at boundaries, especially boundaries that exist on several levels at once. Clear boundaries of different strengths (Module 4's punctuation scale) are what build the hierarchy.
- **First impressions are spotty.** On first hearing, memory is "peppered" with salient, unexpected moments, not built in order. Different listeners tend to agree on which moments stand out. First and last events of a sequence are remembered best.
- **A flat list is fragile.** Music with no higher-level boundaries — one riff after another, with no grouping above the phrase — can only be remembered as a chain, each phrase cueing the next. It takes many more hearings to learn, and one break in the chain loses the rest.
- **Schemas tell listeners where they are.** A familiar form (verse–chorus, a genre's usual shape) gives every section a place. Music without a known schema is remembered as a string of local associations, which can make it feel more immediate, and harder to place.

## Kinds of direction

Snyder borrows Kramer's term **linearity**: earlier events create expectations about later ones, so the music seems to move somewhere <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 13, Linearity" />.

- **Bottom-up linearity**: graduated change in one direction (rising, thickening, accelerating) plus **incomplete closure** (phrases that end on weak beats, short notes, rising lines), which carries momentum across gaps.
- **Top-down linearity**: learned goals, like a cadence or a return to the tonal centre.
- **Without tonal goals**, a piece can still have goals by **repeating important patterns at goal points** and reinforcing the arrival with secondary parameters. This is the situation of most riff-based heavy music.
- **Nondirected linearity**: progressions that move but toward no defined goal.
- **Multiply-directed linearity**: a progression and its goal are separated, like intercut plots in a film.
- **Web forms**: no line at all, only associations between similar material. Listeners have less sense of location, and similar places get confused in memory.

## Exploiting memory or defeating it

Finally, Snyder sorts music into two broad strategies <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 13, Memory strategies" />. Most music **exploits** long-term memory: clear grouping, degrees of closure, hierarchy, recognizable returns. Some music deliberately **sabotages** it to keep the listener in the present:

- **High information**: random or uncategorizable sounds (noise, glides, highly irregular rhythm) — "nuance overload", no patterns to chunk.
- **Low information**: very similar but not identical patterns ("formatted gestures") that interfere with each other in memory, so the present seems to vary without your being able to say how.
- **Memory length**: gaps longer than short-term memory (10–12 seconds of silence) or a single sound so long it has no frame, like a drone.

A piece can use different strategies in different parameters, or in different sections. That's a formal resource in itself: a section that defeats memory, framed by sections that reward it.

## Practice

<Exercise title="One riff, four boundaries">

1. Take one riff and play it for four bars.
2. Make four versions of what follows: change one parameter; change two; change three or more; change three or more for just one bar, then go back.
3. Play each to someone and ask: “new section, or the same one?” Compare their answers with Snyder's rule.

</Exercise>

<ApplyIt>

After one listen to a long song you don't know, write down every moment you remember, in the order you think they happened. Then listen again and check. Which moments survived, which were misplaced, and what did the survivors have in common?
