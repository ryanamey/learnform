## The same cadence, three placements

Course II dealt with what a cadence *is*: which harmonies and which melodic notes make an ending, and how strong it is (*Punctuation degrees*, *Cadence and its deviations*). This lesson adds the metrical side: **where in the bar and in the hypermeasure the final chord lands**, and how long it is allowed to last. The writers disagree about this more than about anything else in rhythm.

## Yust: the tonic must fill the unit

Yust argues that metrical placement is part of a cadence's **syntax**, not just its rhetoric <Source book="yust" where="Ch. 7.1" />. In Baroque periods (Bach suites, Corelli sonatas), a cadence that is harmonically complete is made weaker by placing it badly: the resolving tonic arrives on a weak beat, or the melody reaches the tonic late. In these pieces placement can count for more than the difference between a half and a full cadence. Mozart uses placement to tell apart two half cadences of equal harmonic strength.

His central rule is the **hypermetrical rule of closure**: a final tonic must be sustained to the end of every hypermetrical unit it begins in. Two consequences:

- **A cadence in the fourth bar is the normal way to close**, because the tonic then fills the unit. He disagrees with Rothstein, for whom this was an unfortunate fact of tonal music, and says it is exactly how hypermeter should behave.
- **A cadence on the next strong downbeat does not close.** If the tonic lands on the downbeat that starts a new unit, and a new phrase begins there, the cadence is **elided**: harmonically complete, but with no time to rest. An accent theory would call that downbeat *stronger*. Yust's point is that the stronger position prevents closure.

Composers used this on a larger scale. Haydn often elided the cadence at the end of a main theme, so that the theme confirms the key without stopping the music too early. Beethoven pushed the moment of full **tonal-rhythmic closure** (a perfect cadence within the largest unit, with the tonic sustained to its end) later and later, in some pieces out of the exposition altogether <Source book="yust" where="Ch. 7.3–7.5" />.

## Lerdahl & Jackendoff: a downbeat can be a goal

GTTM treats the cadence as a **structural accent**, a point of gravity in the harmony, which is independent of metrical accent <Source book="gttm" where="Ch. 2" />. A cadence can land on a weak or a strong beat. The traditional names are **feminine** and **masculine** cadences, and equating structural with metrical accent would erase the difference. They describe two ways a section can end:

- The cadence comes on a weak beat, before the next section begins. The effect is formal "rhyme" and balance (a Schubert waltz, a Chopin mazurka).
- A long **structural anacrusis** (a whole transition, a slow introduction) drives toward a cadence on a strong beat that also begins the next section. Grouping, meter and harmony converge on one moment, a **structural downbeat**: long tension, then instant release. In the first movement of the "Hammerklavier", the antecedent cadences weakly, while the extended consequent cadences on a strong hypermetrical beat that overlaps the next phrase.

Their structural downbeat and Yust's elided cadence describe the same event, a cadence on the next strong downbeat. Yust stresses that it *doesn't close*; GTTM that it is *charged*, an arrival that is also a launch.

## Stephenson: rock lands on the downbeat

In rock, Stephenson finds, cadences usually fall on a **strong downbeat**: the third, fourth or fifth downbeat of a four-bar unit, depending on the phrase model <Source book="stephenson" where="Ch. 1" />. His models include:

- **2+2**: the cadence on the third downbeat, with the rest of the unit filled by a response (backing vocals, a guitar hook).
- **Extension-overlap**: the phrase runs on to the fifth downbeat, overlapping the next unit; common at the end of a chorus as the intro riff returns.
- **Elision**: the fourth bar is reinterpreted as the first of the next unit.
- **Units that end on V and start on I**: the tonal resolution happens at the join between units, not at their end, so each unit leans into the next. This, he says, is why the fade-out is the style-honest ending: the music has no built-in place to stop.

<KeyIdea>Where a cadence lands is part of what it means. The tonic filling its unit closes (Yust); a tonic on the next downbeat is either a non-closure (Yust) or a structural downbeat (L&J); rock tends to land on downbeats and lean on (Stephenson).</KeyIdea>

## Hasty: end as aim, and the compound closing pattern

Hasty distinguishes two meanings of "end": *becoming past* (from the point of view of the next beginning) and *goal* (from the point of view of the event still happening) <Source book="hasty" where="Ch. 13" />. A phrase's goal is not its last chord but the whole process that ends in it. A half cadence ends one phrase and opens a larger event, as a realised measure opens a larger one.

He also names a durational pattern that helps a long measure close: **long–long–short–short–long** (LLSSL), which he calls **compound directed closing**. The acceleration makes the last unit feel like the continuation of a continuation, and so like an ending. He traces it from Notre-Dame motets to the first movement of Beethoven's First Symphony, where it shapes almost every phrase join. Because closure comes late in the pattern, it leaves room for an anacrusis that opens into the next measure, so it is rarely used to end a whole piece. Repeated inside itself, it is typical of the **sentence**.

## Where the writers stand

<Positions title="Where a cadence lands">
  <Position book="yust" where="Ch. 7">Placement is syntactic; the tonic must fill the hypermeasure; a cadence on the next downbeat is elided and doesn't close.</Position>
  <Position book="gttm" where="Ch. 2">Structural and metrical accent are independent; masculine and feminine cadences; structural anacrusis to structural downbeat.</Position>
  <Position book="stephenson" where="Ch. 1">Rock cadences mostly fall on strong downbeats; extension-overlap and elision; units lean into the next.</Position>
  <Position book="hasty" where="Ch. 13">End as aim; LLSSL as compound closing; half cadence as an end that opens a larger event.</Position>
  <Position book="caplin" where="Ch. 3, 5">Cadence is defined by harmony and formal function; elision and evaded cadences are deviations of function (see Course II).</Position>
  <Position book="snyder" where="Ch. 5, 12">The downbeat is the point of greatest metrical closure; closure is graded and comes from several parameters.</Position>
</Positions>
