## Reward arrives before the peak

Salimpoor and colleagues scanned listeners who reliably get chills from their own favourite music <Source book="salimpoor" />. Reward-related dopamine activity appeared in **two distinct places at two moments**. One region rose during the roughly **15 seconds *before*** a chill and then dropped at the chill itself. Another peaked **during** the chill. Anticipation was rewarding in its own right, separately from the peak.

Caveats: 8–10 participants, all selected because they get chills easily, listening to music they chose and knew well. The study can't establish cause.

<KeyIdea>Give big moments an audible approach: 15 seconds or more of forward-pointing change that's predictable in kind but not in timing.</KeyIdea>

## Groove: the inverted U

Vuust & Witek treat meter as the brain's **prediction model** <Source book="vuust-witek" />:

- **Syncopation** creates tension by violating that model. It works best when it **contradicts the meter without undermining it**.
- In Witek's study of 50 funk drum breaks, pleasure and the urge to move **peaked at medium syncopation**. Very simple patterns gave little response. Very complex ones overwhelmed the prediction model, so listeners lost the beat. People who dance a lot rated the breaks as more pleasurable, whatever their formal training.
- A **3:4 cross-rhythm** can be heard as either triple or duple and tends to *flip* between readings. Trained musicians can hold the counter-meter, but it takes effort, and less for the more skilled.
- **Repeated grooves stay engaging** because listeners learn the expected deviations.

## Odd meters are learned

Hannon & Trehub (2005) found that North American adults missed rhythm changes in complex Balkan-style meters, while Bulgarian and Macedonian adults, and 6-month-old infants, did not. Preference for simple meters is **learned through exposure**, not built in. (Summarised from the psychology report; not page-verified here.) For a single song, the implication is that repetition is how the audience learns your meter.

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. **Anchor layer.** In every complex section keep one layer clear: kick on the cycle downbeat, steady hats, a pedal note or drone. Matthews et al. (2019) found simpler harmony strengthens the groove response to syncopation.
2. **State, repeat, then displace.** Introduce an odd-meter riff plainly, repeat it enough to be learned, and only then add displacement or polyrhythm.
3. **Calibrate to the audience, not the drummer.** What the band hears as "simple" sits far to the right of the inverted U for most listeners. Use the listening test to find where they actually are.
4. **Body sections vs. puzzle sections.** Use simpler or longer cycles where you want people to move. Save the puzzles for listening sections.
5. **Approach every climax audibly.** Combine Module 3's levers and cues over 15 seconds or more.

</Steps>

<ListenerSide grade="moderate">

Groove's inverted U comes from funk loops in 4/4. A 2024 study reportedly found no effect of drum complexity, and no study has tested groove in odd meters. Salimpoor's anticipation finding comes from a tiny, selected sample. Learned meter (Hannon & Trehub) is strong evidence for enculturation; that the same learning happens *within* a single song is an inference.

</ListenerSide>
