## Allan F. Moore (ed.), *Analyzing Popular Music* (Cambridge, 2003) — guide

Read in full, November 2026 (all eleven chapters; the bibliography, discography and index are reference matter and were skipped). This is a collection of essays by different authors, not Moore's own *Song Means*. It's a paraphrase for finding your way back into the book, not a substitute for it.

What the collection shares: analysis of recorded popular music is always interpretation, it should serve a question, and the recording (not a score) is the object. About half of it is method and cultural theory; the other half has concrete devices for a band writing long songs.

## For form and arrangement

### Ch. 6 · Rob Bowman — performance and meaning in "Try a Little Tenderness"
- Four recordings (Crosby 1933, Franklin 1962, Cooke 1964, Redding 1966) share words, chords and melody within a range, yet mean very different things. Meaning lives in **performance**: timbre, rhythm, arrangement, tempo. Bowman argues Redding "wrote" a new song through performance.
- Redding's version is a model **additive build**: almost empty first verse; drums enter with rim and hi-hat only; fills multiply; horns take pads; full kit held back until bar 65; a new circular tag section (four bars of chromatic tension, four of release) repeated to a two-bar break as climax. Tempo rises steadily from about 96 to 114 bpm; vocal rasp is applied additively.
- Vocal devices: a palette of timbres; sibilance as engagement vs suppression as control; straight tone then late vibrato; rests and repeated words as halting speech; words giving way to vocables.
- **App:** *Where the climax goes* (additive build). Vocal devices → Roadmap, Module 6.

### Ch. 5 · Stan Hawkins — house music as rhetoric ("French Kiss")
- A 9'53" track mapped as about seventy four-bar units in eleven **cellular groove patterns**, each defined by one entering feature, over constant kick, organ and hi-hats. Contrast against constancy keeps expectation alive.
- The break: tempo slowed almost to a stop, drums gone, voice exposed; the groove returns slow and speeds up into the final phase.
- Meter is inferred by the listener; in house it lives in recurring patterns of motion more than single accents.
- **App:** *Repetition, process and absorption*.

### Ch. 8 · Allan Moore — Jethro Tull and modernism
- **Idiolect vs style:** Tull cross many styles while keeping one identity.
- The idiolect rests on an **opposition of acoustic/folk and electric/hard-rock ensembles**: the same lyric set twice in one song, sometimes with the rock version as the arrival point. "Aqualung" is a rough arch alternating period-based verses with open riff sections.
- Rhythm by **displacement** (a beat or half-beat) rather than Yes-style addition; a motif with no clear downbeat over a regular beat, later shortened ("Nobody's Car").
- *Thick as a Brick*: one gesture gradually **exchanged** for another through a cycle, with a third gesture hiding the swap.
- Songs re-arranged over decades; quotation of other records; a song interrupted by a foreign style when the lyric names it. **Fragmenting a surface within a secure context** (modernist) vs **fragmenting the context** (postmodern).
- **App:** *Riff cycles as variations*, *Contrast and transition*, *Moment form and strata*. Idiolect → Roadmap, Module 0.

### Ch. 9 · John Covach — new wave and musical worlding
- **Musical worlding:** listeners hear new songs through songs they know; a piece is a point in a web, and style is what heard pieces share.
- Rock form types: contrasting verse–chorus; compound AABA (two verse–chorus units, a bridge, a return).
- Foreigner's "Feels Like the First Time": a **formal parenthesis**, a minor-key chorus inserted before the expected chorus by reinterpreting chords on the way in and out. "Cold as Ice" puts chorus before verse.
- The Cars' "Just What I Needed": a faster harmonic rhythm introduced in the chorus is **carried into later returns** of the interlude; the song works out a major/relative-minor ambiguity across its length.
- **App:** *Contrast and transition*, *Returning and ending*, *What listeners actually hear*.

### Ch. 3 · Dai Griffiths — from lyric to anti-lyric
- Lyrics lean toward **lyric** (rhyme-led, words and music as one) or **anti-lyric** (prose-like statement, striking words, music as a bed).
- **Verbal space:** each musical phrase gives a line of fixed length; lines can be full or empty, words placed early or late, density doubled for contrast or built up across a song.
- Rhyme types: full, near, deliberate non-rhyme; rhymes across parts of speech push the thought forward. REM's "Nightswimming": a six-bar loop where melody divides 4+2 and harmony 2+4, inviting repetition.
- **App:** Roadmap, Module 6.

### Ch. 4 · Robynn Stilwell — sound and score in *The X-Files*
- Music and sound design as one continuum: **ambient** (space, hum), **atmospheric** (pad with loose meter), **minimalist** (metered cells).
- Natural sounds organized into rhythm; percussion timbres matched to the physical space; one distinctive sample reused as a recurring sign, brought further forward each time.
- Once a sound world is established, departures read as meaning.
- **App:** Roadmap, Module 3 (timbre).

## Method and context

### Ch. 1 · Allan Moore — introduction
- Analysis as interpretation; "why did the music sound like it did?"; information as differences that make a difference; explaining musical decisions against the idea of music as magic.

### Ch. 2 · Robert Walser — ten apothegms and four instances
- Ten working principles, among them: analysis is relational and should state its goal; "structure" is a spatial metaphor for a temporal experience; analysis is reductive like a map, which is why it's useful.
- One childhood prayer quoted in a gentle country song and in Metallica's "Enter Sandman" (Phrygian riffs, a half-step key change at the end of each verse) means opposite things: same material, different setting. **App:** Roadmap, Module 7.

### Ch. 7 · Adam Krims — Marxist analysis and urban geography
- Mainly an argument about capitalism, genre and the image of the city in rap. One production device: the **"hip-hop sublime"**, loops that agree on 4/4 but clash harmonically and are detuned against each other by about a quarter tone, heard as menacing. **App:** Roadmap, Module 3.

### Ch. 10 · Chris Kennett — is anybody listening?
- The same track means different things depending on when, who, how attentively, where, on what system and for what task. Doc Scott's "Shadowboxing": one breakbeat for eight minutes, elements switched on and off rather than developed.
- **App:** *What listeners actually hear*, *Repetition, process and absorption*; the listening-situation profile → Roadmap, Module 5.

### Ch. 11 · Martin Stokes — talk and text
- Everyday talk about music is part of musical knowing. Sara Cohen's Liverpool bands: rehearsing "the hard bit" (the change between riffs), counting repeats before a change, a drum roll to cue it, a proposed 3/4-against-4/4 contrast that felt "too choppy" without anyone saying why.
- **App:** Roadmap, Module 0.
