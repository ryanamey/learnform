## Dynamics is more than volume

LaRue defines dynamics broadly: **all aspects of intensity**, whether marked in the part or implied by the forces playing <Source book="larue" where="Ch. 2, Dynamics" />. Adding players doesn't simply add loudness. More brass can blur and muffle the brass tone; a string quartet sounds much fuller than a trio, more than the one extra player would suggest. For a band, this means that "louder" can come from gain, from doubling, from register or from adding a part, and each gives a different kind of intensity.

He measures dynamics like everything else, by **degree of contrast** (from p–f in most Baroque and Classical music to Mahler's fff next to ppp) and **frequency of contrast** (immediate jumps, crescendos, or long gradations). Together these give two basic procedures:

1. **Terrace (block) dynamics**: sections set at contrasting levels, like organ registrations or the alternation of full orchestra and soloists in a concerto grosso.
2. **Tapered dynamics**: gradual slopes to a new plateau, which then lasts long enough to contrast with the previous one.

Both work at any scale. Corelli echoes full orchestra and soloists every half-bar; a full orchestra returning after a long solo episode in a Bach or Vivaldi concerto is a large structural event.

## The intrinsic crescendo

LaRue's strongest example of a build doesn't depend on the dynamic markings <Source book="larue" where="Ch. 6, Growth in Middle Dimensions" />. In the coda of the first movement of Beethoven's *Eroica*, the excitement rises through **cumulative orchestral additions, more complex fabric and more rhythmic activity**. He finds this far more powerful than a crescendo in the surface dynamics alone. The length of such builds, and the size of their contrast, is for him one of the most revealing features of a composer's style.

Belkin calls preparing an event in this way **announcing**: pointing the listener to an important event in advance and building expectation for it, so that it feels inevitable <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 20, Announcing" />. In a Bach organ fugue, each new entry adds a voice, so the entries are a "natural textural crescendo". His exercise is to make the **length of preparation proportional to the importance** of what it prepares.

## Long builds and terraces in progressive rock

Smolko finds both procedures on *Thick as a Brick*, and a third that is rare in mainstream rock <Source book="smolko" where="Ch. 5, Dynamics" />:

- **Abrupt switches** between quiet acoustic folk passages and loud electric rock, and moments where the whole band drops out to leave the organ.
- **Terraced dynamics**: flute and organ play a passage quietly, then the whole band repeats it loudly.
- **A long, gradual crescendo**: one instrumental passage builds for about a minute and a half, which he compares to a distant army marching closer. He notes that long crescendos like this are rare in mainstream rock but common in progressive rock, citing Genesis's "Watcher of the Skies", and he mentions King Crimson's *Larks' Tongues in Aspic, Part One* for its extreme dynamic contrasts.

Caplin's terms for the same energy shapes come from Wallace Berry <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 5" />: a **progressive** dynamic (energy building, as in introductions, by crescendo and accumulating texture) and a **recessive** dynamic (energy dissolving, as in closing sections). Caplin notes that closing sections don't always recede; some sustain or even increase the energy.

## When elements disagree: reversibility

A build is rarely made by every element at once. LaRue points out that one element can rise while another falls, and the result can go either way <Source book="larue" where="Ch. 6, Growth in Small Dimensions" />. In a passage from Brahms's Clarinet Trio, the surface rhythm and the chord changes speed up, but the texture thins and the dynamics fall, and the falling elements win: the passage winds down. He calls this **reversibility**, and draws a practical conclusion. Two musicians can disagree about the shape of a phrase because each is listening to a different element, and both can be right.

Belkin's **coordination principle** describes the same thing from the composer's side <Source book="belkin" where="Conclusion" />. Elements don't all have to move in the same direction or by the same amount; controlling their interaction is how a composer fine-tunes a mood. But **only at a major climax or ending do all aspects normally point the same way**.

## Climaxes and their scale

LaRue places the **climax** between motion and shape: it intensifies motion, and it also sets the scale for the whole piece's profile <Source book="larue" where="Ch. 6, Growth in Large Dimensions" />. His questions for any piece: Is one section the climax of the whole? Is there more than one climax, perhaps a hierarchy of them? How far is the highest point from the lowest? Belkin grades accents in a similar way, as **mild, medium, strong and climactic**, in proportion to their formal importance <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 20, Accenting" />. Where to put the climax is the subject of Course VII.

<KeyIdea>Intensity comes from forces as well as from volume. Terraces jump between levels, tapers slope between them, and the strongest builds add parts, density and activity together (LaRue's intrinsic crescendo). When elements disagree, the listener can hear either direction.</KeyIdea>

  <Position book="belkin" where="Ch. 20; Conclusion">Announce important events with preparation proportional to importance; grade accents; all elements agree only at a major climax.</Position>
  <Position book="smolko" where="Ch. 5">Abrupt contrasts, terraced dynamics and long gradual crescendos, the last more common in prog than in mainstream rock.</Position>

  <Position book="snyder" where="Ch. 13">Loudness can't form recognisable patterns; it works in increasing and decreasing progressions and large contrasts.</Position>
</Positions>

## Practice

<Exercise title="Draw the energy line">

1. Pick a long song. Draw one line across the page for its whole length.
2. Mark each place where the intensity jumps (a terrace) and each stretch where it slopes (a taper).
3. For each slope, list what changes: parts added, density, register, gain, actual volume.

</Exercise>

<GuitarPro title="Three builds to the same arrival">

1. Write a 4-bar arrival (a riff or chorus).
2. Write three 8-bar builds that lead into it: one by adding parts, one by increasing rhythmic density with the same parts, and one by rising in register.
3. Then make a fourth build where one element rises and another falls. Which direction wins?

</GuitarPro>

<ApplyIt>

For your next long piece, decide where its single climactic moment is, and make that the only place where every element points the same way. Plan the preparation before it: its length should match its importance.
