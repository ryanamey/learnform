## Texture has a rhythm of its own

Course III treated rhythm as durations and meter. LaRue widens it. For him rhythm has three **layers** <Source book="larue" where="Ch. 5, The Layers of Rhythm" />:

1. the **continuum**: the hierarchy of pulse, meter and larger units that we expect to continue;
2. **surface rhythm**: the actual durations;
3. **interactions** with the other elements: whenever changes in Sound, Harmony or Melody fall into a roughly regular pattern, they produce a rhythm too.

From the third layer he names several rhythms: harmonic rhythm, contour rhythm and, for Sound, **textural rhythm, timbral rhythm and the rhythm of dynamic levels** <Source book="larue" where="Ch. 2, Contributions of Sound to Movement" />. They can't always be written as note values, but they shape the motion.

## Aaaa becomes Aa Aa

His clearest example is from a Corelli concerto grosso <Source book="larue" where="Ch. 5, Example 5-2" />. The same material repeats literally, which on its own would be nearly static. But it alternates between the full orchestra and the small solo group, so a string *aaaa* is heard as *Aa Aa*: the alternation of forces creates stress without changing a pitch, and the listener hears it as meter.

## Scoring can make the large units audible

LaRue's most interesting claim concerns long spans <Source book="larue" where="Ch. 2, Contributions of Sound to Movement" />. Above the phrase, regular units are hard to feel as beats: real music rarely gives 8+8+8+8 bars in perfect order, and details distract. This is exactly where Sound helps most. **A change of orchestration every sixteen bars** can remind the listener of a paragraph structure that would otherwise disappear among the melodic and rhythmic details.

This adds a voice to the hypermeter debate in Course III (see *Seminar: Is hypermeter real above two bars?*). LaRue doesn't claim the large unit is metrical; he says texture can make it perceptible.

He also notes that we accept **equivalent** rather than equal modules: a phrase pattern of 4+5+4+3 bars can still be heard as four-bar units. One reason he suggests is that the same length feels shorter when intensity rises and longer when it falls <Source book="larue" where="Ch. 6, Growth in Large Dimensions" />.

## Sound marks the joints

For form, LaRue rates Sound highest <Source book="larue" where="Ch. 2, Contributions of Sound to Shape" />. Changes of timbre, dynamics and texture create **the most easily observed articulations** in the flow. When the surface rhythm or overlapping lines leave the joint unclear, a change from voices to instruments, or from loud to soft, decides it. Sound also helps memory: a theme is easier to recognise when its colour comes back with it, and an old theme in a new instrument, a new octave doubling or a fuller scoring is both familiar and fresh.

## How heavy is a joint?

Every change makes some articulation, LaRue says, and we respond only to the larger ones. The **weight** of an articulation depends on three things <Source book="larue" where="Ch. 6, Articulation" />:

1. its **duration** (a rest, a long note);
2. the **number of elements** that change at once;
3. their **concinnity**: how exactly the changes coincide.

His example is a section joint in a Mozart sonata (K. 545): a rest, a texture that thins from melody-and-accompaniment to bare octaves, a surface rhythm slowing from sixteenths to quarters, a held harmony, and then a new theme on a high note not yet used. Five changes, timed together.

Two other writers reach a similar rule by different routes:

- **Snyder**, from memory research <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 13" />: a change in **one** parameter (louder, a new register, a tempo change) is heard as articulation *inside* a section; two may or may not make a new section; **three or more at once** almost certainly do. A new configuration must also last longer than short-term memory (several seconds), or it is heard as an interruption.
- **Belkin**, from composing <Source book="belkin" where="Conclusion, Coordination principle" />: no single dimension can make a convincing result alone. A change of orchestration in the middle of a phrase with nothing else changing sounds like a wrong entry.

And one writer warns against confusing loud with closed. **Caplin** separates a cadence's **syntactical** strength (its harmonic type) from its **rhetorical** impact (loudness, texture, metric position) <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 3" />. A huge texture can mark a weak ending; a soft one can close completely. Texture tells the listener *where* the joint is; whether the music has actually *ended* is, for Caplin, a matter of harmony.

## Four kinds of textural contrast

Tim Smolko's study of Jethro Tull's *Thick as a Brick* finds four ways the band contrasts textures across its 43 minutes <Source book="smolko" where="Ch. 5, Texture" />:

1. **Homorhythmic vs polyrhythmic**: the whole band plays one motive in rhythmic unison, then breaks into a semi-improvised texture of independent parts, then comes back together. He compares it to white light split into a spectrum and recombined.
2. **One instrument vs the group**: acoustic guitar, organ, flute or voice alone, or a drum solo, against the full band.
3. **Gradual addition and subtraction** of instruments.
4. **Sound vs silence**: chords separated by reverb tails and rests, chaotic episodes cut by silence.

He also notes that in progressive rock the bass and drums are often melodic too: the players compete rather than only complement each other.

## Clearing the stage

Caplin, after Schoenberg, describes the classical way of preparing a new section: **liquidation**, the gradual removal of an idea's characteristic features, often with a thinning of texture, which "clears the stage" for the next theme <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 2, 11" />. He also notes that transitions in classical music often end with a textural gap (what Hepokoski and Darcy call a medial caesura), but that the gap is a common sign, not a requirement.

<KeyIdea>Alternating textures create rhythm and even meter (LaRue). A joint's weight depends on how many elements change together and how exactly (LaRue, Snyder, Belkin). Texture marks where the joint is; whether the music has closed is, for Caplin, harmony's job.</KeyIdea>

## Joints between layers

LaRue also describes joints where the layers of the texture *don't* change together <Source book="larue" where="Ch. 6, Articulation" />:

- **Overlap and anticipation**: some parts start the next phrase early or carry the last one over the boundary while the others stop. His example: the opening of Beethoven's Ninth, where some strings anticipate the new phrase while others sweep across the barline.
- **Elision**: one bar is both the end of one phrase and the start of the next, so 4+4 becomes 4+3.
- **Truncation**: the last bar of a phrase is cut off by the next phrase coming in too early.
- **Lamination**: for some time, the joints in two layers of the texture simply disagree. Continued long enough, this produces a polyphonic effect.

He extends "dissonance" to any conflict with a prevailing norm, so these are **modular dissonances**. And he describes **dimensional stratification**: different elements marking units of different sizes at once. In a Stamitz symphony, the surface rhythm repeats every bar and a half, the melodic curve every two bars, and register and dynamics every four. A brief conflict between strata gives energy; constant conflict, he warns, risks disintegration.

## Where the writers stand

<Positions title="What texture does for form">
  <Position book="larue" where="Ch. 2, 5, 6">Sound mainly underlines motion but gives the clearest articulations; textural rhythm can make large units audible; joints weigh more when more elements change together.</Position>
  <Position book="snyder" where="Ch. 13">One changed parameter is articulation within a section; three or more at once is a new section.</Position>
  <Position book="belkin" where="Ch. 20; Conclusion">No dimension convinces alone; coordinated changes tell the listener they're intended.</Position>
  <Position book="caplin" where="Ch. 2, 3, 11">Texture gives a cadence rhetorical force, not syntactical strength; liquidation and a textural gap prepare new sections.</Position>
  <Position book="smolko" where="Ch. 5">In progressive rock, four kinds of textural contrast carry long-form structure.</Position>
</Positions>

<ListenerSide grade="moderate">

Snyder's rule of thumb rests on a cognitive model (Miller and Johnson-Laird) and on Deliège's experiments on segmentation; that larger changes mark larger boundaries is well supported. LaRue's claims about textural rhythm and orchestration every sixteen bars are analytical observations, not tested findings.

</ListenerSide>

## Practice

<Exercise title="Weigh the joints">

1. Take a song with at least six sections. At each joint, list every element that changes.
2. Rank the joints by number of changes. Does the ranking match how big each joint feels?
3. Find one joint where a single element changes. Is it heard as a new section or as a variation inside the old one?

</Exercise>

<GuitarPro title="Texture every four bars">

1. Write a 4-bar riff and copy it four times (16 bars).
2. Keep the notes identical, but change the scoring every 4 bars: full band; guitar and bass only; full band with the guitar an octave higher; guitar alone.
3. Then try changing the scoring every 2 bars, and then every 6. Which grouping does the listener hear?

</GuitarPro>

<ApplyIt>

Plan the joints in your next song by weight. Give the biggest structural joint three or more coordinated changes, the medium joints two, and keep single changes for variety inside sections. Choose one joint where a layer overlaps the boundary instead of stopping with the others.

</ApplyIt>
