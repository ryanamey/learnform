## The question

What is a period, what is a sentence, and is one better than the other? The words have changed meaning more than once, and the writers in this course draw the lines in different places.

## A short history of the word "period"

Caplin gives the history in a box of his chapter on the period <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 3" />:

- **Eighteenth century:** a period was any unit ending with a cadence, usually a perfect authentic one.
- **A. B. Marx (mid-nineteenth century):** two symmetrical phrases of differing cadential strength, the first weaker (*Vordersatz*, *Nachsatz*).
- **Schoenberg:** restricted the period to cases where the second phrase begins with the material of the first.
- **Common North American textbook usage, until recently:** any two-phrase unit, which gives the "contrasting period", where the second phrase begins with new material.
- **Caplin:** follows Schoenberg.

## The positions

  <Position book="schoenberg" where="Ch. V–VIII">Identified the sentence. Period: antecedent with more remote motive-forms, then a consequent that repeats the beginning. Ranks the sentence as the higher form, because it develops at once. Says only a small percentage of classical themes are periods, and the Romantics use fewer.</Position>
  <Position book="caplin" where="Ch. 2–4, 6">Sentence: presentation + continuation (with cadential function). Period: antecedent (weak cadence) + consequent (stronger cadence). Calls the sentence "undoubtedly the most important theme type" in classical music. No ranking of value: the period has balance and repose, the sentence mobility and drive. Rejects the contrasting period (those are hybrids) and the double period (he speaks of a compound period).</Position>
  <Position book="belkin" where="Ch. 6">Period: two phrases as question and answer, sharing material, the first cadence less conclusive; his examples run from Schubert to film music. Uses double period (Beethoven Op. 26) and paragraph (more than two phrases). His chapter is called *Presenting*, a verb for grouping phrases so the listener learns the ideas; it's not Caplin's presentation phrase.</Position>
  <Position book="snyder" where="Ch. 5">No theory of theme types. Describes the mechanism a period uses: an incomplete closure followed by a more complete one, graded closure across several parameters, repetition at the point of arrival.</Position>
</Positions>

## Caplin's hybrids

Caplin's own contribution is the **hybrid theme**, which he describes as new to the theory of form <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 4" />. The most common is an antecedent followed by a continuation: it begins like a period and ends like a sentence. What textbooks called a contrasting period, he says, isn't wrong as a description, but it misses the continuation and cadential functions of the second phrase. He arranges the types on a spectrum from sentence to period, with hybrids between.

## Where they agree

All four accept graded closure (a weaker ending answered by a stronger one). Schoenberg and Caplin share the definitions of sentence and period; Caplin credits Schoenberg with discovering the sentence.

## Where they differ

- **Ranking.** Schoenberg: the sentence is higher. Caplin: different characters, no ranking.
- **How strict is "period"?** Schoenberg and Caplin: the consequent must begin like the antecedent. Belkin: shared material and question–answer, a broader definition. Many textbooks: any two phrases.
- **Terms.** Contrasting period (textbooks) vs hybrid (Caplin). Double period (Belkin, textbooks) vs compound period (Caplin).
- **Frequency.** Schoenberg reports periods as a small share of classical themes; Caplin calls the sentence the most important type. These agree in direction; neither is a count you can check here.

## Questions to decide

1. Which definition of period will your band use: Schoenberg–Caplin's strict one or Belkin's broader one?
2. Is a ranking useful to you? Does your music need more of the sentence's drive or the period's repose, and where?
3. Do you want the hybrid category? Try labelling three sections of your songs; how many need it?
4. Riff sections often repeat a two-bar idea and then break it up. Do you hear that as a sentence's presentation and continuation, or does it need a different name?
