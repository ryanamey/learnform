## Meter changes do formal jobs

Biamonte's central claim is that metric dissonance in rock isn't decoration. It does **formal work**, small-scale and large-scale, much like harmonic tension in Classical form <Source book="biamonte" />. She separates three levels: rhythm (inside the beat), meter (the bar), and hypermeter (groups of bars, usually 4). Then she sorts dissonances by what they *do*.

<KeyIdea>Put metric dissonance where a section begins, joins, ends or contrasts — not everywhere. Where it sits is what makes it form.</KeyIdea>

## Small-scale functions

| Function | What happens | Examples Biamonte cites |
|---|---|---|
| **Initiating** | An ambiguous opening, a "metric fake-out": the listener guesses the downbeat wrong, then corrects | The Beatles, "Martha My Dear"; The Cars, "Just What I Needed" |
| **Linking (partial bar)** | A truncated or extra bar at a section join creates momentum, often a 2/4 bar in 4/4 where beat 3 is reheard as a new downbeat | Prince, "Purple Rain"; Derek and the Dominos, "Layla" |
| **Cadential (hemiola)** | Before a cadence the beat or bar value shifts. 4 → 3 contracts and accelerates; 3 → 4 expands and slows | David Bowie, "Changes"; Led Zeppelin, "Rock and Roll"; The Beatles, "The End" |

## Large-scale functions

| Function | What happens | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Loose verse / tight chorus** | Verses are irregular, choruses regular, so the chorus feels like an arrival | Alice in Chains, "Them Bones" (7/4 verse, 4/4 refrain); Rush, "Limelight" |
| **Dissonant middle** | The bridge or development is the most unstable section | Yes, "Roundabout"; Radiohead, "Paranoid Android" |
| **Dissonant frame** | Unstable outer sections around a stable centre | Rush, "YYZ"; Led Zeppelin, "Kashmir"; Pink Floyd, "Money" |

## What the corpus says about Tool

Biamonte tabulated metric dissonance across several catalogues. **Tool (39 songs, *Undertow* to *10,000 Days*) was the most metrically dissonant corpus in the study.** Within Tool, *Lateralus* was the most consonant album. The finding that matters most for form: **Tool often reverses the usual pattern.** Their choruses are more often *less* metrically settled than their verses, a "tight verse / loose chorus" design. In most rock the chorus is the arrival of stability. In Tool it's often the arrival of instability.

The only Tool song the article discusses individually is "Lateralus", where the metric and syllabic patterns follow the Fibonacci sequence, as the band has confirmed in interviews.

## How to use it

<Steps>

- **Choose a polarity for each song.** Loose verse / tight chorus means the chorus arrives as relief. Tight verse / loose chorus (Tool) means the chorus arrives as intensification. Decide which one the song needs.
- **Put a partial bar at one join, not every join.** One extra or missing half-bar before the climax works like a pushed downbeat. Used everywhere it stops being noticed.
- **End a section with a hemiola.** Contract (4 → 3) to speed into the next section; expand (3 → 4) to slow into it.
- **Use a dissonant middle as the development.** If the outer sections are fairly regular, the bridge is where the meter can break, and the return feels like coming home.

</Steps>

<ListenerSide grade="moderate">

Biamonte also notes a production habit: when metric dissonance occurs, the consonant bass and drums are often mixed more prominently. The listener gets the anchor at the front and the dissonance on top. This fits the "contradict without undermining" principle (Vuust & Witek). It's an observation from records, not an experiment.

</ListenerSide>
