## The smallest thing a listener can keep

A motive is the smallest musical unit that has a *character* — something short enough to hold whole in your head and distinctive enough to recognize when it comes back. Schoenberg describes its raw ingredients as intervals and rhythms combined into a recognizable shape <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. III" />. Belkin adds the condition that makes the difference: a motive needs **internal contrast** <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 1" />.

An evenly-played chromatic scale has intervals and rhythm, but nothing in it stands out from anything else, so nothing sticks. Give the same notes a repeating short–short–long rhythm and suddenly there are landmarks: a place where it pauses, a note that is longer, a beat that is louder. Memory works by noticing difference, even inside four notes.

<KeyIdea>A motive is memorable because something inside it contrasts with something else inside it.</KeyIdea>

Here is the motif this whole module uses. Call it **M**.

<MotifPlayer

  notation="E3/16 E3/16 G3/8 A3/8! r/16 E3/16 Bb3/8. A3/16 G3/4"
  bpm={96}
  caption="Contrasts inside one bar: a quick stutter vs. longer notes; stepwise rise vs. the jump to B♭ (a tritone above E); an accent and a gap in the middle; a slow fall at the end."
/>

Count the contrasts: rhythm (fast pair vs. long tail), direction (up, then down), interval colour (plain steps vs. the tritone), dynamics (the accented A), and silence (the sixteenth rest). That is a lot of identity for one bar. Each of these features is something you can later keep or change — which is the subject of the next three pages.

## Rhythm is the strongest ingredient

Both authors point at the same thing from different sides. Schoenberg: preserving rhythmic features is the most effective way to keep variants coherent. Belkin: changing a motif's rhythm or its accent pattern is among the most drastic changes you can make. In other words, **rhythm is the motif's fingerprint**; pitch is closer to its clothing.

The classic proof is four notes most people on earth can recognize:

<MotifPlayer
  label="Beethoven, Symphony No. 5 — the opening cell"

  compact
  caption="Three short repeated notes and a long one. Beethoven builds a whole movement mostly from this rhythm, changing the pitches constantly."
/>

For a guitarist this is good news. Riff-based music is rhythm-first by nature: a palm-muted cell on one note *is* a motif if its rhythm has internal contrast.

## Not only pitch and rhythm

Belkin points out that a motive can also be carried by timbre, articulation, register or dynamics. A single note with an unusual sound and a strong accent can already function as a motive. This is where your instrument matters: a specific pick attack, a harmonic, a slide, a feedback swell, a particular tuning, a vocal break between registers — any of these, repeated deliberately, becomes material.

<Aside>

Why this matters for form: Snyder's model splits listening into three time scales — single events (under about a second), patterns held in short-term memory (a few seconds), and form, which only exists in long-term memory <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 1" />. A motive lives at the middle level. If it doesn't survive into long-term memory, nothing that happens to it later can be heard as a transformation — so everything big depends on something small being memorable.

</Aside>

<ListenerSide grade="moderate">

Snyder: what stands out from its surroundings is what gets noticed and kept, and a short pattern repeated soon becomes an expectation. That is the perceptual case for Belkin's internal contrast: a motive with no edge gives memory nothing to hold. Vuust & Witek's prediction view adds that once a pattern is learned, the listener starts anticipating it, so a strong motive is also the start of every surprise you'll write later. The "rhythm is the fingerprint" point is the composers' observation (Schoenberg, Belkin), consistent with this research but not tested by it directly.

</ListenerSide>

## Practice

<Exercise title="Four characters, one motive each">

Based on Belkin's first exercise set, adapted for guitar and voice.

1. Invent four 1-bar motives with these characters: *menacing, nostalgic, restless, weightless*.
2. For each, write down its internal contrasts (at least two) the way we did for M.
3. Now keep the notes and change only the non-pitch dimensions — tone, register, articulation, dynamics. Can you turn *menacing* into *weightless* without changing a single pitch?
4. Enter the best two into the Motif bank.

</Exercise>

<EPTask>

Take the strongest riff or vocal hook from one EP track and list its internal contrasts. If you can't find two, that's a diagnosis: it may be a texture rather than a motive. Either give it a contrast (an accent, a rest, one longer note, one unexpected interval) or accept that it's a texture and give the song a real motive somewhere else.

</EPTask>
