## Variants that have children

Schoenberg separates two kinds of change <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. III" />. A **variant** is a local decoration: it changes a detail and has no consequences. **Developing variation** is different. Each new form of the motive grows out of the *previous* form, not just the original, and the changes have consequences for what comes next. The result feels like growth rather than repetition with decoration.

He compares a piece to a photograph album showing one subject's life under changing circumstances. The order of the photos isn't chronological, though. It follows what the listener can understand.

<KeyIdea>Schoenberg's developing variation: each motive-form grows out of the previous one, and the coherence lies in the chain, not only in resemblance to the original.</KeyIdea>

## Melody or theme?

Schoenberg draws a distinction that explains why some great tunes are useless as the start of a long piece <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. XI" />. Every note added after the first creates a little unrest, a problem. A **melody** solves its problem by **balance**: it answers itself quickly, keeps close to its harmony, phrases regularly, and needs nothing added. A **theme** solves its problem by **carrying out its consequences**, like a hypothesis that needs proofs. It sharpens its problem instead of settling it, puts remote ideas side by side, and only makes sense once you hear what happens to it later.

<KeyIdea>Schoenberg: a melody settles its own problem by balance; a theme carries out its consequences and needs what follows it.</KeyIdea>

He values both. A simple melody can carry deep content: he finds no nobler contrast than speaking lightly of a great grief, and cites Heine on making little songs out of great sorrows. He adds that real pieces mix the two and that theory exaggerates the split. Applied to a long piece, his distinction raises a question rather than a rule: does your opening idea settle itself, or does it leave something (an odd note, an unsettled rhythm) that asks to be continued?

## Two tools for keeping the chain audible

**1. Double motives.** Belkin shows that a motif made of two clearly different parts (call them *a* and *b*) can carry a long passage just by reordering them: *a b*, *a a b*, *b a*, *a b b a* <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 1, Double motives" />. That gives much more variety than a single cell while staying close to the source.

<ExampleList set="double-motive" />

Motif M is already a double motive. *a* is the stutter-and-rise, and *b* is the fall from the tritone. A riff section can run for a long time on combinations of these two.

**2. Wandering, then a clear return.** Belkin analyses a Bach allemande where the motif drifts through increasingly remote forms, even introducing a contrasting syncopated figure. It works for three reasons:
- the new figure is prepared by details just before it
- it arrives at a point where new material is natural (after a cadence, or at a peak)
- the music then returns to a **literal** statement of the original

The rule he draws: the listener needs regular doses of easy familiarity to follow the argument without strain, so a wander is followed by a clear return.

## Ornamentation: one variant, a whole section

The opposite strategy is to make one decorated version of the motif and keep it as the basis of an entire section <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 1, Motivic ornamentation" />. The connection is heard once, at the switch. After that the new version sets its own rules. Variation forms work this way, and so do many songs where a later section is "the same riff, but…" played its new way for a long stretch.

## Working one idea over minutes

Schoenberg's chapters on larger forms show how composers keep one idea going over a long span <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. XVI, XX" />. His editors prefer the word **elaboration** to "development": the material is adapted, varied, expanded, condensed, recombined and carried through keys, but seldom grows into something new. Useful habits from his analyses:

- **Promote a minor detail.** Often a few features that were inconspicuous at first dominate a whole section. Beethoven builds one development from incidental octaves at the end of the exposition.
- **Shrink the units as you go.** The early segments are longer and more stable; approaching the return, segments get shorter and the harmony changes faster. That gives a climactic condensation and a partial liquidation at the same time.
- **Reduce to a residue.** In Beethoven's op. 28 a motive is cut from eight bars to four, then two, until only a rhythmic reminiscence is left, repeated for sixteen bars without a chord change.
- **Follow one figure through its forms.** In Mozart's String Quartet K. 465 the motive widens to a climax, its upbeat turns into a broken chord that takes over, the motive is squeezed into running notes within a third, cut to one bar, and finally dissolves.

His conclusion warns against the opposite approach: large forms can't be built by laying musical bricks together or pouring material into ready-made moulds <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. XX, Conclusion" />.

## Does form depend on derivation?

The writers don't agree on how much derivation matters.

  <Position book="schoenberg" where="Ch. III–IV, XX">The motive is the germ: the smallest common multiple and greatest common factor of the piece. Homophonic music is a style of developing variation, and coherence comes largely from motive-forms derived from one basic motive.</Position>
  <Position book="caplin" where="Ch. 2">Formal functions don't depend on where material comes from. Fragmentation concerns only the length of the units, whether their contents derive from the basic idea or are new. A continuation is a continuation either way.</Position>
  <Position book="belkin" where="Ch. 1, Ch. 11 note">Uses derivation constantly (double motives, wandering, ornamental variants) but calls the belief that all ideas derive from one basic motive debatable as a generalization. Contrast can also come from changing several dimensions at once.</Position>
  <Position book="snyder" where="Ch. 12–13">Describes pattern families: keep the rhythmic contour, change pitch or timbre, and the ear hears relatives. Whether the relation is heard depends on memory, not on how the composer derived it.</Position>

## The tendency of the smallest notes

One drift needs watching in any chain of variants. Schoenberg compares the smallest note values to a falling body: one sixteenth soon becomes many, then whole passages <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. V" />. Each variant tends to be a little busier than its parent, and if nothing stops it, the music runs out of faster values long before it reaches its climax. Restricting it, he says, takes care. Place the acceleration where you want the energy, and pull it back afterwards.

<SmallestNotesLab />

For a band this applies to every layer: hats going to sixteenths, a double-kick figure creeping in, a guitar line filling every gap. Once the fastest level arrives, there is nothing faster left to build to.

<ListenerSide grade="moderate">

Snyder: long-term memory keeps a sketch, not detail, so a variant is heard as related only if enough of the source is remembered. Belkin's advice to end a wander with a clear return fits that account, though he argues from practice. Whether listeners actually hear a chain of derivations as a family tree has not, as far as these sources report, been tested directly.

</ListenerSide>
