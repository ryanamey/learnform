## Christopher Hasty, *Meter as Rhythm*

Hasty is a music theorist (Harvard); *Meter as Rhythm* was published by Oxford University Press in 1997. Its starting point is philosophical: Western theory has split **meter** (a measuring grid, mechanical, the same every bar) from **rhythm** (expressive, free, unrepeatable), and Hasty argues the split comes from our concepts, not from what we hear. His sources are as much philosophy (Whitehead, Bergson, William James) as music theory.

**How it was read for the app:** in full, preface through Ch. 16, except Ch. 14, where the Monteverdi analysis was read in full, the Schütz analysis in part, and the Webern and Babbitt analyses only at their openings and conclusions. Notes and bibliography skimmed. The music examples are known only through the prose.

## His question

**What is meter if it is not a grid of points but something that happens while we listen?** His answer is **projection**: a duration that has begun and ended offers itself as a model for the duration that follows. The new beginning can confirm that potential, come too early or too late, or force us to reinterpret what we heard.

## His toolkit

- **Beginning, continuation, anacrusis, deferral** instead of strong and weak beats (Ch. 7, 9).
- **Projection and projected potential**; realised, denied, reinterpreted (Ch. 7).
- **Mensural determinacy**: how definite a duration feels. It is strong up to about two or three seconds and fades at the level of phrases (Ch. 12).
- **The stop test**: stop where the next unit should begin and feel whether the silence has a definite length (Ch. 12).
- **Three metrical types**: equal (2, 4), mediated unequal (3, felt through deferral), pure unequal (5, 7, 3+3+2), the last less flexible (Ch. 9).
- **Metrical particularity**: the same rhythm in the same time signature can be metrically different, as in two Bach courantes (Ch. 10).
- **Overlapping, interruption and gradual detachment** at phrase joins (Ch. 13).
- **Projective types**: opening and closing measures; the pattern long–long–short–short–long as "compound closing" (Ch. 13).
- **Phrase expansion as a contraction of projective activity** (Ch. 12).
- **Constituent vs phrase**; segmented and unsegmented phrases; a "diffuse present" in some 20th-century music (Ch. 15–16).

## Where he agrees with the others

- With **London and Snyder** on the limits of the present: meter is definite only up to a few seconds.
- With **Lerdahl & Jackendoff**: large-scale "meter" is weak; above a few bars we hear grouping, not strong and weak.
- With **Caplin and Schoenberg**: his acceleration toward the end of a long measure is close to fragmentation and liquidation in a sentence.

## Where he differs

- **Beats are not points.** Against Lerdahl & Jackendoff and Kramer, a beat is a duration that begins and continues.
- **No conflict of meter and rhythm.** Against Krebs, Cohn and Biamonte, there are only competing projections and reinterpretations. See *Seminar: Is metric dissonance a conflict?*
- **Meter is not habit.** It is reproduced actively each time, not carried by inertia. This puts him against Lerdahl & Jackendoff's metrical inertia, Yust's, and London's own use of the idea.
- **Hypermeter.** Large measures feel like "first and second", or like sections of comparable length, not like strong and weak. He criticises expansion models (Rothstein). See *Seminar: Is hypermeter real above two bars?*
- **The autonomous moment.** Against Stockhausen and Boulez, a vivid moment needs the past and future around it.

## Where the app uses him

Course III: meter as four answers, projection, odd meters (pure unequal), hypermeter, cadence placement (end as aim, the compound closing pattern), and who decides the meter. Course VII: moment form and timelessness.

## How to read him

Read the Preface, then Ch. 6–7 (definitions, projection), then Ch. 9 (accent, anacrusis, metrical types). After that, Ch. 12–13 for phrase-level questions. Part I (Ch. 1–5) is a history of ideas and can come later. Read slowly; he asks you to test each claim by listening.
