## The question

When a motive comes back changed, what has to stay for it to still be *the same motive*? The writers point to different dimensions, and two of them use the word "primary" for opposite lists.

## Schoenberg: rhythm first, among important features

Variation changes less important features and preserves more important ones; which are important depends on the compositional aim <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. III" />. Rhythm is singled out: rhythmic features are more easily remembered than intervallic ones, and in his chapter on the period he puts it in capitals that preserving the rhythm allows extensive changes in the melodic contour <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. V, VII" />. Contour alone can also be the motive.

## Belkin: all dimensions, and "primary" means the strongest for contrast

A motive needs internal contrast, which can lie in pitch and rhythm and, more rarely, in timbre, articulation, register or dynamics <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 1" />. In his similarity table, a swapped accent pattern is among the most drastic changes (5), while an enlarged leap barely counts (1). In ensemble writing he calls tempo, timbre, register and dynamics the **primary dimensions**, because they make the most audible contrasts, more than harmonic refinements <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 4" />.

## Snyder: "primary" means patterns you can recognise again

Following Leonard Meyer, Snyder calls pitch, harmony and rhythm (duration) **primary parameters**: they form patterns built from distinct categories that can be recognised again. Loudness, tempo, timbre, register and density are **secondary**: the ear tracks them as more or less, which makes them suited to contrasts and gradual build-ups <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 13" />. He adds that attempts to make timbre or loudness carry recognisable patterns haven't succeeded for average listeners, while noting that secondary changes stand out strongly in first impressions. Rhythmic contour, on his account, is often the most recognisable feature of a pattern <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 12" />.

## Caplin: the whole texture, and harmony decides

Caplin's basic idea is a whole texture (melody, harmony, accompaniment), not a tune <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 2" />. Whether a second statement is a repetition depends heavily on its harmony: exact repetition, statement–response and sequential repetition are told apart by their harmonic context, and you can't tell which one you have from the melody alone. In a period, a contrasting idea may even share the basic idea's motives and rhythm and still be a contrasting idea if its harmony differs (he shows a case where the opening motive returns over a different harmony) <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 3" />.

## Where they agree

Schoenberg and Snyder agree closely on rhythm as the feature that holds variants together. Belkin's table points the same way (accent pattern changes are drastic). All four accept that context changes what counts as the same.

## Where they differ

- **The word "primary."** For Belkin, tempo, timbre, register and dynamics; for Snyder, pitch, harmony and rhythm. Belkin's list is about what makes contrast; Snyder's about what forms recognisable patterns. They may be answering different questions with the same word.
- **Timbre as identity.** Belkin allows a motive carried by timbre; Snyder reports that timbre hasn't been shown to work that way for average listeners, though composers may hear it through long familiarity.
- **Harmony.** Caplin makes harmony decisive for what counts as repetition; Belkin says harmony alone rarely makes a dramatic contrast. These may not conflict: one is about formal function, the other about audible contrast.

## Questions to decide

1. When your band says "the same riff, but heavier", which dimension changed? Is that, for you, the same motive (Schoenberg, Snyder) or a strong contrast (Belkin)?
2. Do you want to use "primary" in Belkin's sense, Snyder's, or avoid the word?
3. For a guitar-synth or effects-based sound: can a timbre be your motive? Test it on someone who hasn't heard the song.
4. Over a riff, does changing the bass note make a repetition into something new, as in Caplin's harmonic view?
