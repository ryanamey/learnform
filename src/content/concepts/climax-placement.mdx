## The question that organises modern song form

Recent theory of rock and pop form asks **where the energy goes and whether material returns**, more than what the sections are called. Two sources give you the main shapes.

**Schwitzgebel's taxonomy of perceptual climax** <Source book="schwitzgebel" where="§3" /> describes how a pop song reaches its peak in the final third, through texture and cues (see *Cues and arrivals*). Her baseline, the **prototype** (verse–chorus twice, bridge, last chorus), may use cues but has no perceptual climax at all, and plenty of hits are built that way. The four climax types play with what the listener expects the last chorus to sound like:

- **Release** (Glass Animals, "Heat Waves"): the bridge drops the beat and builds; a soar, then a full bar of silence, then a single tom hit on the upbeat launch the final chorus. The contrast is the climax.
- **One more time** (Ed Sheeran, "Bad Habits"): after the bridge's cues, the chorus arrives *sparse* (voice and one instrument). A second cue (everything withheld, the pickup line repeated) brings the chorus back in full. The climax is the deferred payoff.
- **Stairstepper** (Halsey, "So Good"): the first two choruses start thinner than expected and step up halfway through after a cue (panned claps on the upbeat). The final chorus arrives full from its first beat, so it matches the textbook expectation while breaking the one the song itself set up. Songs whose every chorus adds a layer belong here too.
- **Hybrid** (Justin Bieber, "Anyone"): a combination, here release cues into a final chorus that starts sparse for a few seconds before an upbeat brings in the full texture.

**Osborn's terminally climactic form** <Source book="osborn13" /> describes songs whose most memorable moment is **not** a repeated chorus but a **single, new section at the end**:

- The **terminal climax** brings in new material (melody and lyrics not heard before), happens **once**, and works as a real main section, not a short coda. It usually takes over what a chorus would normally do: a repeated hook, often the song's title, the clearest statement of the home key, the highest vocal note, a half-time or anthemic groove.
- The verse and chorus are deliberately **understated**, so they don't compete with the ending. Osborn found the reverse a useful clue: when verse and chorus barely contrast, expect the contrast to arrive at the end.
- It differs from Spicer's **cumulative** ending (Journey's "Don't Stop Believin'"), which gets its power by bringing back earlier material all at once. A terminal climax is a surprise: the listener can't know what it will be on first hearing.
- Archetypes, counted by **section groups** (runs of sections that share a character): **two-part** (verse–chorus group → climactic group), **three-part** (verse–chorus group → a multi-section bridge group, often in a third key → climactic group), and **extended** (the bridge group splits again into contrasting loud and soft groups).
- **Where it falls.** In two-part forms the climax starts near the golden section of the track; across all his examples it averages about 0.71 of the duration, later in longer forms because the middle grows.

### The climactic ladder

Osborn's model for why this works <Source book="osborn13" where="pp. 5–9" /> <Source book="osborn10" where="Ch. 2" />:

- **Rock climaxes are sections, not moments.** Hooks repeat for a whole section, and studio compression flattens momentary peaks, so loudness changes come from adding or removing parts. The result is terraced plateaus rather than one peak.
- **The ladder.** Picture sections as rungs of memorability: intro, bridge and outro low; verse above them; chorus at the top. A terminal climax needs a rung *above* the chorus.
- **Two kinds of evidence.** *Syntactic* climax comes from writing: a repeated hook (especially repeated words), added voices or a new texture, a meter change (especially to half-time or double-time), a key change (especially to the relative major or minor). *Statistical* climax is measurable: highest or lowest pitch, loudest or quietest section, fastest or slowest rhythm.
- **Map each parameter onto the rungs.** In Radiohead's "Faust Arp" the highest sung note rises from verse to chorus to climax. In Saves the Day's "Rocks, Tonic, Juice, Magic" the hook is sung once per verse, twice per chorus, four times in the climax. In a live Dashboard Confessional performance the guitars go from palm-muted (verse) to open (chorus) to an extra overdrive pedal (climax), and the drummer from closed hi-hat to ride to a crash wash.

## Tool's "Pushit" as Osborn reads it

Osborn's extended example is Tool's "Pushit" (*Ænima*), analysed in both the article and his dissertation <Source book="osborn13" where="pp. 23–27, Ex. 23" /> <Source book="osborn10" where="Ch. 3" />:

- **Verse–chorus group (to about 2:57).** Out of a swarming noise intro, the verse riff is metrically ambiguous: you can count it as fast 12/8 or as 6/4, and Danny Carey's open hi-hat on every third attack against a duple kick and snare adds the dissonance. Verse and chorus share **the same guitar-and-drum groove**; the chorus is distinguished by overdriven, doubled guitars and ride and crash cymbals instead of slightly dirty guitar and closed hi-hat. The first two minutes work as **one growing arc**.
- **Loud group.** Justin Chancellor changes the bass notes under the unchanged guitar riff, his habitual way to make contrast in a bridge (Osborn also points to "Forty-six & 2" and "Schism"). Then the guitar joins in power chords and the meter settles, for the first time, into clear 12/8.
- **Soft group (4:16).** The music flips at once to a plainly duple 6/4 groove, with electronic percussion, clean guitar and an improvised vocal.
- **Loud group (6:26).** The soft group's bass groove continues under a guitar solo and becomes the unifying thread; a new riff and the voice return around 7:25.
- **Climactic group (8:22).** A call-and-response between band and voice, with the song's **highest sung note** in a lyrical, *bel canto* tone, notated without barlines because the groups of two and three are free. A stop and a freer passage lead to a held scream, then a 6/8 groove (9:06) under the **terminal climax** (9:18). Instead of a sing-along hook, which Osborn calls outside Tool's style, the lyrics keep developing and only the last line repeats, for closure.
- **Outro.** A short unison tag stops on a **non-tonic** chord, leaving the song unresolved.

His chart of the shorter "Hooker with a Penis" shows the three-part type: three verse–chorus pairs with metric shifts along the way (grooves in 5/8, 11/8 and 6/8), a bridge built on metric contrast, two climactic hooks working together, and then a short outro built from the opening guitar riff. Osborn notes that Tool often ends this way: the opening riff comes back briefly after the climax to tie the song together ("Forty-six & 2", "Eulogy", "H.", "Ænema").

Osborn also notes how terminal climaxes are prepared: mainly by **adding and removing layers** rather than playing louder, by **saving register** for the end, by **saving harmonic clarity** for the end, and by repeating hooks more at each level (verse, then chorus, then climax).

### Why bands end with something new

Osborn interviewed two bands about it <Source book="osborn10" where="App. D" />. Matt Carter of Emery gave the plainest reasons: the band gets bored playing a third chorus night after night, and it's easier to write a new, more intense part than to dress up an old one; the heaviest part belongs at the end, because if the favourite part is in the middle, why listen on; and if every part is heavy, none of it is. He added that once a song slows down or changes feel, speeding back up to the opening rarely feels natural, so songs tend to turn into "one long adventure", and that sections can still be verses by *function* even when they don't share chords. The post-rock band Sleepy Eyes of Death said their final builds are conscious but they try not to make them a formula, and that they edit hard: urgency tires the ear after a few minutes. Osborn's own conclusion is that nobody sets out to write a terminally climactic form; it appears when a band experiments with not recapitulating.

For a story-driven band, Osborn's reading of Coheed and Cambria adds one more argument: a plot's climactic moment belongs in the song **once**. Repeating it three times as a chorus would be like a novel repeating its climax.

## A third shape: cumulative build

Mark Spicer's "(ac)cumulative form" (summarised in the Master source list; the article is paywalled and not yet read here) describes songs built by **adding layers one at a time** over a stable base until the full texture arrives. It's the post-rock model, and the natural partner of Reich's gradual process (Module 5).

Moore, who calls the song-length version **bolero form** after Ravel, adds the details that make it work <Source book="moore12" where="Ch. 6, pp. 175–176; Ch. 3, p. 85" />:

- **Growth with one cessation.** Doves' "Satellites" grows across four verse–chorus units (the third without voice), not quite steadily, then strips everything away except the voice for the last verse, and grows the final chorus to the maximum: louder, richer, denser, with more reverb. The single drop before the end is what makes the last climb hit.
- **Sections matter less than the sweep.** In a bolero form, the repeats of verse and chorus aren't the point; the listener is meant to feel gradually swept away. Texture changes are the form, but each one has to point towards the whole.
- **Cumulation most often opens a track.** Deep Purple's "Smoke on the Water" introduces guitar, hi-hat, snare and kick, bass, and finally voice and organ; New Order's "Blue Monday" works the same way. Building the intro this way is a standard move. Building the whole song this way is the rarer, larger decision.
- **It can be combined with an arch.** Deep Purple's "Child in Time" grows its opening section to a climax (texture and the singer's range), breaks into an improvised middle, then repeats the whole directed growth. See *Form design* for arch forms.

## An additive build across a whole song

Rob Bowman's analysis of Otis Redding's 1966 "Try a Little Tenderness" is the most detailed account of a song-length build in the reading so far <Source book="moore03" where="Ch. 6 (Bowman)" />. The song is a 1933 Tin Pan Alley standard; Redding keeps the melody, chords and words within a recognisable range and builds a different piece out of the performance:

- **A1** is almost empty: guitar and bass on beat 1, single fills from piano, organ, guitar and sax.
- **A2**: the drummer enters with only a rim click and closed hi-hat.
- **B**: the piano fills more often, the organ brightens, ninth chords appear.
- **A3**: the guitar plays a continuous comp, organ and piano both comp, the horns hold pads for the first time.
- **The full kit arrives on the pick-up to bar 65.** Holding it back that long is delayed gratification, and bringing it in at the end of a strain avoids a cadence and drives straight into the next section.
- **C**, a new section Redding added, repeats an eight-bar tag: four bars of tension (chords every two beats rising chromatically, horns on every off-beat, call and response at close range) and four bars of release (descending, the title line, attacks on the beat). The third time through, a two-bar break of voice, kick and hi-hat is the climax.

Meanwhile, the drummer raised the tempo from about 96 to 114 bpm so evenly that the beat length shrinks steadily across the whole track, and Redding's vocal rasp is applied the same way: light, then heavier, withheld for half the bridge, peaking at the break. Every parameter rises together, which is the condition Farbood found gives the clearest rise in felt tension (see *Building tension*). Bowman also notes a change of logic: the written A and B strains are linear, while the added C section is circular, a repeated cell rather than a story.

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. For each song, choose **one** primary shape: telos chorus, stairstepper, release, one-more-time, terminal climax, or cumulative.
2. Write down what you're **saving** for the peak: the highest vocal note, the clearest tonic, the full band, a new groove (half-time or a new meter), or new lyrics.
3. Check the sections before the peak. Do any of them spend what you're saving?
4. Across a record, use **different shapes**. Four telos-chorus songs in a row is one shape repeated four times.

</Steps>

## The ending must be the biggest event

Belkin's chapter on endings <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 18 Ending" /> adds a warning that applies to every shape above. A loud ending has to be **the** climax of the whole piece: ending on a secondary climax disappoints. A fade-out ending has to fade further than any internal calm. And at the very end, **every dimension should agree** (register, loudness, harmony, texture). If only one dimension arrives, the ending sounds like a stop sign, not a conclusion. Module 4 takes this further.
