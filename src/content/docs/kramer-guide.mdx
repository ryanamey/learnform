## Jonathan D. Kramer, *The Time of Music* (1988) — chapter guide

Read in full, November 2026. The long analytic interludes were read by section, not bar by bar: ch. 5 (Beethoven op. 135), ch. 7 (Schoenberg op. 19 and Webern op. 29), the moment-by-moment walk through Stravinsky's *Symphonies of Wind Instruments* in ch. 9, and the numeric proportion analyses in ch. 10. Two short parts of ch. 4 (4.10, 4.12) were skimmed. The appendices are proofs about Fibonacci numbers.

This guide is a paraphrase for finding your way back into the book, not a substitute for it. Each chapter lists its main ideas, what they mean for a band writing long riff-based songs, and where the app uses them.

Kramer's own framing: his categories of time are **ways of listening**, not boxes a piece fits into. One piece can be linear deep down and nonlinear on the surface, and meaning often comes from the two pulling against each other.

## Part 1 — Time, linearity and technology

### Ch. 1 · Music and time
- Music lives in two times at once: the clock time of the room and the "virtual" time the music creates. Deep listening puts the music's time first, and that time can be interrupted, reordered or suspended.
- **Linearity** is becoming: events follow from earlier events. **Nonlinearity** is being: principles that hold for the whole piece. Every piece has both.
- **App:** *Kinds of musical time*.

### Ch. 2 · Linearity and nonlinearity
- Linear = shaped by implications from earlier events. Nonlinear = shaped by the piece's standing rules (instrumentation, a constant texture, proportions), which you learn as you listen and then stop questioning.
- In tonal music the return home is certain, so the suspense is in the route and the timing. A surprise can change how everything after it is heard, even if it never comes back.
- **Nondirected linearity:** constant motion with goals you can't predict in advance.
- A constant texture becomes nonlinear once it's certain, so breaking it hits hard (the spinning-wheel figure stopping in *Gretchen am Spinnrade*).
- **Multiply-directed time:** clear direction, but frequently interrupted; goals arrive somewhere other than the end of the process that implied them. Needs recognisable gestures (beginning, cadence, climax, transition) placed in the "wrong" spot. Simply reshuffling a standard form doesn't count, because each process inside it is still linear.
- **Moment time:** self-contained sections, with the order seemingly arbitrary. The piece starts and stops rather than beginning and ending. Contrast between moments must dwarf contrast inside them.
- **Vertical time:** one extended present, no phrase hierarchy, no climax. The piece defines a bounded world early and must not step outside it.
- Which mode you hear depends on context and on the listener: familiarity can make vertical music feel linear.
- **App:** *Kinds of musical time* (five-mode lab); *Trance, ecstasy and the listening test* (vertical time).

### Ch. 3 · Technology
- Records are artworks in their own right, not documents of a performance. The splice makes instant discontinuity possible, and film editing (flashback, freeze, jump) offers models for musical time.
- Performers never play exact ratios; even "metronomic" playing keeps small, consistent deviations. Machines can play rhythms humans can't. A human struggling audibly with a hard rhythm has its own energy, which a machine version loses.
- "The recapitulation is on the disc": listeners who replay a record can absorb denser, less redundant music.
- **App:** background for Module 7 (the record as form); the machine vs. human feel point supports *Time feel*.

### Ch. 4 · Meter and rhythm
- Meter isn't a grid but a felt series of timepoints of different weight. Once established it has inertia and resists change; rhythm pushes against it.
- **Three accents:** *stress* (loud, sharp, after a silence), *rhythmic* (the focus of a group, often near its end), *metric* (always the start of a unit). When rhythmic and metric accents coincide after disagreeing, the moment is strongly articulated.
- A phrase is a rhythmic group; a hypermeasure is a metric unit. They are not the same thing.
- Irregular hypermeter comes from **extension**, **contraction** and **overlap** (one bar is both the end of one unit and the start of the next). Irregularity at one level can be absorbed at a deeper one.
- Cues for metric weight: new harmony, long notes, register, texture or timbre change, a new voice entering, contour change, dynamics. Ambiguity is expressive.
- Moment forms and vertical music have few metric levels.
- **App:** *Accent and hypermeter* (Module 2, hypermeter lab).

### Ch. 5 · Interlude: Beethoven, op. 135, first movement
- Linearity can come from processes, not only from harmony: a fragmented opening that gradually becomes continuous; a gesture in the wrong place that later makes sense; irregular groupings that settle into regular ones.
- **For a riff band:** a "problem" stated at the start (fragments, odd groupings, a misplaced gesture) can be the thread the whole song resolves.
- **App:** *Gestural time*.

## Part 2 — Beginnings, endings and discontinuity

### Ch. 6 · Beginnings, endings and temporal multiplicity
- Closure comes in degrees; the strongest closes several earlier phrases at once. Middles are the least conventional and most piece-specific part.
- After the goal arrives, the ending has to last long enough to use up the momentum. **Liquidation** strips the material down to generic figures so nothing implies a future.
- An ending must announce itself. If the listener needs the silence to know it's over, it failed.
- **Ending as process** (all tensions resolved) vs. **ending as product** (the closing gesture). A closing gesture in the wrong place gets applause too early.
- **Gestural time:** a gesture's meaning (opening, closing, transition) is recognisable out of context, so music can sound an ending first and a beginning later. Such reorderings work because of *how* they are paced; reordering a simple linear piece rarely improves it.
- Multiply-directed time needs strong underlying linearity: the more goal-directed the music, the stronger the reordering.
- **App:** *Gestural time: endings in the wrong place* (gesture lab). Complements Belkin on *Beginnings and introductions* and *Returning and ending*.

### Ch. 7 · Interlude: Schoenberg op. 19 no. 1, Webern op. 29
- Without tonality, linearity comes from texture, motive, meter and gesture. Clear meter can be a **goal** reached late rather than a starting assumption.
- Closure where two grouping systems finally end together (the same principle as accent convergence in ch. 4).
- **App:** *Accent and hypermeter*.

### Ch. 8 · Discontinuity and the moment
- Stockhausen's moment form: no build-up, no transitions, "immediately intensive", each moment centred in itself.
- Carter's objection: every moment should be led into and out of. Kramer's reply: listeners also remember a piece as an unordered whole (**cumulative listening**), and discontinuity makes them search memory for distant connections.
- Moments are self-contained through stasis or a process that completes inside them. Returns are fine if nothing builds up to them; if something does, the return becomes a recapitulation goal. Craft is needed to make the order *seem* arbitrary.
- Overall length is unrelated to moment lengths; a 4-second moment and a 2-minute one can carry equal weight.
- Stasis is relative: inside activity is small compared with the contrast between sections.
- Paradoxes: moment form uses linear listening to destroy linear time (a long moment after many short ones is felt immediately); it defeats memory, because nothing develops; yet its proportions depend on remembered lengths.
- **App:** *Moment form and strata*; the "stop" ending in the form-plan check.

### Ch. 9 · Interlude: Stravinsky, *Symphonies of Wind Instruments*
- Moments are distinguished by tempo, harmony type and cell material; a change in some but not all of them makes a sub-moment. Listeners learn as they go how much change marks a new moment.
- Cone's **stratification, interlock and synthesis**: strands alternate (A1 B1 A2 B2), the listener hears each strand as a continuing line, and later the strands merge. For long-range links to be heard, a strand's register or colour should stay unused in between.
- Moments of very different lengths have equal structural rank. A climax can still exist.
- **App:** *Moment form and strata* (strata lab).

## Part 3 — Duration, perception and timelessness

### Ch. 10 · Duration and proportion
- Proportions are easiest to perceive with static sections, strong cuts and no regular metric clock to count with.
- The golden section can nest; Howat's Debussy analyses show separate processes (dynamics, motive, texture) each with its own golden-section shape, and their coincidence making the big arrival. Kramer found mostly other consistent ratios in Stravinsky.
- Proportion is one source of balance among several. It matters most where linear drive doesn't swamp it, and context, not an abstract ratio, decides whether a passage is too long. Intent doesn't matter; the result does.
- **App:** *How long it feels* (proportion section); *Designing the whole*. Treated as a planning tool, not a law of perception.

### Ch. 11 · The perception of musical time
- Duration in passing differs from duration remembered.
- **Time as information:** patterned material is stored compactly, so it is remembered as shorter; unpatterned material as longer. Both empty and extremely dense intervals feel longer than moderately filled ones; an ultra-dense texture fuses into one low-information sound.
- Over short spans meter dominates our sense of duration; over long spans information does. Tempo is a third factor.
- Listeners alternate between a cumulative mode (building a memory image) and an active mode (timing and expectation). Rhythmic, metric music pulls toward timing; static music toward information.
- **Specious present:** about 8–10 seconds; the phrase is the basic unit of perception.
- **App:** *How long it feels* (duration lab); *What listeners actually hear*; *How a form is remembered*.

### Ch. 12 · Time and timelessness
- Vertical music erases past and future and resists chunking. Unsympathetic listeners hear stopped time (boredom); sympathetic ones hear timelessness. It demands active listening to the inside of the sound.
- Kramer's *Vexations* experience: linear listening, then boredom, then a switch into an expanded present where tiny changes become huge.
- Tension inside stasis: *Les Moutons de Panurge* keeps small linear threads (a pulse emerging, a note left hanging for minutes) inside narrow limits; its start and end are linear and exciting.
- Fraser's time levels map onto Kramer's modes (atemporal ↔ vertical; prototemporal ↔ moment; eotemporal ↔ multiply-directed; biotemporal ↔ nondirected; nootemporal ↔ linear), and can operate on different levels of one piece.
- **App:** *Trance, ecstasy and the listening test* (vertical time); *Repetition, process and absorption* (anatomy of a process piece).
