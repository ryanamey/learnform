## The same tempo can feel half as fast or twice as fast

Jose Garza's study of post-2000 metal (black, death, doom, grindcore, metalcore, progressive, sludge and thrash) gives your drummer a precise vocabulary <Source book="garza" where="[5]–[9]" />. **Time feel** is the perceived pulse created by the rate at which the kick and snare alternate, *not* by the tempo. Change the feel and the music seems faster or slower while the beat stays the same.

<Steps title="The feels">

- **Normal time:** snare on 2 and 4, the default backbeat.
- **Half time:** snare on 3. Slower and more spacious; common in breakdowns.
- **Quarter time:** kick and snare alternate once per bar. Even slower.
- **Double time:** backbeats on the off-beats between beats.
- **Faster feels:** quadruple time (a kick–snare pair on every eighth, the traditional blast beat) and beyond. In compound meters (12/8) Garza counts **triple time** (three times normal) and **sextuple time**. He suggests renotating very fast feels at a faster tempo in a familiar feel.
- **Quarter time** in his use: kick and snare alternate once per bar, the kick on strong hyperbeats and the snare on weak ones.

Two refinements: the **backbeat** belongs to the feel, not to beats 2 and 4 (in double time it falls on the "ands", in half time on beat 3), and in 12/8 the same feels apply to the dotted-quarter beats. Listeners judge tempo partly by the kick–snare rate, so a feel change is heard as a speed change even when the beat stays put. And post-2000 metal bands jump between feels freely inside one song rather than picking one.

</Steps>

## Cymbals and riffs can agree or argue

Garza's key move is to show that **guitar, bass and the timekeeping cymbal either reinforce, clarify or contradict** the feel set by kick and snare.

- **Cymbal rate:** once per beat (crash or china on each beat, typical of breakdowns), beat divisions (hats or ride in 8ths, the most versatile), every other beat (sparse, for very slow feels), and subdivisions (16ths, rare at speed). More cymbal hits per bar reads as a faster feel.
- **Riff types** <Source book="garza" where="[10]–[18]" />, each with feels it usually goes with:
  - *Straight, open divisions*: unmuted steady eighths; the unmarked default, normal time, often under a chorus so the vocal comes forward.
  - *Breakdown*: palm-muted, low, uneven rhythms in unison with the kick, with silence between notes; slow feels, crash or China cymbals; the sound of "heaviness". (A breakdown *section* doesn't require this riff type, and vice versa.)
  - *Long durations*: notes longer than a beat; quarter and half time, doom.
  - *Pedal tone*: a palm-muted low note running underneath open higher notes; normal and double time; "constant power".
  - *Weak-beat syncopation*: unmuted, rhythms borrowed from both kick and snare; double time. Where breakdown riffs are the rhythm, syncopated riffs follow the drums.
  - *Tremolo*: constant double-picking; feels faster than double time, blast beats.
- **Cymbal rate:** once per beat (normal and half time; a China in a breakdown cuts through the syncopation), beat divisions (the most versatile; the default in normal time, also double time, and the black-metal "wash" of sound), every other beat (quarter time, and a heavier half time), and subdivisions (only at extreme speeds, where hands allow). Three generalizations: fewer cymbal hits per bar reads as slower; eighth-note cymbals are the most flexible; and drummers almost never let the kick–snare alternation outrun the cymbal. Closed hats are avoided in breakdowns because they lack weight.

**Agreement clarifies, contradiction creates dialogue** <Source book="garza" where="[29]–[38]" />. When the drum pattern alone is ambiguous (double or quadruple time? half time with a displaced snare, or normal time with a missing one?), the riff type and cymbal decide it. When they point to a *different* feel from the kick and snare, the passage carries two feels at once:

- **Contained intensity.** A quarter-time kick and snare under a pedal-tone riff and a ride in eighths: urgency held back, released in the phrase's last bar with open sixteenths and a fill.
- **Fast and slow at once.** Blast-beat kick and snare under long notes and a cymbal every other beat. In black metal, blast beats with tremolo picking can sound strangely static; mixing in slow elements makes the music seem fast and slow together.
- **Feel as narrative.** In one song Garza reads fast-feel traits as action and slow-feel traits as a call to action: the verses describing what the villains do are fast, the switch to quarter time comes when the singer turns to the listener, and the ending combines both, the listeners now acting.
- **A compound riff** changes type within one player's part (breakdown, pedal tone, tremolo inside four bars), so the contradiction is horizontal, inside one instrument, rather than between instruments.

<KeyIdea>A new section doesn't need a new riff. Change the feel, change the cymbal, or set them against each other.</KeyIdea>

## The backbeat is the consonance

Biamonte argues that the standard rock beat (kick on 1 and 3, snare on 2 and 4, hats in eighths) shouldn't be heard as syncopation <Source book="biamonte" where="§6" />. It's the **timeline** the other parts are organized around, it runs through the whole song, and nobody expects it to resolve, so she calls it a *displacement consonance*. Her analogy is the dominant-seventh chord in the blues: it's the basic chord there, and tension comes from altering it, not from resolving it. In rock, tension comes from patterns set *against* the backbeat.

What she says about the beat itself is useful to a drummer:

- **Low on 1 and 3, high on 2 and 4.** The pattern is defined by register, not by drums: stomps and claps in Queen's "We Will Rock You", a guitar riff that doubles it in Hendrix's "Foxy Lady". Listeners still feel an accent on 1 and 3.
- **The snare is a rebound.** Because the kick rings longer, she hears the snare as a reaction to the kick rather than a lead-in to the next beat. Subdivisions change that: **pickups into the snare** make the backbeat lean forward (anacrustic); **kick pairs on 1 and 3** make the snare sound more like a rebound (continuational).
- **Where the snare sits** in microtiming does the same (she draws on Butterfield's research). An early snare, on top of the beat, adds drive and nervous energy; a late snare, behind the beat, sounds relaxed. A late backbeat is often described as the norm.

### The clave family

The other common family of rock patterns comes from the clave: the **tresillo** (3+3+2 eighths), the **double tresillo** (3+3+3+3+2+2 sixteenths, the guitar in AC/DC's "For Those About to Rock"), and the **son clave** (3+3+4+2+4) <Source book="biamonte" where="§3, §6" />. Each starts and ends in line with the bar but is out of phase in the middle, so it moves from dissonance to consonance every bar or two. That's why, in her survey, they're often played by several instruments together, while a straight displacement usually stays in one part.

## Re-grooving

Moore's term for one more kind of change: **re-grooving**, where the meter stays (or nearly stays) the same but the way the band articulates it changes <Source book="moore12" where="Ch. 3, pp. 52, 66–67" />. In the Beatles' "We Can Work It Out", two bars of 3/4 in the bridge are heard as drawn-out triplets against the 4/4, not as a new meter. The Fall's "Mr. Pharmacist" moves from shuffle to straight and back. Tractor's "Hope in Favour" slides from straight 4/4 eighths into 12/16 so smoothly that the join is hard to find. Radiohead's "Paranoid Android" combines both kinds of change: sections in different tempos, and meter changes within one tempo.

For a band, re-grooving is a section change the listener feels in the body without hearing new material: same riff, new subdivision or new kit pattern. It sits between a time-feel change (this page) and a full meter change.

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. Take one riff and play it in **three feels**: half, normal and double. You now have three sections of different energy, all from the same material. That's the most economical development there is.
2. Use **cymbal rate as an energy control** independent of the kick and snare: crash quarters for weight, hats in 16ths for urgency, no cymbal for a dry, exposed section.
3. Use **contradiction for climaxes**: a slow-feel kick and snare under a fast-feel riff and cymbal gives "fast and slow at once", a tension that wants release.
4. Write the feel into the form plan for every section (the riff board's *Feel* field).

</Steps>

<ListenerSide grade="moderate">

Farbood found that onset density and tempo are among the strongest drivers of felt tension, after loudness and pitch height. Feel and cymbal rate are exactly those levers, changed without touching the tempo. Janata and colleagues found that the easier a rhythm is to move to, the more groove listeners report. Half-time sections are easy to couple to, so they hit physically. Busier feels raise arousal.

</ListenerSide>
