## A rigid frame, rotating riffs

Olivia Lucas's study of Meshuggah's *obZen* (2008) gives the clearest model of how odd riffs and steady structure coexist <Source book="lucas" where="[8]–[11]" />:

- The songs are built from **segments of 8, 16 or 32 bars of 4/4**. That hypermetric frame is the form. It's often carried by a steady cymbal pulse, a snare backbeat, or long notes in the lead guitar.
- **Riffs of other lengths**, for example 9 eighths, 7 quarters or 25 sixteenths, loop inside the frame and drift in and out of phase with it.
- At the end of a segment the riff is **cut off mid-stride**, and the next riff starts on the next downbeat. That cut-off is where the band's characteristic lurch comes from.
- The songs don't use verses and choruses. Segments without vocals alternate with segments with vocals, a song may use a dozen riffs with only one or two coming back, and the segments don't tell the listener where in the song they are. The **frame**, not a chorus, holds the song together.

The band describes it the same way. Everything is conceived and counted in 4/4 and learned in chunks of four, eight or sixteen bars, and rhythm guitarist Mårten Hagström says they don't start from crazy patterns: they find the groove first and work with "the weird stuff" until it makes sense <Source book="lucas" where="notes 1, 8" />. They compose by programming, and drummer Tomas Haake says he resists writing what the body can't play by "feeling" his way through it. Live, the title track of *obZen* is played about 10 bpm slower than the record to let the groove breathe.

### How present the frame is

The 4/4 is usually audible somewhere (cymbals on the quarters, a snare backbeat, whole notes in the lead guitar), but how loud it is changes during a song. In "Bleed" the cymbals state the quarters clearly until about 1:25, then switch to eighths and blur; at 1:40 they join the guitar pattern, leaving the vocals as the only layer still carrying the 4/4, until the lead guitar marks the downbeats from 1:58. The frame stays in the structure while its *audibility* is turned up and down, and that is a dial you can write. Lucas also refuses to choose one correct hearing: a listener who follows the 4/4 and feels "weirdness" against it and a listener who hears two cycles at once are both getting the music.

Chancellor describes the same principle from inside Tool: put four beats behind any odd-meter riff and you can make it feel straight. The band overlaps time signatures on purpose <Source book="tool-interviews" />.

<KeyIdea>The frame is the form; the misalignment is the content. The listener rides the frame and feels the riff rotate against it.</KeyIdea>

## Beginning in the middle

Lucas adds a subtler idea. A riff's **temporal beginning** (what you hear first) and its **rhythmic beginning** (what *sounds* like the start, once accents and long notes are weighed) can differ. As the riff repeats, listeners may re-hear where it starts, so it seems to have begun before it began. Long durations tend to sound like endings. Strong attacks and register peaks tend to sound like starts.

Her case studies show the same frame-and-cut-off logic handled in different ways:

- **Lethargica:** a 23-beat riff (5+6+6+6, paired like question and answer) whose heard start and felt start agree. It loops twice and most of a third time in sixteen bars and is cut off when the vocals enter, which makes the seam obvious.
- **Pineal Gland Optics:** a pitch pattern of 8 attacks against a rhythm pattern of 14 attacks, realigning every 56 attacks. The riff's longest note, a dotted eighth near its end, gradually comes to sound like its *start*, and this re-hearing is clearest when it coincides with the lead guitar's slow pitch change. At the next section, that long-note gesture falls exactly on the downbeat, so the figure that blurred the riff's beginning becomes the clean entry of the next segment. The boundary is kept, but softened.
- **Pravus:** riffs whose first heard note is functionally an ending. In one segment (a 9-eighth riff) the vocals run over the start, so it sounds as if the riff had been going on underneath all along; it's cut off exactly where its third accented note is expected, which makes the boundary feel abrupt. In another (a 7-quarter riff split 3+4 by a short "catching" note), the riff restarts halfway through the segment, which you only notice afterwards, and the next segment begins with the same rhythm on new pitches, which blurs the join.

So the cut-off is a **design choice**. Lucas's four cases give four kinds of seam: an abrupt cut, a riff whose ending lands on the boundary, a cut at the moment of highest expectation, and a join blended by a shared rhythm. In all four, the riff yields to the frame in the end. Other options exist in the literature: altering the riff's last repetition so it realigns with the frame (Lucas, citing Pieslak and Capuzzo, calls these **tails**), or, as on Meshuggah's later album *Koloss* (2012), letting riffs spill over the segment edge.

## Fitting the riff to the frame: tails

Jonathan Pieslak's 2007 article, the first music-theory study of Meshuggah and the one Lucas builds on, describes the earlier albums (*Destroy Erase Improve*, *Chaosphere*, *Nothing*) as using the tail rather than the cut-off <Source book="pieslak" where="pp. 220–225" />:

- **"Rational Gaze"** (about 134 bpm): guitars, bass and kick play four bars of 25/16 and one of 28/16, which adds up to 128 sixteenths, exactly eight bars of 4/4. Against them the cymbals keep quarter notes with the snare on beat 3, so the listener hears four-bar hypermeasures.
- **"Stengah"**: five bars of 11/8, then a sixth shortened by a quarter note to 9/8. That's 64 eighths, again eight bars of 4/4.
- **"New Millennium Cyanide Christ"**: a repeated 23/16 riff. Pieslak later re-hears it as a group, the same group again, and a three-sixteenth fragment of it (10 + 10 + 3), since at about 144 bpm counting it as a meter makes little sense.

His key distinction: this is **not quite metric dissonance** in Krebs's sense. A true 25-against-16 dissonance would have to run sixteen times before the layers realign. Meshuggah instead **lengthens or shortens one statement** so the riff block fits a symmetrical frame of 4, 8 or 16 bars. The odd meter and mixed meter on the surface exist *to serve* a square hypermeter. Pieslak contrasts this with King Crimson, Rush and Zappa, whose odd meters don't add up to symmetrical units; Everett called that kind of irregular meter a source of asymmetric phrase rhythm. The early 5/16-over-4/4 riff in "Humiliative" is the plain dissonant case: it realigns after five bars and the hypermeter carries on.

So you have three ways for an odd riff to meet the end of a frame. Each sounds different:

| Seam | What happens | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Tail** | The last statement is lengthened or shortened so the riff ends on the frame | The riff and the frame agree at the boundary; the odd meter feels designed |
| **Cut-off** | The riff keeps its length and is stopped mid-stride | A lurch; the frame wins |
| **Drift** | No adjustment; the riff only realigns when the arithmetic says so | True metric dissonance; boundaries blur |

## When the riff stops looping: streams

Pieslak's main case study is *I* (2004), a single 21-minute track in about fourteen sections, and it changes the method <Source book="pieslak" where="pp. 225–241" />. Here there are **no short repeating riffs**: patterns repeat over long spans (32 beats, for example), so a time signature no longer describes anything. He replaces meter with three tools:

- **Grouping** (Lerdahl & Jackendoff) and **pattern recurrence** (Yeston): find where a pattern comes back, at whatever length.
- **Attack-point intervals**: write the riff as the gaps between attacks in sixteenths (2-2-4, 2-1-2-2-2-3…) together with its pitches. Two passages are related when their interval strings match, even if one is shortened or lengthened at the end. This is the most practical tool on the page for a band: it's how you can notate and compare riffs that have no meter.
- **Textural accent** (Lester): the entrance or exit of the voice or a solo guitar marks the 4- and 8-bar units when the drums no longer do.

What he finds:

- **The hypermeter survives without a meter.** In the passage 3:35–4:50 (sections of 4, 8 and 4 hypermeasures, each 16 beats), the A section is a two-hypermeasure group heard twice, and each half is one hypermeasure-long stream. Section B has no such repeated unit: it's one long group heard twice with small changes (the second ends differently). The missing middle level comes from **another layer**: a solo guitar melody of four notes, eight beats each, repeated four times, which marks every two hypermeasures. When the riff stops marking a level of the structure, give that job to another instrument.
- **Pitch can do the drums' job.** In 1:55–3:35 (8, 4 and 8 hypermeasures, the mirror of the later passage) the guitars barely move in pitch, and the change from one pitch to the next marks the section boundaries. Meanwhile the hi-hat on quarters and the snare on the off-beats run unchanged as a second stream against the guitars.
- **A transition can be sized to finish a cycle.** In the second A section the solo melody is 22 beats long, and 22 doesn't divide the section's 128 beats. The transition into the next passage, a whammy-bar dive lasting exactly four beats, brings the total to 132 = 6 × 22. The frame marks the section, and the length of the transition lets the misaligned layer complete its sixth statement.
- **Small-scale techniques recur as large-scale form.** Two quiet clean-guitar sections divide *I* into three nearly equal parts (0:00–7:47, 8:40–14:42, 16:01–21:00). But a clear surface meter appears only twice, a 7/4 opening and a clear 4/4 at 10:34, which splits the piece into two halves. Two-part and three-part readings coexist: a "formal dissonance" that mirrors the layered rhythm inside it. The 7/4 opening is itself a quotation of an Anthrax riff ("Time"), which lasts one hypermeasure and then gives way.

Not everyone hears it the same way: Osborn accepts that 4/4 doesn't fit the *I* passages but hears combinations of 3/4 and 4/4 in them rather than meterless streams, and finds Pieslak's barless notation better suited to Dillinger Escape Plan <Source book="osborn10" where="Ch. 1, n. 81" />. For your own notation, use whichever version the band can count together.

<KeyIdea>Odd on the surface, square underneath. Meshuggah's odd meters are mostly adjusted to land on a symmetrical frame, and when the riffs stop repeating, the frame is still there, carried by melody, pitch changes and texture.</KeyIdea>

### Freedom and control

Lucas reads the riff/frame struggle through Robert Walser's account of metal as a contest between controlling power (bass, drums, rhythm guitar) and escape (the solo, the voice). In Meshuggah the riff tries to deform the frame and the frame almost always wins, which matches lyrics about escaping deceptive systems. Whether you want your riffs to lose, win or negotiate is part of what a section says.

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. **Fix the frame first.** Choose the segment length (8, 16 or 32 bars of 4/4) and what carries it: the hats, the snare backbeat, a crash on each segment start, or a long-note melody.
2. **Choose the riff length for the cycle you want.** Its realignment with the frame is the landmark. Check where it falls (the lab shows it).
3. **Decide each seam:** cut off mid-stride (a lurch), add a tail so the riff ends exactly on the boundary (smooth), let it drift across, or carry the rhythm into the next section with new pitches (a blurred join).
4. **Use rotation for surprise.** Starting a familiar riff from a different note makes the listener re-hear where it begins, without writing a new riff. Put the riff's longest note or highest accent where you want it to sound like a beginning.
5. **Decide how audible the frame is, section by section.** Cymbals on the quarters, then on the eighths, then following the riff: the frame stays, the listener's grip on it changes.
6. **Write long riffs as attack-point intervals.** For riffs too long or irregular for a time signature, notate the gaps between attacks (in sixteenths) plus the pitches. Compare sections by their interval strings, and decide which other layer (voice, lead melody, pitch change, cymbals) marks the hypermeter.
7. **Size transitions with the arithmetic.** If a layer doesn't divide the section, a fill, a dive or a held chord of the right length can let it finish its last statement.

</Steps>

<ListenerSide grade="moderate">

The frame is what makes this listenable. Vuust & Witek: complexity engages when it contradicts the meter without undermining it, and the steady 4/4 frame is the meter that's never undermined. Lucas also saw two audiences at once at a Meshuggah show, some moshing and some counting. In her reading both were looking for the groove, just in different ways, and ambiguous passages make listeners build the meter actively rather than receive it (Butler). Pieslak's interviews with fans (twenty formal interviews and talks at twenty-seven concerts) point the same way: they named the structure itself, "the notes", as the main attraction, even when they lacked the theory terms for it <Source book="pieslak" where="pp. 243–244" />.

<Exercise title="Build a frame-and-cycle section">

1. Drummer: a 16-bar segment of 4/4 with one clear frame marker, such as a crash every 4 bars.
2. Riff: a cell of 9, 11 or 13 sixteenths, looped from bar 1 and cut off at bar 17.
3. Make two versions of the seam into the next section: a hard cut-off, and a blend (keep the rhythm, change the pitches).
4. Try the riff rotated: start it from its third note. Does the listener re-decide where it begins?
5. Now write a **tail**: work out the remainder (16 bars = 256 sixteenths, minus the full statements) and recompose the last statement to that length so the riff ends exactly on bar 17. Compare it with the cut-off version.

</Exercise>
