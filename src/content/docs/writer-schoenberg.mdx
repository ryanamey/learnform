## Arnold Schoenberg, *Fundamentals of Musical Composition*

Schoenberg (1874–1951) wrote this book in the United States between 1937 and 1948 as a textbook for his students at UCLA. It was unfinished when he died; Gerald Strang and Leonard Stein completed and edited it, and it was published in 1967. Most of its examples are from Beethoven's piano sonatas.

**How it was read for the app:** every prose page, chapters I–XX and the appendix, from a scanned copy. The music examples are images and are known only through their captions and Schoenberg's descriptions; any example played in the app is re-notated from a public-domain score.

## His question

How does a composer make music **comprehensible**? His answer starts from the motive and builds upward: the motive is varied, phrases are built from its forms, phrases make themes, themes make larger forms. Form means organization, the parts working like those of a living organism, and one can only understand what one can keep in mind <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. I" />.

He teaches through **practice forms**: deliberately simplified, stiff models for students, not descriptions of masterworks. He says so himself, and accepts that exercise results will sound mechanical at first.

## His toolkit

- **The motive** is defined by use: anything treated as a motive by being repeated. It is the germ of the piece (Ch. III).
- **Variation**: change less important features, keep important ones. Preserving rhythm is singled out as the strongest source of coherence (Ch. III, VII).
- **Developing variation** versus **variant**: changes that have consequences for what follows, versus local decoration (Ch. III).
- **The phrase**: about a breath, ending like a comma; length depends on tempo (Ch. II).
- **The sentence** (which he identified) and **the period** as the two ways of building a theme; the sentence ranked higher (Ch. V–VIII).
- **Complementary repetition**: a tonic form answered by a dominant form (Ch. V).
- **Liquidation**: removing characteristic features until nothing demands continuation (Ch. VIII).
- **Melody versus theme**: balance versus consequences (Ch. XI).
- **The accompaniment** has its own motive, kept, modified, liquidated or dropped (Ch. IX).
- **Advice for self-criticism**: play melody and harmony separately, watch the highest note, try removing bars (Ch. XII).
- **Irregularity**: odd lengths are fine when they have a reason (Ch. XIV).
- **Larger forms**: ternary, minuet, scherzo, variations, rondo, sonata; he prefers "elaboration" to "development" (Ch. XIII–XX).

## Where he agrees with the others

- With all three: repetition is needed for intelligibility, and repetition alone is monotonous.
- With Caplin and Belkin: units shorten toward a goal (liquidation, fragmentation, shorter phrases).
- With Snyder: rhythm is remembered more easily than intervals; the phrase is the size of a breath.
- With Caplin: the deletion test, trying the music without some bars to see what they do.

## Where he differs

- **One motive for everything?** He comes closest to it; Belkin calls it debatable; Caplin's functions don't depend on it. → *Seminar: Does everything grow from one motive?*
- **Sentence over period.** He ranks them; Caplin doesn't. → *Seminar: Sentence and period*
- **Looseness and popular music.** He links looseness to the neglect of deeper implications; Caplin treats looseness as a skill, not a weakness (Course IX).
- **Coda, scherzo, plagal cadence.** Caplin disagrees with him on each (Course X; *Riff harmony*).
- **Pedal points.** He is sceptical of them except for constructive uses; Snyder describes drones as a way of keeping tonality alive in memory (Course VIII).
- **A popular touch.** He notes that the classical masters sought it too, citing Mattheson: a theme should contain something the world already knows (Ch. V).

## Where the app uses him

Course I throughout (motive, variation, developing variation, phrase, sentence and liquidation, vocal line); Course IV (the motive of the accompaniment); Course VII (climax, condensation); Course IX and X (tight and loose, transitions, codas, larger forms).

## How to read him

Start with chapters I–V and VIII, then IX, XI and XII. Read slowly: the book is short and dense, and each chapter assumes you will write the exercises. The examples are worth hearing; most are Beethoven sonatas, freely available in public-domain scores and many recordings.
