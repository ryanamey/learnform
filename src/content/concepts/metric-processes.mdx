## The meter has a plot

Krebs's central idea is that "the meter" of a piece is more than its time signature <Source book="krebs" where="Ch. 4" />. A piece has a **primary consonance** (the meter you'd write at the top), but it is rarely sounding plainly all the time. Antimetrical layers fade in and out, get stronger and weaker, change into related dissonances and resolve. He calls this succession the **metrical progression**, and reading it tells you a lot about a piece:

- Does one dissonance keep coming back, even when the theme doesn't? Then it works as a **motive**.
- Is the piece built from one **family** of related dissonances (say, D2+1 and its slower relatives D4+2 and D8+4)?
- How much is dissonant, how much consonant? A mostly consonant piece with a few incursions is a different animal from a mostly dissonant one with islands of consonance (compare Tool's corpus profile on *Metric dissonance as a section marker*).
- How often does the state change, and does that rate change? That's the **metrical volatility** of a section.

Boil the progression down and you get a basic **metrical narrative**: C–D–C (starts settled, grows dissonant, settles again), C–D (ends unresolved), or D–C (starts dissonant, resolves). Krebs doesn't think a strict, Schenker-style reduction works for meter (duration matters too much, and there are too few consonances to reduce to), so he uses these informal bird's-eye views.

<KeyIdea>Don't just choose a meter for each section. Choose what happens to the dissonance across the song: where it's hinted, where it grows, what it turns into, and how it resolves.</KeyIdea>

## Mapping it

A **metrical map** lists each dissonance (and any consonance other than the main one) down the left side and draws a line over a bar grid wherever it's active: solid for strong, dotted for weak. Gaps are the primary consonance. Two maps side by side show at a glance that two pieces share a narrative (both D–C, say) but differ in how often they change state. It's the meter map from your analysis kit, with more detail.

## Getting into a dissonance

- **Abruptly**, usually by adding an antimetrical layer on top of the established meter (much more common), or by dropping the metrical layers and starting new ones.
- **Prepared.** One or two antimetrical accents inside a consonant passage hint at a dissonance that only arrives fully later, like a suspension prepared in harmony. Schumann scatters such hints, sometimes many bars earlier, so the full dissonance feels like a consequence rather than an accident. A single off-beat accent in your intro can prepare the displaced riff of your bridge.

## Changing a dissonance from inside

| Process | What it is | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Intensification / deintensification** | Same dissonance, made more or less audible: more continuous, more accent types, stronger types (dynamic, chord change), moved to an outer voice or more parts | Builds or eases tension without changing the pattern |
| **Augmentation / diminution** | A slower or faster member of the same family: D2+1 (eighths) → D4+2 (quarters) → D8+4 (halves), or the reverse | Diminution speeds up and adds excitement; augmentation broadens |
| **Tightening / loosening** | Same displacement, contradicting the beat more or less often: D8+1 (beat 2 every other bar) → D4+1 (every bar) → D2+1 (beats 2 and 4) | Tightening raises tension; loosening relaxes |
| **Submerging / surfacing** | The metrical layer disappears, leaving only the antimetrical one (which listeners soon hear as the meter), then comes back | A hidden conflict, then a sudden reveal |

Krebs ties them to tension directly: intensification, diminution and tightening raise it; deintensification, augmentation and loosening lower it. Submerging is ambiguous, less obvious contradiction on the surface but a deeper denial of the meter. Schumann's examples show these processes coordinated with a section's form (a dissonance intensified in the middle of a small ternary form and weakened at the return) and with other domains (an augmentation process peaking together with a crescendo, the highest register and trills).

## Getting out: resolution

Metrical dissonances don't imply a particular resolution the way a leading tone does, but context creates the expectation: once a meter is established, a superimposed layer is expected to drop out. Krebs's ways out:

- **Abrupt resolution** by dropping the antimetrical layer, often with a **corrective accent**: a dynamic hit on the first downbeat of the restored meter. That's the crash on 1 every drummer already plays.
- **Deceptive resolution**: the *metrical* layer drops out instead, and the antimetrical one becomes the new meter.
- **Gradual resolution** by deintensification, by loosening (thinning the antimetrical accents first), or, for a compound dissonance, by **shedding layers** one at a time until only the meter remains.

Even an abrupt resolution has a soft edge: the listener keeps the abandoned layer going in their head for a few pulses (an *indirect* dissonance), so there's always a brief transition.

## Meter and form

Krebs's chapter on form lists the ways Schumann ties metrical states to the structure <Source book="krebs" where="Ch. 6" />. Every one of them works for a riff band:

- **Resolve at the cadence.** Dissonance clears at the end of a phrase or section, often with the cadence getting more consonant time at bigger boundaries.
- **A marker dissonance.** One specific dissonance placed at formal joins (the end of the exposition, before the return) to help listeners find their way through an unusual form.
- **One state per section.** A section gets its own metrical state: a dissonant introduction that resolves as the main section starts (the feeling of "getting down to business"), a contrasting middle with a different state, a closing theme set apart by displacement.
- **Links between sections.** The same dissonance, or a related one (an augmentation), returns in a contrasting section and quietly connects them.
- **Coordination with pitch.** Unstable, sequential passages carry metrical dissonance and resolve together with the harmony; pitch dissonance and metrical dissonance rise and fall together; a short harmonic detour gets its own metrical state as a "metrical parenthesis". The registral peaks of a line can each be marked by dissonance.

The revisions in Schumann's manuscripts show the same thinking <Source book="krebs" where="Ch. 5" />. He usually *added* dissonance by inserting a dissonant segment or by adding a new part that cuts against existing material, and *removed* it near endings and at returns, to sharpen the contrast between sections, or to connect passages.

## What it can mean

In the songs, metrical dissonance paints things in the text (a roar, a shudder, a bell, waves), highlights the crucial word or turning point, sets two layers of reality or time against each other (clock time against a lover's impatience), and above all represents agitation, excitement or loss of control. Resolution at the right word means arrival or homecoming; an unresolved dissonance at the end leaves the conflict hanging. Sometimes the poem's own meter suggests it: a triplet foot among duple ones seeds a three-against-two setting. In instrumental music Krebs reads it as conflict, but also as panic and breathlessness at speed, as humour, and as a dreamlike hovering when no layer clearly wins <Source book="krebs" where="Ch. 6" />.

## Playing it as a band

Krebs's chapter on performance is written for pianists, but it translates to a band almost directly <Source book="krebs" where="Ch. 7" />:

<Steps>

- **Know where every conflict starts and ends**, and which layers it's made of. Map it.
- **Practise the layers separately, then together.** Then play an *aligned* version (the displaced part moved back onto the beat) and go back to the real one; the difference tells everyone what the passage is for.
- **Don't overplay the antimetrical layer.** It's usually written strongly enough. Overstress it and it becomes the new downbeat. The part that often needs a little help is the **meter** itself.
- **When the downbeat disappears entirely, keep feeling it.** That tension carries to the audience. A visible cue (a breath, a lean, a look) helps the band and the audience.
- **Don't hesitate at the start of a displaced passage.** Coming in a fraction late turns the displaced layer into a new downbeat.
- **Use repeats.** When a passage comes back, you can tilt it toward a different layer so the listener hears it from a new angle.
- **Feel the tension curve**, and find an image or story for it. A performance with a clear narrative carries more than an accurate one.

</Steps>

<ListenerSide grade="theory">

Krebs's model comes from analysis and from performers' experience, not from experiments. The claims about tension (tighter and faster displacements feel more intense; listeners keep an abandoned layer for a few pulses) are plausible and partly backed by later meter research, but untested on rock listeners.

</ListenerSide>

<Exercise title="Write a metrical plot">

1. Take one riff and decide its narrative: C–D–C, C–D or D–C.
2. Write four versions of it: consonant, a hint (one or two antimetrical accents), a loose displacement (every other bar), and a tight one (every beat it can contradict).
3. Order them into a 32-bar section that prepares, tightens and resolves with a corrective accent. Then try loosening into the resolution instead and compare.
