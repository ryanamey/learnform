## Your voice is a second arrangement

The band changes texture section by section; the voice can do the same without changing a note. Kate Heidemann's article gives singers a way to describe and plan that <Source book="heidemann" where="[1.1]–[2.10]" />.

Her starting point: listeners understand a vocal sound partly by **imagining making it**. Hearing a singer, we half-consciously mirror what the throat and body would have to do (Arnie Cox calls this mimetic engagement), and so we "catch" the feeling of the performance. That's why timbre reads as the inner state of a body, and why it's hard to put into words. Two things help:

- **What the ear measures.** Listening studies find two main acoustic dimensions of timbre: how much energy sits in the upper partials (roughly, brightness) and how the sound starts (the attack). Different listeners lean on different cues, so descriptions disagree.
- **What isn't timbre but travels with it.** Vowels, accent, vibrato and slides aren't timbre in the strict sense, but in a vocal performance they're always mixed in.

## Four questions

Instead of reaching for adjectives ("bright", "raw"), Heidemann suggests asking what the body seems to be doing, in four places <Source book="heidemann" where="[3.1]–[3.27], Table 1" />:

| Question | The range of answers | Examples she uses |
|---|---|---|
| **1. How are the vocal folds vibrating?** | Clear and efficient; **breathy** (air leaking, relaxed), turning into hissing or grainy when pushed; **harsh or hoarse** (rough, irregular, overblown, as in screaming); **creak** or vocal fry; **growl** (vibration above the folds); sudden **breaks** between modes; the **onset**: smooth, breathy, creaky or a hard glottal start | Astrud Gilberto and Michael Hutchence (breathy); Otis Redding (harsh accents); Louis Armstrong and many metal singers (growl) |
| **2. How is the throat and mouth shaped?** | From smallest to largest: **oral twang** (tight, "baby" voice); **twang** (narrowed throat, raised larynx: a resonant sound that cuts through a band, the basis of belting); a **speech-like yell**; **sob** (larynx low, throat wide: dark, crooning, easy on the folds). The mouth adds: a wide "megaphone" for high belted notes, or a nearly closed, terse delivery | Robert Plant and Aretha Franklin (twang); Alex Turner (speech-like); Bing Crosby (sob); Kurt Cobain's verse in "Smells Like Teen Spirit" (terse) |
| **3. Where does it seem to buzz in the body?** | The nose (nasal); the front of the face ("in the mask", the projecting voice); the head (head voice, falsetto, the whistle register); the chest. "Mixed" voice carries a chest-like tone above the break with more twang | Bruce Dickinson (mask); Al Green (breathy falsetto); Gladys Knight (chest) |
| **4. How much breath support and body anchoring?** | A modifier on all of the above: belting and harsh sounds communicate full-body effort; breathy and sob sounds communicate a relaxed body | |

She also gives the singer's view of extreme sounds. Carrying a creak higher in the throat, or using a growl produced above the vocal folds, are ways singers approximate screaming with less wear; voice teachers warn against rough, overblown sounds made at the folds themselves. Her own listening verdict is that nothing quite replaces the weight of a real scream, which sounds like a sacrifice the singer is making. If you scream in the band, learn the safer versions from a voice teacher; the four questions give you a shared vocabulary for that conversation.

<KeyIdea>Describe a vocal sound by what the body seems to be doing: folds, throat, where it buzzes, how much effort. Then you can plan it like any other part.</KeyIdea>

## Timbre as form

Heidemann's two Aretha Franklin analyses are, in effect, a lesson in planning timbre across a song <Source book="heidemann" where="[4.1]–[4.6]" />:

- **"Respect"** opens with a clear, twangy, ringing sound on a high note, with hints of roughness kept just under control. She hears confidence: a voice that fills the room.
- **"(You Make Me Feel Like) A Natural Woman"** opens breathier and slightly hoarse, with less breath support. The ringing power is still there, but **latent**. It comes out in the first chorus, together with a swell in the band and backing vocals. The verse sounds like weariness; the chorus like triumph and gratitude. The timbre carries the persona's change.

The reading elsewhere in the app points the same way. In Tool's "Pushit" the climactic group begins with the song's highest sung note in a lyrical, *bel canto* tone that the song hasn't used before; in Hopesfall's "The End of an Era" the climax brings the only sung melody in a screamed song (see *Where the climax goes* and *Designing the whole*). And Otis Redding's "Try a Little Tenderness" adds rasp step by step across the whole track (see *Where the climax goes*). A timbre held back is a resource, exactly like a held-back cymbal or chord.

Heidemann suggests charting a performance along a few scales, section by section: breathy ↔ clear, regular ↔ rough, constricted ↔ open throat, face ↔ chest, relaxed ↔ anchored.

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. **Map your own default.** Heidemann's advice to analysts applies to singers: describe your habitual setting first, since you hear every other voice against it.
2. **Give each section a timbre**, not just a melody: for example a breathy, close-mouthed verse; a twangier, projecting pre-chorus; an open, anchored chorus.
3. **Save one timbre for the peak.** Choose the sound the song hasn't used yet (a clean high tone after rough singing, or a scream after clean singing) and don't spend it early.
4. **Coordinate with the band.** A breathy verse needs space in the mix; a twangy, resonant voice can sit inside a loud band. Decide the vocal timbre and the guitar sound together.
5. **Rehearse the extremes safely.** Screams, growls and fry are techniques; learn them with a teacher before you put them in every chorus.

</Steps>

<ListenerSide grade="moderate">

The acoustic findings (brightness and attack as main dimensions, listeners differing in which they use) come from listening studies Heidemann cites. The mimetic account of how we understand voices is a theoretical position (Cox) supported by research on embodied cognition, and her analyses are explicitly first-person. Treat the four questions as a description tool, not a measurement.

</ListenerSide>
