## The smallest thing a listener can keep

A motive is the smallest musical unit that has a *character* — something short enough to hold whole in your head and distinctive enough to recognize when it comes back. Schoenberg describes its raw ingredients as intervals and rhythms combined into a recognizable shape <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. III" />. Belkin adds the condition that makes the difference: a motive needs **internal contrast** <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 1" />.

An evenly-played chromatic scale has intervals and rhythm, but nothing in it stands out from anything else, so nothing sticks. Give the same notes a repeating short–short–long rhythm and suddenly there are landmarks: a place where it pauses, a note that is longer, a beat that is louder. Memory works by noticing difference, even inside four notes.

<KeyIdea>A motive is memorable because something inside it contrasts with something else inside it.</KeyIdea>

Count the contrasts in Belkin's sense: rhythm (fast pair vs. long tail), direction (up, then down), interval colour (plain steps vs. the tritone), dynamics (the accented A), and silence (the sixteenth rest). Each of these features is something you can later keep or change, which is the subject of the next three lessons.

## Rhythm as a carrier of identity

Three of the writers point at rhythm from different sides. Schoenberg: preserving rhythmic features is the most effective way to keep variants coherent, and rhythms are remembered more easily than intervals <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. III, V" />. Belkin: changing a motif's rhythm or its accent pattern is among the most drastic changes you can make <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 1" />. Snyder, from memory research: the rhythmic contour of a pattern is often what is recognised most easily, so patterns that keep their rhythm while pitch or timbre changes are heard as one family <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 12" />. Snyder also counts intervals among the main features by which a familiar melody is recognised, which is why a tune survives transposition <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 10–11" />. How much weight each carries is a question the writers answer differently; the seminar below sets them side by side.

## Not only pitch and rhythm

Belkin points out that a motive can also be carried by timbre, articulation, register or dynamics; a single note with an unusual sound and a strong accent can already function as one <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 1" />. On a guitar or in a voice that would mean a pick attack, a harmonic, a slide, a feedback swell, a tuning, a register break.

Snyder draws the line differently. He separates **primary parameters** (pitch, harmony, rhythm), which form patterns that can be recognised again, from **secondary parameters** (loudness, tempo, timbre, register, density), which the ear tracks mostly as more or less. On his account, secondary parameters shape contrasts and build-ups, but for average listeners they have not been made to carry recognisable patterns the way pitch and rhythm do <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 13" />. Note the clash of words: Belkin calls tempo, timbre, register and dynamics the **primary** dimensions, meaning the most powerful for contrast <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 4" />. Same word, opposite list.

## Anything repeated becomes a motive

Schoenberg's working definition is practical rather than abstract: any feature becomes a motive **if it is treated as one**, which means repeated, with or without variation <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. III" />. Even the contour alone can be the motive, while rhythm and intervals change around it. So the question to ask of a riff is not "is this a motive?" but "what am I going to keep bringing back?" Whatever that is, the listener will learn it as the motive.

He also says what to expect from it: everything depends on its treatment, repetition alone gives monotony, and variation keeps the important features while changing the less important ones. The next lesson is about choosing which.

## Motive, basic idea, theme

The writers don't cut the material at the same size. Schoenberg's hierarchy, as his editors fix it, runs motive, phrase, then larger units up to the theme <Source book="schoenberg" where="Preface" />. Caplin works mainly with a larger unit, the **basic idea**: usually two bars, often made of a repeated one-bar motive, small enough to group upward into a phrase and large enough to break down into motives. He warns against calling the motive itself the basic idea, and he treats it as a whole texture (melody, harmony and accompaniment), not a tune <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 2" />. He also separates **characteristic** material, typical of beginnings, from **conventional** material such as scales and cadential formulas. Snyder adds a size limit from the listener's side: a theme meant to be transformed is usually no longer than a phrase, so that it fits short-term memory <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 3" />.

## The groove is a motive too

Schoenberg gives the accompaniment a motive of its own <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. IX" />. A regular accompaniment figure is built like a small theme and treated by **rhythmic repetition** and **harmonic adaptation** (it follows the chords). His rules for it read like advice to a rhythm section:

- **Keep it for several bars.** Its steadiness (he calls it the *motus*) is one of the strongest unifiers a passage has.
- **It must be able to be modified, liquidated or abandoned** as the melody demands. Near a cadence it is usually simplified; a climactic rise usually needs a richer version.
- **Leaving it out is a resource.** Unaccompanied bars give contrast and transparency. His remark that a gap in the accompaniment never sounds bad is worth remembering in a dense band.
- **The bass is a second melody.** It takes part in every change of harmony, keeps to one register and stays continuous, because the ear pays great attention to the bass.
- **Complementary rhythm**: one part fills the gaps of another so that the motion never stops. An **ostinato bass** can carry a whole passage while the texture above it keeps changing.

And the kind of accompaniment sets the character before any melody does: a march can't be built on a chorale accompaniment <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. X" />. For your band this means the drummer's groove and the bass figure are motivic material in their own right, with their own presentation, development and liquidation, not a backdrop.

## Practice

<Exercise title="Four characters, one motive each">

Based on Belkin's first exercise set, adapted for guitar and voice.

1. Invent four 1-bar motives with these characters: *menacing, nostalgic, restless, weightless*.
2. For each, write down its internal contrasts (at least two) the way we did for M.
3. Now keep the notes and change only the non-pitch dimensions — tone, register, articulation, dynamics. Can you turn *menacing* into *weightless* without changing a single pitch?
4. Keep the best two in a Guitar Pro file, named.

</Exercise>
