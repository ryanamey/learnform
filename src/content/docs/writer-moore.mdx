## Allan F. Moore, rock analysis

Moore is a British musicologist, emeritus professor at the University of Surrey, and one of the founders of the analysis of recorded popular music. Two of his texts are used here: the article "Analysing Rock: Means and Ends" (based on papers given in Bologna in 1998), and *Song Means: Analysing and Interpreting Recorded Popular Song* (Ashgate, 2012). An essay collection he edited, *Analyzing Popular Music* (2003), is used separately.

**How it was read for the app:** "Analysing Rock" was read in full (our copy has no journal header, so its exact publication details are not confirmed). *Song Means* is read only through the Google Books preview: ch. 1 in full, ch. 2 and 3 except hidden pages, a passage of ch. 6 and the questions in ch. 11. The full book is being sought.

## His question

**How can we analyse a recorded rock song as a recording, starting from what listeners actually hear, and how does a song make meaning against the norms of its style?**

## His toolkit

- **Style first**: a style is a set of tendencies, not rules; a song creates **friction** against them ("Analysing Rock").
- **Four functional layers**: explicit beat, functional bass, melody, harmonic filler; layers are jobs, not instruments; the filler marks the style ("Analysing Rock"; *Song Means* ch. 2).
- **The sound-box**: the recording as a virtual space of width, depth, height and time; the **diagonal mix** as the norm since the early 1970s; blocks, points, haze, holes and masks (*Song Means* ch. 2; "Analysing Rock").
- **Four ways of making space** (from his U2 analysis): a registral gap, reverb, sustained pads, a fast surface over slow harmony ("Analysing Rock").
- **Voice**: register, resonance, rhythmic placement, pitch approach ("Analysing Rock"); persona and delivery (*Song Means*, not yet read in full).
- **Timbre as deviation from a norm** (*Song Means* ch. 2).

## Where he agrees with the others

- With **Lavengood**: layers and timbre are defined against the norms of a style; Lavengood adds the novelty layer.
- With **Belkin**: background planes should differ from the foreground; Moore's filler is close to Belkin's "enriching" planes.
- With **LaRue**: texture changes are among the easiest articulations to hear (Moore found students could follow form by texture when they couldn't follow themes).

## Where he differs

- **Texture first.** He argues rock analysis should begin from texture and sound, not from pitch, against the tradition that treats these as "secondary parameters". He cites the objection himself (Meyer; Butler) and says his position is not yet backed by psychological or ethnographic research. See *Seminar: Is texture primary or secondary?*
- **Meaning.** He reads layouts and timbres as meaningful (the singer kept back in the mix as one of the band). These readings are interpretations, which Course XIII will take up.

## Where the app uses him

Course IV: *The four layers of rock*, *The sound-box*, *Heaviness*, *Vocal timbre*, *Constant texture and stasis*, the texture seminar. Courses VII and XIII: climax, form, persona (*Song Means*).
