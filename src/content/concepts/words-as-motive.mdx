## Your second instrument decides a lot before you do

Belkin gives the voice a chapter of its own because it differs from instruments in ways composers often ignore <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 3" />. Speech already has rhythm, stress and melody. Say "you love me" as a question, a statement and a sarcastic comeback, and the words stay the same while the pitch contour and timing carry three different meanings. Singing heightens all of that.

That makes the text a **pre-existing motive**. Its stress pattern is a rhythm and its natural intonation is a contour. Setting it well means *amplifying* those shapes, not fighting them.

<KeyIdea>Stressed syllables want length, height or accent; weak syllables want to be short and low. Enhance the shape of the words, never contradict it, unless you want an effect.</KeyIdea>

## Belkin's process, adapted for songwriting

1. **Mark the stresses.** Speak the line naturally and mark every stressed syllable.
2. **Mark the speech contour.** Speak it slowly with normal intonation and note where the pitch rises and falls. Statements usually fall at the end; questions rise.
3. **Write the line so it doesn't contradict either.** You can exaggerate: a long, high syllable can become longer and higher. Don't put a long high note on "the", and don't make a statement rise like a question.
4. **Check the vowels at the climax.** Sustained singing happens on vowels. Open vowels (*ah*, *oh*) carry power at the top; closed ones (*ee*, *oo*) are much harder to belt. If the emotional peak lands on a closed vowel, change the word or move the peak.

## Recitative vs. aria: two vocal modes in one song

Belkin borrows a distinction from opera. **Recitative** delivers information: few pitches, speech-like rhythm, a light accompaniment. **Aria** delivers emotion: the words matter less than the feeling, so lines expand, repeat and use the full range.

Most rock songs move between the two without naming it. Verses tell, choruses feel. Prog vocalists often blur it deliberately: a spoken, nearly monotone delivery over a complex band, then a sudden opening into long sustained lines. **Where you switch modes is a formal decision.** It marks sections at least as clearly as a riff change.

Two more practical points from the chapter:
- Singing takes much longer than speaking the same words, roughly two to three times longer, so plan lyric density.
- The punctuation of the text should be audible in the music, with different strengths. A comma is not the end of a verse.

## Range as form

Voices are most comfortable in the middle of their range. The extremes cost more and *mean* more, so Belkin's advice is to save them for deliberate effect. That makes register a powerful formal tool: **plan the register arc.** Where is the highest note of each song? Of a whole record? If every chorus goes to the top, the top stops meaning anything.

Schoenberg makes the same point from the vocal side, and calls it structural <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. XI" />. The top of a voice is "vulnerable" and tiring, so **reserve it for the climax**. The lowest register is stronger than the middle but can't carry too much drama; the middle is the most convenient and has the least extreme expression. The registers differ in colour, and a voice needs time to develop its full sound, so fast staccato or sudden accents are hard to sing. His test of a singable line: relatively long notes, smooth links between registers, waves, more steps than leaps, and enough harmonic support for the singer to find the pitch, especially at the start.

## Where the words sit: verbal space

Two readings name something singers handle by instinct. The musical phrase gives every lyric line a fixed span of beats, and the words can fill it, leave it empty, or crowd one end of it. Dai Griffiths calls this **verbal space** <Source book="moore03" where="Ch. 3 (Griffiths)" />; Moore pictures the beats as fence posts and asks how the words cluster around them <Source book="moore12" where="Ch. 3, p. 64" />. Devices both describe:

- **Change the density.** The middle eight of the Beatles' "Please Please Me" packs syllables in fours where the verse had one or two. Hank Williams's "Mind Your Own Business" fills the verse with detail and leaves the chorus bare: just the title.
- **Fill gradually across the song.** The chorus hook of the Archies' "Sugar, Sugar" grows from two syllables to five, six, seven, each time with a longer pickup. Griffiths hears Gladys Knight and the Pips start with lines that are nearly empty and pack them by the end.
- **Make the space the meaning.** In Madness's "Our House" the bridge squeezes its words closer and closer until "nothing would come between us" leaves no space between words at all; then the space opens again.
- **Move the line against the downbeat.** Griffiths calls the first beat of a line its pillar. Words can start on it, before it, or after it, and a line that finally lands its key word on the pillar after avoiding it is a small arrival.

Moore adds the rhythmic side. Vocal **anticipation** (arriving just before the beat) is so normal in rock that Black Sabbath's "Paranoid" anticipates almost every note of its verse; it reads as forward motion. **Delay** (arriving late) is rarer and sounds wistful or held back. Placing words early in one section and late in another is a contrast the band never has to play.

<ListenerSide grade="moderate">

Sung words are harder to follow than spoken ones, because singers sustain vowels and consonants carry most of the meaning. Belkin's rule that the setting may exaggerate the spoken rhythm and contour but never contradict it is how you keep lyrics intelligible at all. The register point connects to Module 5: the highest notes are both physically costly and rare, which is why they work as peaks. Spend them everywhere and they stop marking anything.

</ListenerSide>

2. Mark stresses and speech contour on paper.
