## The balance problem

The moment you use a motive, you have the central problem of all musical form: **repetition versus novelty**. Repeat it unchanged ten times and attention dies. Change everything about it and the listener can no longer follow — the result sounds like a stream of unrelated ideas <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 1, Motivic variants" />.

Schoenberg puts it as a rule: variation means changing *some* features while keeping others <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. III" />. The skill is in choosing which.

## A scale from 1 to 5

Belkin proposes rating each variant by how strongly it still sounds like its source:

| Rating | What it means | Where it belongs |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | Instantly the same idea (e.g. transposition) | Inside the same section; clear reminders later |
| **2** | Close; obviously related | Developing a line while staying on one idea |
| **3** | Medium: you hear the link, and the change | Transitions — turning one idea into another |
| **4** | Distant: the difference is louder than the link | New sections with a hidden family resemblance |
| **5** | New to the ear | Real contrast; save for special moments |

Belkin draws two consequences. Close variants create continuity and distant ones create contrast, so the scale works as a dial between flow and change. And vivid contrasts spent early become the norm, so later ones have to be bigger: he advises saving them <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 1" />.

<KeyIdea>Belkin: every variant is a choice of distance from its source, and the distance decides whether it sounds like continuation or contrast.</KeyIdea>

### What Belkin's ratings imply

- **Rhythm and accent.** In his table, inversion with the rhythm kept is a 2, while a swapped accent pattern is a 5. The even-rhythm variant in the lab keeps every pitch and is rated 5 on that basis.
- **Leap sizes.** Enlarging a leap is a 1 in his table; he notes the ear treats leaps mostly as "not a step".
- **Time changes.** Augmentation and diminution are 4s, though they keep everything except speed.
- **Displacement.** Moving a motif against the beat changes its accent pattern, so on his criteria it rates high. Course III follows what rock and metal writers say about displacement (metric dissonance), which treats it as a central resource.

## How much variety a passage can take

Schoenberg adds three limits that decide how far you can go at a given moment <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. V, VIII, XV" />:

- **Speed limits variety.** Rapid music is harder to grasp, so fast pieces show *less* variety than slow ones. A riff at 180 bpm can stand far less transformation per bar than a ballad line. His summary: only as much variation as the character, length and tempo require.
- **State it twice, then travel.** Once an idea has been stated and answered (a twofold statement), the idea and the tonality are established. After that, remote variants can appear straight away without losing the listener, and their own repetitions justify irregular lengths and unusual harmony.
- **Keep the rhythm and you can change almost anything else.** Keeping the rhythmic features is, in his words, "so strong a motive relation" that intervals and contour can change a great deal without incoherence. That is the lab's first lesson again, from the composer's side.

In the other direction, he warns that too many rhythmic changes or accent shifts in a return prevent recognition <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. XIII" />. The more the return matters, the more of its rhythm you keep.

## Context changes the rating

A variant's rating is not fixed. Belkin's own example: a change that would normally count as distant sounds close if it arrives immediately, on the same pitches, as part of an obvious process like a speed-up. The same transformation heard four minutes later, after other material, might not be recognized at all.

Snyder gives a memory explanation <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 3–4" />. Inside the short-term memory window of a few seconds, the source is still "present", so the comparison is easy. Across sections, the source has to be retrieved from long-term memory, which keeps a sketch rather than the details. In his discussion of parallelism he states it as a rule: two patterns close together can differ a lot and still be heard as related, but patterns far apart must be very similar for the link to be heard, which he gives as a reason recapitulations are so often exact.

<ListenerSide grade="weak">

The 1–5 ratings are Belkin's craft judgements, not measured results. Margulis's work on repetition (Course XII) supports the general shape: exact and near repetition is what lets listeners orient and participate, and change is heard against what they already hold. Where a variant actually lands on the scale for *your* listeners is something to test with the A/B lab and a listening session, not to assume.

</ListenerSide>

## Practice

<Exercise title="Ten versions of one riff">

From the roadmap's month-2 project, made measurable.

1. Take one of your motifs (or M).
2. Write **two variants for each rating, 1 to 5**: ten in total. Use at least five different techniques: transposition, inversion, rhythm kept with new pitches, fragment, augmentation or diminution, displacement, register, a new tone or tuning.
3. Save them next to the source in Guitar Pro, each named with its rating.
4. Play them to someone else without telling them the ratings. Where they disagree with you is the useful part.

</Exercise>
