## The question

A section ends with a strong cadence, and the final chord lands on the downbeat where the next section begins. Is that the strongest possible ending, or **no ending at all**? The question decides how you join sections and how you end songs.

## Yust: it doesn't close

Yust's hypermetrical rule of closure says the final tonic must be sustained to the end of every hypermetrical unit it begins in <Source book="yust" where="Ch. 7.1" />. If it lands on a downbeat that a new phrase then occupies, the cadence is **elided**: complete in harmony, but without closure. A theory of accents would call that downbeat stronger; for Yust the stronger position is exactly what prevents the ending. Composers used elision to keep music moving: between a main theme and its transition, and in Beethoven's "open expositions" that put off full closure.

## Lerdahl & Jackendoff: a charged arrival

GTTM separates structural accent (the cadence) from metrical accent <Source book="gttm" where="Ch. 2" />. A cadence can land on a weak beat (feminine) or a strong one (masculine). When a long structural anacrusis drives to a cadence on a strong beat that also begins the next section, grouping, meter and harmony meet in a **structural downbeat**, "dynamically charged", in contrast to the formal rhyme of cadences on weak beats before the next phrase.

## Stephenson: rock lands on the downbeat

In rock, cadences mostly fall on strong downbeats, and units often end on V and begin on I, so that resolution happens at the join <Source book="stephenson" where="Ch. 1" />. The music leans forward from unit to unit; a fade-out, he says, is the honest way such a style ends.

## Hasty and Caplin

Hasty: a half cadence ends one phrase and opens a larger event; the end of an event is always its aim, not a point <Source book="hasty" where="Ch. 13" />. Caplin treats elision as a deviation of function: the end of one phrase is also the beginning of the next <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 3, 5" />.

## Where they agree

All agree that a cadence landing on the downbeat of a new section is a powerful moment, and that it joins sections rather than separating them. All agree that metrical placement changes what a cadence does.

## Where they differ

- **Closure**: Yust says no closure without the tonic filling its unit; GTTM describes the overlapped cadence as an arrival that is also a launch; Stephenson treats strong-beat cadences as rock's norm.
- **Strength**: for an accent theory the downbeat cadence is the strongest; for Yust it is the least closed.
