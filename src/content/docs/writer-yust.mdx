## Jason Yust, *Organized Time*

Yust is a music theorist at Boston University. *Organized Time: Rhythm, Tonality, and Form* was published by Oxford in 2018. It treats rhythm, tonality and form as three independent "temporal structures" with the same formal shape: hierarchies of timespans. A great deal of music's interest, for him, lies where the three disagree.

**How it was read for the app:** the Introduction, Ch. 1, Ch. 5 (formulas skimmed), Ch. 6, Ch. 7.1 and 8.1 were read. The rest of Ch. 7 and the opening of Ch. 8.2 were skimmed. Ch. 2–4 (tonal and formal structure), 8.3–8.4 and 9–14 were not read for Course III; Ch. 3 and 11 (form) will be read for the form courses.

## His question

**How do rhythm, tonality and form each organise time, and what happens musically when they don't line up?**

## His toolkit

- **Timespan networks**: meter as containment of spans, with unsounded beats in the listener's mind (Ch. 1).
- **Rhythmic structure vs metrical structure**; **metricality** (how many levels a passage actually articulates), speech mode vs dance mode (Ch. 1).
- **Rhythmic transformations**: meter change, swing vs squeeze, syncopation (Ch. 1, 5).
- **Projection and piggyback projection** (after Hasty), with **metrical inertia** (Ch. 1).
- **Timespan intervals**: lengthened and shortened projections, extension vs expansion (Ch. 5).
- **Hypermetrical shift**: metrical deletion, the hypermetrical pause, the extended beginning; **indefinite hypermeter** (Ch. 6).
- **The hypermetrical rule of closure**: a final tonic must fill the hypermeasure; an **elided cadence** prevents closure (Ch. 7).
- **Three kinds of syncopation**: contrapuntal, tonal, structural (Ch. 8).

## Where he agrees with the others

- He adopts **Hasty's** projection, **Lerdahl & Jackendoff's** metrical deletion, and **Cohn's** hemiola vocabulary.
- He agrees with **London and Brower** that entrainment fades above about two bars.

## Where he differs

- **Hypermeter.** Regular rhythmic structure at 4 bars, sometimes 8; more than L&J, Hasty and London allow, much less than the Schenkerians. See *Seminar: Is hypermeter real above two bars?*
- **Odd groupings.** Patterns like 3+3+2 are "rhythmic topoi" (Agawu's term), not meters. This puts him against London and L&J.
- **Krebs's layers.** These are rhythmic structures; there is only one meter.
- **Cadences.** Closure needs the tonic to fill the hypermeasure, so a cadence on the next strong downbeat is an elision, not a strong ending. Compare L&J's "structural downbeat". See *Seminar: Does a cadence on the downbeat close or open?*
- **Against Hasty**: meter is real apart from rhythm, and metrical inertia exists.
