## Every passage has a job in time

Caplin builds his whole theory of form on one observation: a passage of music doesn't only have content (a riff, a chord loop, a groove). It also expresses **where it is in time** <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 2, Focus on Function" />. Some music sounds like a beginning. Some sounds like the middle of something. Some sounds like an ending. And two more positions frame these three: music that sounds like it comes **before the beginning**, and music that comes **after the end**.

| Temporal function | What it sounds like | Caplin's names for it |
|---|---|---|
| Before the beginning | Getting ready; something is about to start | Thematic introduction, slow introduction |
| Beginning | Stable, presents its material, opens up | Basic idea, presentation, antecedent, main theme |
| Middle | Unstable, moving, "in the thick of it" | Continuation, transition, development core |
| End | Closing in, liquidating, arriving | Cadential idea, consequent, cadence |
| After the end | Settling, prolonging an arrival that has happened | Codetta, closing section, coda |

His analogy is a class: the lecture begins and ends, but arriving, finding a seat and chatting come before the beginning, and packing up comes after the end. Both belong to the experience of the class.

<KeyIdea>Before you ask what a section is made of, ask what job in time it does. Two sections built from the same riff can do opposite jobs, and two sections with nothing in common can do the same job.</KeyIdea>

## What makes music sound like a beginning, a middle or an end

The functions are recognisable because they have conventional characteristics <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 2–5" />:

- **Beginnings** present an idea and repeat it over one stable harmony (the riff on its home chord, twice). Repetition strengthens a beginning; it doesn't end anything. That's why a presentation never ends with a cadence.
- **Middles** show at least one of four signs: **fragmentation** (units get shorter), **faster harmonic change**, **busier surface rhythm**, and **sequence** (a pattern moved step by step). Caplin stresses that these are independent and can stand in for each other. If the units don't get shorter, the chords can change faster; if the chords don't speed up, the drums can.
- **Endings** bring a conventional, usually falling gesture over a closing progression, after the characteristic features have been **liquidated** (see *The sentence and liquidation*).
- **Before the beginning** has minimal melodic content, so it can't be mistaken for the basic idea: often just the accompaniment, a vamp. It tends to build energy (a "progressive" dynamic, in Wallace Berry's term).
- **After the end** circles the home note and avoids opening new melodic space, which would sound like a new beginning. Its units usually get **shorter and quieter** (a "recessive" dynamic), though some codas keep or even raise the energy.

## Three distinctions that make the idea useful

**Type is not function.** "Sentence" and "period" are types: patterns with no fixed place in a piece. "Main theme" or "transition" are functions. A sentence can serve as a main theme, a period as a refrain. In Tool terms: "the 7/8 riff in a three-times-plus-fill pattern" is a type; "the riff that opens the song" is a function <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 3, Focus on Function" />.

**Grouping is not function.** Caplin's analogy is boxes of cereal. Fragmentation is about the size of the boxes, not what's in them: fragments can come from the basic idea or be completely new. When you judge whether units are getting shorter, measure against the **largest unit that was repeated**, not against some smaller unit inside it <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 2, Ch. 6" />.

**Content is not function.** The same material can change jobs. Caplin shows a main theme's fanfare returning in a coda over a stubborn tonic with no cadence, where it now works as a closing section. In a so-called reversed recapitulation, main-theme material that comes back late is reshaped to work as a subordinate theme <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 14–15" />. For a band that reuses riffs across a long song, this is the key point: **a returning riff is only a return if it does the returning job.**

## The order of functions, and formal dissonance

Functions have a natural syntax: beginning, then middle, then end. A theme that put a consequent before a presentation, or a continuation before an antecedent, would be illogical <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 4, Focus on Function" />.

But music can break the order on purpose. Because the functions are so conventional, a passage can sound **intrinsically** like a middle even when it is placed at the start. Caplin calls this a mismatch between intrinsic and contextual function, a kind of **formal dissonance** <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 12, Focus on Function" />. Carefully controlled, it gives a looser, more urgent form. Too much, and the order of events stops making sense.

The common cases, all from the classical repertoire, all directly usable:

- **Starting in the middle.** A theme opens with a sequence or with short fragments, as if it had already been going for a while. Caplin even writes a beginning that could have preceded one such Beethoven theme, to show what's missing. A sequence is the clearest way to do it; fragmentation alone only sounds like a middle once you've heard a longer unit.
- **Starting with an ending.** A whole theme sits on one stretched closing progression.
- **Starting on the dominant.** A passage holds the tense chord from the previous section, so it feels like it hasn't begun yet.
- **Beginning before the previous section has ended.** A section can stop without any ending and the next can start anyway. Caplin's analogy: a speaker who starts a new sentence before finishing the last. The syntax is disrupted, not destroyed.

This is the theory behind the moments in prog that feel like falling into a song already in motion. Compare *Gestural time*, where Kramer describes the same effect from the listener's side.

## Hearing it differently afterwards

Caplin borrows a symbol from Janet Schmalfeldt: **⇒**, read "becomes". Some functions are only understood in retrospect <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 2–3, 11" />:

- **Continuation ⇒ cadential.** What seemed like a middle turns out to be one long approach to the ending.
- **False closing section.** What sounds like settling after a cadence turns out to be the start of the next section, once it begins to move.
- **Failed consequent.** A phrase starts like the answer that should close a section, then ends open, so the section goes on.
- **Reinterpreted half cadence.** An ending in a new key is heard, a moment later, as a pause on the home key's dominant.

Both hearings are valid, each at its own moment. For a writer, these are ways of **making the listener revise**: write a passage that clearly does one job, then let what follows change the meaning.

## Analysing a song that fits no type

Caplin's advice for themes that don't fit any standard type applies to most of the music you love <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 10, Analyzing Nonconventional Themes" />. Don't work from the top down ("is this a sentence?"). Work from the bottom up:

1. Map the harmony (or the bass and the riff's home note).
2. Group the surface into small units.
3. Group those into larger units.
4. Only then assign functions, even if only "beginning", "middle", "end".

His point: what makes a theme nonconventional is that its functions are combined in unusual ways, **not that it has no functions**. A form-functional reading may still not emerge; then describe the harmonic plan and the grouping, and how the units present themselves in time.

<ListenerSide grade="moderate">

Caplin's functions are a music theorist's categories, not tested perceptual findings. But they rest on things listeners demonstrably track: grouping boundaries, repetition, harmonic stability and closure (see Module 1's memory pages and Snyder on closure). The claim that a passage can sound like a middle out of context is plausible because those cues are conventional, which also means they are style-dependent. Rock listeners learn rock's versions of them (riff repetition, the fill before a change, the crash on the downbeat), so test your function cues on listeners who know the style.

</ListenerSide>

## Practice

<Exercise title="Five jobs, one riff">

1. Take one riff from your motif bank.
2. Write it as a beginning: state it, repeat it, keep one harmony, no landing.
3. Write it as a middle: use only part of it, move it in sequence, speed up the changes.
4. Write it as an ending: liquidate it into a plain falling line and land.
5. Write it as an after-the-end: fragments over the home note, each shorter.
6. Play the four in order, then start with the middle. What does the formal dissonance do to the energy?

</Exercise>
