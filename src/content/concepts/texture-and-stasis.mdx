## Constant texture

Many pieces keep one texture from start to finish: a Bach prelude in continuous broken chords, a Chopin étude, a Schubert song with the same accompaniment figure throughout. Jonathan Kramer explains what such a texture does to our sense of time <Source book="kramer" where="Ch. 2" />.

He distinguishes two kinds of musical logic:

- **Linearity**: what happens is implied by what came before. Melodies, cadences and harmonic progressions work this way.
- **Nonlinearity**: what happens follows from a principle that governs the whole piece or section, present from the start and gradually learned. Instrumentation, a constant texture and proportions work this way.

A constant figuration is nonlinear: once the listener is sure it will continue, it carries no further information. But that makes **a break in it** enormous. His example is Schubert's "Gretchen am Spinnrade", where the spinning-wheel accompaniment runs through the song and then stops. The effect is different from a linear surprise (a deceptive cadence, say), because what is broken is not an expectation built in the last few bars but a rule the whole song has taught.

Snyder gives the perceptual side: an unchanging pattern **habituates**, moving out of focus into the background <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 2, 13" />. A pure, unaccented pulse is the limiting case: it organises time but articulates nothing <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 12" />.

## Activity without direction

LaRue makes a related distinction among three states of motion <Source book="larue" where="Ch. 1; Ch. 6" />: **stability**, **local activity** ("motion in equilibrium") and **directional motion**. A busy texture can be locally active and still go nowhere. His small example: a figure that keeps circling back to its starting note (C B C D C B C...) has only local activity, while the same figure rising step by step (C D E, D E F...) has direction.

## Vertical time

Kramer pushes nonlinearity to its extreme in what he calls **vertical time** <Source book="kramer" where="Ch. 2, 12" />. A vertical piece has no phrases (or phrases of equal weight), no climax and no ending in the usual sense: it *starts* and *ceases* rather than beginning and ending. It defines its sound-world early and must not step outside it, because any move outside becomes a major articulation and breaks the effect. Structure lies between the simultaneous layers rather than from one moment to the next. He compares it to sculpture: the listener chooses where to look and for how long.

He describes what happens to a listener. An unsympathetic listener experiences slowed or stopped time, and boredom. A sympathetic one can enter an expanded present and become very sensitive to small details. Kramer's own account is of a relay performance of Satie's *Vexations*: after the information ran out he switched into this mode, and three hours felt like forty minutes. He insists that vertical music demands active listening (to the inside of the sound, its edges, its overtones), or it is simply boring.

Two points matter for a band:

- By the 1980s, Kramer notes, vertical time had become **one tool among others**, for example a linear foreground over a static background.
- **Remembered duration** behaves the opposite way to experienced duration: periods with little information are remembered as short, rich ones as long <Source book="kramer" where="Ch. 12" />. Snyder reports the same finding <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 13" />.

## Moore's fast-over-slow

Moore's analysis of U2 (in the lesson *The sound-box*) names a related device: a **fast surface over slow harmony**, like delay-guitar arpeggios over a held organ chord <Source book="analysing-rock" where="U2 analysis" />. He compares it to a landscape seen from a moving car, where near things rush past and far things seem still. The texture is active and static at once, and the effect he describes is space rather than motion.

## Does music have to go somewhere?

Here the writers disagree.

**Belkin** treats direction as a norm of Western music and a basic requirement of craft <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 13 and note; Ch. 15; Ch. 20, Accenting" />. Music must not stay on static terrain; a static organ note held for a minute tells the listener within seconds that nothing will happen. He quotes Roger Sessions: the experience of a piece should intensify overall ("progression, or cumulation"). In a note he qualifies his contrast with "some eastern cultures": static music elsewhere is usually functional or ritual, not concert music.

**Kramer** treats nonlinear and vertical time as fully musical ways of organising time, not as failures of direction <Source book="kramer" where="Ch. 2, 3, 8, 12" />. He links them to cumulative listening (memory putting a piece together out of order) and to recordings, which let listeners choose their own spans, and he gives the listener an active role in making form.

**Snyder** describes both mechanisms (directional progressions in secondary parameters, and habituation) without preferring either <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 13" />.

This matters for a band that works with handpan and meditative music alongside heavy prog: whether a long static passage is a failure of craft or a different kind of time is not settled among these writers.

<KeyIdea>A constant texture is a rule the piece teaches; breaking it is a large event (Kramer). Activity is not direction (LaRue). Whether music must always go somewhere is a real disagreement between Belkin and Kramer.</KeyIdea>

## Where the writers stand

<Positions title="Constant texture and stasis">
  <Position book="kramer" where="Ch. 2, 8, 12">Constant texture is nonlinear; breaking it has a huge effect; vertical time is a valid mode that asks for active listening.</Position>
  <Position book="belkin" where="Ch. 13, 15, 20">Music should progress and intensify; static music is mostly functional or ritual.</Position>
  <Position book="larue" where="Ch. 1, 6">Stability, local activity and directional motion are three different states; busy is not the same as moving.</Position>

  <Position book="analysing-rock" where="U2 analysis">A fast surface over slow harmony creates a sense of space.</Position>

## Practice

<Exercise title="Teach a rule, then break it">

1. Choose one texture (an ostinato, a drum pattern, a guitar figure) and keep it constant through a whole section of 30 seconds or more.
2. Break it once: stop it, change its register or cut it to silence.
3. Compare the effect of the break with a linear surprise in the same place, such as an unexpected chord.

</Exercise>

<GuitarPro title="Static and directional">

1. Write a 4-bar figure that circles around one note (local activity).
2. Rewrite it so that the same rhythm climbs or descends (directional).
3. Write a version with a fast figure over one held chord, as in Moore's U2 analysis. Which of the three would suit an introduction, a build and an ending?

</GuitarPro>

<ApplyIt>

Decide whether your next long piece has a static section. If it does, define its sound-world in advance and list what may not happen inside it. Then decide what breaks it, and where.

</ApplyIt>
