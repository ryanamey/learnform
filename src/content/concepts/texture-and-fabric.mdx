## A vocabulary for sound itself

Jan LaRue's *Guidelines for Style Analysis* (1970) divides music into five elements, which he abbreviates SHMRG: **Sound, Harmony, Melody, Rhythm** and **Growth** (his word for form, made of Movement and Shape) <Source book="larue" where="Ch. 1" />. Sound is "sound considered in itself", not as raw material for melodies or chords <Source book="larue" where="Ch. 2" />. He divides it into three:

1. **Timbre**: which colours, in which registers, how idiomatically used.
2. **Dynamics**: all intensity, whether marked or implied by the forces playing.
3. **Texture and fabric**: how the sounds are arranged, at one moment and over time.

His book is a checklist for style analysis, so its value for a band is the vocabulary: words precise enough to describe what a section *sounds like* and how it differs from the next.

## Texture and fabric

LaRue proposes splitting one familiar word in two <Source book="larue" where="Ch. 2, Texture and Fabric" />:

- **Texture** is the combination of sounds at a particular moment.
- **Fabric** is the continuous web of textures and dynamic levels across a passage, as cloth is woven from threads.

For a single moment he lists pairs of descriptors: **thick or thin, simple or doubled, continuous or gapped, alternating or overlapping, balanced or top-heavy or bottom-heavy, pure or mixed**. For the fabric he names the conventions that have lasted: homophonic or chordal, polyphonic or contrapuntal, melody plus accompaniment, and "sectionally specialised" orchestral fabrics in which each section has a job (brass sustain, winds double or answer, strings carry melody, accompaniment and bass). That last one is close to Moore's four layers in *The four layers of rock*.

## Linear and massive

For whole movements, LaRue borrows a distinction from the art historian Heinrich Wölfflin, who contrasted *linear* and *painterly* styles in painting <Source book="larue" where="Ch. 2, Sound in Large Dimensions" />. In music it becomes **linear** fabric (built from lines) and **massive** fabric (built from blocks of sound). His example is a passage of Beethoven's *Pathétique* sonata that alternates the two.

Since few pieces are purely one or the other, he uses three grades: *mainly linear*, *mixed line and mass*, *predominantly massive*.

## Contrast: how much, how often

The two measurements LaRue applies to every aspect of Sound are **degree of contrast** (how big the changes are) and **frequency of contrast** (how often they happen) <Source book="larue" where="Ch. 2" />. A composer can change colour sharply and rarely, or slightly and constantly, and either can be a personal style. Some keep a whole movement in one colour and contrast only between movements; others change within movements, which weakens the contrast between them.

For middle-sized spans, which he thinks are the most important for Sound, he gives a list of opposites to classify a fabric:

| | |
|---|---|
| thick / thin | narrow / broad (range) |
| high / low | loud / soft |
| average / climactic | simple / complex |
| active / stable | abrupt / gradual (changes) |

He always adds a third, in-between grade ("middling", "mixed", "partly"), which he calls the **Rule of Three**: three grades are precise enough and rarely hard to decide.

## Timbre, idiom and reverse idiom

LaRue's timbre checklist <Source book="larue" where="Ch. 2, Timbre" />:

- **Choice**: instruments, voices, electronic and recorded sounds.
- **Range**: the whole span used, preferred registers, forcing extremes, "split" ranges with gaps.
- **Degree and frequency of contrast**, and whether changes are sudden or gradual.
- **Idiom**: writing that suits the instrument, and extensions of technique (his examples: Liszt's piano writing, Paganini's violin).
- **Reverse idiom**: deliberately awkward, harsh or unidiomatic writing for effect. His examples are Stravinsky's staccato choral chords in the *Symphony of Psalms* and the strained high bassoon that opens *The Rite of Spring*.

Reverse idiom is a useful idea for a heavy band. A guitar part that fights the hand, a vocal pushed to the edge of its range, a bass in a register where it barks: when chosen, these are colours, not mistakes.

## The test of an observation

LaRue's advice on method applies to arranging as much as analysis: the right question about a detail is not "is it true?" but **"is it significant?"** A whole chapter on one bar, he jokes, tells you more about the analyst than the music. When the band reviews a mix or an arrangement, the same filter keeps the discussion on what the listener will notice.

<KeyIdea>LaRue's vocabulary: texture (one moment) and fabric (the web over time); linear and massive; degree and frequency of contrast; a three-grade scale for every pair of opposites.</KeyIdea>

## Is Sound primary or secondary?

LaRue takes a position that other writers dispute. For **Movement** (the sense of motion), he thinks Sound is usually secondary: it underlines what melody, harmony and rhythm are doing rather than directing it. For **Shape** (where the joints of the form fall), he thinks Sound is the clearest element of all, because changes of timbre, dynamics and texture are the most easily heard articulations <Source book="larue" where="Ch. 2, Contributions of Sound" />. The next lessons follow both claims, and *Seminar: Is texture primary or secondary?* sets his view beside Meyer's, Snyder's, Belkin's and Moore's.

<ListenerSide grade="moderate">

LaRue's categories are analytical tools, not perceptual findings. His claim that changes of Sound are the easiest articulations to hear agrees with research Snyder reports: large changes of loudness, timbre and number of instruments stand out in first hearings, especially for less experienced listeners.

</ListenerSide>

## Practice

<Exercise title="A fabric chart">

1. Pick a song of 5 minutes or more. Make one column per section.
2. For each section, rate thick/thin, high/low, loud/soft, active/stable and linear/massive on three grades.
3. Mark where the biggest changes happen. Is the song's contrast high or low in degree? In frequency?

</Exercise>

<GuitarPro title="Line and mass">

1. Write a 4-bar chord progression as block chords (massive).
2. Rewrite it as two or three independent lines (linear).
3. Write a version that is half and half: lines in the first two bars, mass in the last two. Where does the change feel strongest?

</GuitarPro>

<ApplyIt>

Fill in a fabric chart for one of your own songs before rehearsing it. If every section is rated the same on most pairs, choose one section to move to the opposite grade on at least two of them.

</ApplyIt>
