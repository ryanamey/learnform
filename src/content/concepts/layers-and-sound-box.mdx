## The mix as a space

The lesson *The four layers of rock* described what each part does. Moore's second tool describes **where each sound appears to be** <Source book="moore12" where="Ch. 2, pp. 29–42" />. The **sound-box** is a model of a recording as a virtual room with four dimensions:

| Dimension | What places a sound there |
|---|---|
| **Width** (laterality) | Left–right position in the stereo field |
| **Depth** (prominence) | Loudness, and above all how much reverb it carries. A little distortion also brings a sound forward; so do breath and lip noise on a voice |
| **Height** (register) | Pitch: low sounds sit at the bottom, high at the top. Bright sounds seem higher than dull ones at the same pitch |
| **Time** | How all of the above changes through the track |

Each one is a dimension you can **plan for form**, just like harmony or meter.

### The norm to deviate from

Since the early 1970s almost every genre has used the same layout, which Moore calls the **diagonal mix**: lead voice, snare and bass in the centre, on a slight diagonal from front to back, with everything else spread around them. Early stereo records tried two alternatives that now sound odd: the **cluster** (everything bunched in one spot) and the **triangular** mix (voice, bass and drums in three different places).

The depth norm is just as stable. The layers central to the song (voice, bass, sometimes guitars) are furthest forward; drums sit slightly back (their attacks cut through anyway); filler and pads sit deepest. Breaking this says something. When the Rolling Stones keep Jagger back in the mix, the singer becomes one member of a band rather than a star with backing.

Most mixes aim for balance. Putting everything on one side disorients, which can be the point: Moore hears the off-centre voice in Hendrix's "If 6 Was 9" as a demand to listen to the words.

<KeyIdea>The diagonal mix is the sound-box's "4/4": a norm so strong listeners don't notice it. That makes every departure audible, so save departures for moments that need them.</KeyIdea>

### How the space is filled

Moore describes the inside of the box with a few terms worth adopting for band talk:

- **Blocks**: large areas of sound with a position (a wall of rhythm guitar on the left).
- **Points**: small, tightly defined sounds (a cowbell, a single bell note).
- **Haze**: one sound spread across the whole space (ride cymbal wash, reverb tails).
- **Edges**: blocks can have **tight** edges (clearly separate) or **loose** ones (bleeding into each other).
- **Holes**: gaps in the space. When one opens, attention goes to what's left.
- **Masks**: sounds hidden by others in the same region. A mask removed is an entry without anything new being played.

### Three aims, with examples

Moore finds producers using the space for quite different ends:

- **Fill it.** U2's "Where the Streets Have No Name" leaves no holes; the guitar's fast, narrow figure encases the space from both sides, and the voice has a lane between guitar and bass, which reads as openness. The Verve's "A Northern Soul" is just as full, but the guitar smothers everything: the same density turns claustrophobic.
- **Make it harsh.** In the first verse of King Crimson's "21st Century Schizoid Man", a clean guitar chord sits on one side while bursts of noise from the other colour it. The space itself makes the distorted vocal more unpleasant. Garbage's "Push It" gets disorientation from many small, harsh, separate sounds spread wide and constantly changing.
- **Blend or separate.** Steely Dan's "Rikki Don't Lose That Number" fits its parts together like a jigsaw: no harsh attacks, similar levels, a polished construction. The Pixies' "Wave of Mutilation" places guitar and bass as if they came from different worlds, which sounds raw and "real", though it's no less constructed.

**Height is the dimension writers forget.** Moore points to a passage in Yes's "I Get Up, I Get Down" where the bass, drums and everything low drop away, leaving high voices and soft chords: the missing bottom is what makes it sound spiritual. Trivium's "Pull Harder on the Strings of Your Martyr" does the opposite, heavy in the low end and short of highs, which matches its dark words. Removing a whole register band is a contrast as large as a key change, and it costs nothing to play.

### Space and form

The sound-box has a time axis, so it can carry form:

- **Entry order builds the space.** In The Jam's "In the City" the guitar starts alone on one side, the bass joins in the centre, the drums enter with a second guitar on the other side, and the voice arrives in the middle. The intro *is* the filling of the box. Peter Gabriel's "The Rhythm of the Heat" does the same with percussion: one hand drum in the centre, then two West African drums wide left and right, then a deeper drum behind, and only then the kit, placed further forward. That's a layout designed in the studio, not a picture of a band on stage, and a direct model for introducing folk percussion.
- **Positions can move, but rarely do.** Moore notes that records move sounds far less than technology allows, which is exactly why a planned move (a voice crossing the space, a guitar leaving the centre) is heard as an event.
- **Holes and unmasking work like entries.** Clearing the centre for one bar, or letting a buried part come forward, marks a boundary without changing the riff.
- **Mixes assume a place to listen.** Dance music is mixed dry for clubs; rock usually gets reverb that suggests a venue (Zagorski-Thomas, in Moore). On headphones the listener is inside the box, and positions are much easier to hear than on speakers.

### Timbre: deviations from a norm

Moore's advice on timbre matches Lavengood's: the useful question is how a sound **deviates from the norm of its style**, along a few continua <Source book="moore12" where="Ch. 2, pp. 44–46" />: harsher or smoother, thinner or thicker, more controlled or more indulgent. Two cautions. Listeners remember a *type* of sound ("a clean nylon guitar"), not the exact sound, so a timbral motive has to be distinctive in type, not detail. And some timbres signify only at the level of **style**: a harsh guitar on an early Stones record says "Rolling Stones", not anything about that song. Of the effects, **reverb continues a sound and echo repeats it**; both make the space bigger.

## Four ways to make space

In his earlier article Moore analyses how U2 make a track sound spacious, and finds four separate techniques <Source book="analysing-rock" where="U2 analysis" />:

1. **A registral gap**: the bass stays low, the guitar stays high, and the middle is left open.
2. **Reverb**, which turns the gaps into "atmospheric" holes that seem full of potential.
3. **Long sustained chords or pads**, which give an empty spaciousness.
4. **A fast surface over slow harmony**: delay-guitar arpeggios moving quickly over a held organ chord. Moore compares it to looking at a landscape, where near things seem to move fast and far things slowly.

Each can be used without the others, so they make a small menu for a section that needs to open out.

Moore's chapter questions for this topic make a good mix checklist: Are all the layers used, and does the instrument holding each one change? Is any instrument in two layers? How is the box filled, and does that change during the track? Blocks, points or both? Do holes open? Is anything masked and later unmasked? Does any sound move across the space? Do the timbres reveal or disguise their sources? <Source book="moore12" where="Ch. 11 Questions" />

<ListenerSide grade="moderate">

The sound-box is a descriptive model, not a perceptual finding. The cues it relies on are well established in hearing research (level and reverberation for distance, brightness for apparent height), but its norms (the diagonal mix, the depth order) come from Moore's listening across decades of records, and his readings of what a layout means are interpretations.

## Practice

<Exercise title="Sound-box sketch">

1. On headphones, pick one section of a song. Draw a box: left to right across, low to high up, and write near/far next to each sound.
2. Is it a diagonal mix? Where are the blocks, points and holes?
3. Repeat for the section before and after. What changed in the space at the boundary: an entry, a hole, a move, a register band added or removed?

</Exercise>

Add a sound-box line under each section of one of your songs: where the new entries sit, which register band is missing, which of the four ways of making space you use, and the one place the space breaks the diagonal norm.
