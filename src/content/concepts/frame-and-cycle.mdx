## A rigid frame, rotating riffs

Olivia Lucas's study of Meshuggah's *obZen* gives the clearest model of how odd riffs and steady structure coexist <Source book="lucas" />:

- The songs are built from **segments of 8, 16 or 32 bars of 4/4**. That hypermetric frame is the form. It's often carried by a steady cymbal pulse, a snare backbeat, or long notes in the lead guitar.
- **Riffs of other lengths**, for example 9 eighths, 7 quarters or 25 sixteenths, loop inside the frame and drift in and out of phase with it.
- At the end of a segment the riff is **cut off mid-stride**, and the next riff starts on the next downbeat. That cut-off is where the band's characteristic lurch comes from.

Chancellor describes the same principle from inside Tool: put four beats behind any odd-meter riff and you can make it feel straight. The band overlaps time signatures on purpose <Source book="tool-interviews" />.

<KeyIdea>The frame is the form; the misalignment is the content. The listener rides the frame and feels the riff rotate against it.</KeyIdea>

## Beginning in the middle

Lucas adds a subtler idea. A riff's **temporal beginning** (what you hear first) and its **rhythmic beginning** (what *sounds* like the start, once accents and long notes are weighed) can differ. As the riff repeats, listeners may re-hear where it starts, so it seems to have begun before it began. Long durations tend to sound like endings. Strong attacks and register peaks tend to sound like starts.

Her case studies show the same frame-and-cut-off logic handled in different ways:

- **Lethargica:** a 23-quarter riff whose heard start and felt start agree. It's cut off cleanly when the vocals enter, which makes the seam obvious.
- **Pineal Gland Optics:** a pitch pattern of 8 attacks against a rhythm pattern of 14 attacks, realigning only now and then. The riff's apparent end lands on the next section's downbeat, which *smooths* the join.
- **Pravus:** riffs whose first heard note is functionally an ending. One segment is cut off exactly when expectation peaks, which makes the boundary feel abrupt. In another, the next section keeps the same rhythm with new pitches, which blurs it.

So the cut-off is a **design choice**. Make the seam a lurch, or hide it.

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. **Fix the frame first.** Choose the segment length (8, 16 or 32 bars of 4/4) and what carries it: the hats, the snare backbeat, a crash on each segment start, or a long-note melody.
2. **Choose the riff length for the cycle you want.** Its realignment with the frame is the landmark. Check where it falls (the lab shows it).
3. **Decide each seam:** cut off mid-stride (a lurch), let the riff end exactly on the boundary (smooth), or carry the rhythm into the next section with new pitches (a blurred join).
4. **Use rotation for surprise.** Starting a familiar riff from a different note makes the listener re-hear where it begins, without writing a new riff.

</Steps>

<ListenerSide grade="moderate">

The frame is what makes this listenable. Vuust & Witek: complexity engages when it contradicts the meter without undermining it, and the steady 4/4 frame is the meter that's never undermined. Lucas also saw two audiences at once at a Meshuggah show, some moshing and some counting. In her reading both were looking for the groove, just in different ways.

<Exercise title="Build a frame-and-cycle section">

1. Drummer: a 16-bar segment of 4/4 with one clear frame marker, such as a crash every 4 bars.
2. Riff: a cell of 9, 11 or 13 sixteenths, looped from bar 1 and cut off at bar 17.
3. Make two versions of the seam into the next section: a hard cut-off, and a blend (keep the rhythm, change the pitches).
4. Try the riff rotated: start it from its third note. Does the listener re-decide where it begins?

</Exercise>
