## Too soon, too late

The first lesson introduced Hasty's **projection**: a duration that has ended becomes the model for the next one <Source book="hasty" where="Ch. 7" />. This lesson follows what happens when the next beginning *doesn't* arrive where it was projected. Three writers describe it in their own terms: Hasty, Yust and Lerdahl & Jackendoff.

## Hasty: projection, denial, reinterpretation

Hasty's vocabulary <Source book="hasty" where="Ch. 7, 9, 13" />:

- **Projective potential**: the length of the duration just completed, offered to whatever begins next.
- **Projected potential**: the expectation, starting with the new beginning, that it will last as long.
- If the next beginning comes **too soon**, the projection is cut off: an **interruption**. If it comes **too late**, the projection was exceeded and the extra time is felt as suspense.
- Sometimes what sounded like a continuation is heard, a moment later, as a new beginning: a **reinterpretation**. His symbol for it is an arrow from \ (continuation) to | (beginning).
- **Deferral** is his account of triple meter: the third beat defers the beginning of the next measure, so triple time feels lilting or off-balance where duple feels square.

At the level of phrases, Hasty says overlaps almost always involve reinterpretation, and the time this takes varies <Source book="hasty" where="Ch. 13" />. In the first movement of Beethoven's First Symphony, one new phrase is almost immediately interruptive; another takes several bars to disengage itself from the measure before it, which he calls the converse of interruption: a **gradual detachment**.

## Yust: projections and piggybacks

Yust uses projection as a practical tool for inferring rhythmic structure, at the level of the bar and above <Source book="yust" where="Ch. 1, 6" />:

- A **simple projection** is two adjacent spans of the same length. The more of them a hearing can include in one well-formed hierarchy, the more plausible it is.
- A **piggyback projection** is a shorter span that falls in the same place in two successive larger spans. A repeated rhythm inside two bars confirms both bars.
- At the start of a piece, avoid long **hanging spans** before the first full unit (the same as GTTM's "strong beat early").
- After a pattern is established, the main reason to change it is **parallelism**: when a distinctive rhythm repeats, the span from its start to its repetition becomes a unit, even if that breaks the old pattern.

He also names the irregular joins <Source book="yust" where="Ch. 5–6" />. A **lengthened projection** adds part of a span (Bach's F-major Prelude from WTC I passes its idea between the hands every bar and a half). A **shortened projection** removes part of one. A **metrical deletion** is a new span that begins before the old one is complete. An **extended beginning** is a beginning delayed by an extra bar.

## Lerdahl & Jackendoff: metrical deletion

GTTM treats the same joins as exceptions to regular meter, produced when groups overlap or elide <Source book="gttm" where="Ch. 4" />. In an **elision** (the end of one phrase replaced by the start of the next), a beat at the four-bar level can come only three bars after the previous one: "the strong beat comes too soon". They write this as a rule that deletes a weak position from an otherwise regular meter. A rarer kind deletes a strong position. You only realise afterwards that the meter has shifted, and you hear two weak beats in a row (their example is the end of Schumann's "Wehmut").

They also describe two ways of hearing a shift <Source book="gttm" where="Ch. 2" />, following Andrew Imbrie. A **conservative** hearing holds on to the old pattern until it is forced to give it up. A **radical** hearing re-reads at once. In the opening of Mozart's Symphony 40, the two-bar level shifts from odd- to even-numbered bars somewhere in bars 9–13. They decline to choose, and say a performer's slight stress can tip the listener either way.

## Where the writers stand

<Positions title="When the next beginning is early or late">
  <Position book="hasty" where="Ch. 7, 9, 13">Projection denied or reinterpreted; interruption vs gradual detachment; triple meter as deferral.</Position>
  <Position book="yust" where="Ch. 1, 5–6">Projections and piggyback projections infer the structure; lengthened and shortened projections, deletion and extended beginnings name the joins.</Position>
  <Position book="gttm" where="Ch. 2, 4">Metrical deletion at elisions (the strong beat comes too soon); conservative vs radical hearings; the performer can decide.</Position>
  <Position book="caplin" where="Ch. 5">Expansion, extension, compression and elision change a phrase's length; Hasty and Yust both criticise treating large expansions as one stretched bar.</Position>
  <Position book="snyder" where="Ch. 12">Once a meter is inferred it takes a long discrepancy to dislodge it.</Position>
</Positions>

<ListenerSide grade="moderate">

That listeners notice events arriving early or late against an established pulse is well established. Whether listeners actually hear projections at phrase length (several seconds and more) is a theoretical claim; Hasty himself says the definiteness fades beyond a few seconds.

## Practice

<Exercise title="The stop test">

1. Loop a two- or four-bar riff of yours.
2. Stop it exactly where the next statement should begin, and feel the silence.
3. Now play the next statement one beat early, then one beat late. Which version gives you the effect you want at that point in the song?

</Exercise>

<GuitarPro title="Three joins">

1. Write two contrasting four-bar riffs, A and B.
2. Join them three ways: B enters on time, B enters in bar 4 of A (deletion), A's last bar is extended by one bar before B (extended beginning).
3. Play the three joins to the band and choose one for the song.

</GuitarPro>

<ApplyIt>

At each section change in one of your songs, write "on time", "early" or "late". Is the pattern of joins doing what you want across the whole song?

</ApplyIt>
