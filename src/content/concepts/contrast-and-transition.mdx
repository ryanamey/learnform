## How big is a contrast?

Every section change is a contrast of some size. Belkin gives you a way to measure it: a **contrast scale from 1 to 10**, from imperceptible to earthquake <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 11 Contrasting" />. The size depends on three things:

1. **How many** dimensions change at once.
2. **How much** each one changes.
3. **How fast** the change happens.

Not all dimensions weigh the same. Belkin's observation is that **register and timbre** changes hit harder than harmonic changes, which are subtle by comparison. He calls tempo, timbre, register and dynamics the **primary dimensions**: refinements of harmony alone won't make a contrast dramatic <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 4, Ch. 11" />. For a band that means the drummer's feel, the guitar's sound and the register are your biggest levers. A chord change is a small one.

Belkin's own calibration points: flute to piccolo is a 1–2; flute to tuba is about a 5; add a tempo change and it's a 7. A video game switching from exploration music to fight music is an 8–10.

<KeyIdea>To measure a contrast, list what stays the same and what changes. To make it smaller, change fewer things. To make it bigger, change more things, more, at once.</KeyIdea>

### What tells a rock listener a new section has begun

Melodic contrast can't mark a boundary on its own, because melody contrasts all the time inside sections. In classical music the boundary cues are cadences and keys; in rock, Stephenson finds them in four other places <Source book="stephenson" where="Ch. 6" />:

- **Text.** Lines that come back mark a chorus or refrain. The title line usually announces the chorus, and grammar helps: a run of dependent clauses ("When I want you…") makes the title line their completion, so it ends a verse rather than starting a chorus.
- **Instrumentation.** The voice entering ends the intro; several voices entering is what makes a chorus a chorus. In one song he discusses, a single chord and an unchanging groove run for five minutes, and the only thing that marks the chorus is the added voices.
- **Rhythm.** Stop time (the whole band hitting the same sparse rhythm) giving way to a steady groove; a guitar pattern changing; vocal rhythm slowing down in the chorus.
- **Harmony.** In a song built on one repeating pattern, any break in it signals a section. And the new section's **first chord** usually tells you at once: verses tend to start on I, choruses and bridges on IV (most often), V or vi.

In REO Speedwagon's "Keep On Loving You", every phrase ends on V and the key never changes, yet everyone hears where the chorus starts: the title line, backing vocals and acoustic guitar arrive together, the guitar plays the song's shortest notes, and the tonic chord appears for the first time, at the start of the phrase. Four cues at once make the boundary obvious; one cue against stable surroundings makes a subtler one; cues that disagree make it ambiguous.


## Contrast is a budget

Three rules from the same chapter:

- **Longer pieces need more and bigger contrasts**, but over the whole piece there should still be more continuity than contrast. Strong contrasts packed close together make the piece sound like fragments.
- **The same contrast loses force when repeated.** Going from the same A to the same B a second time drops it a point or two: the listener knows it's coming.
- **Abrupt changes work in inverse proportion to how often you use them** <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 8" />. A piece that's all hard cuts becomes a collection of little sections. Mix gradual and abrupt.

## Four routes from A to B

Belkin's chapter on connecting gives a procedure for gradual transitions <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 12 Connecting" />:

<Steps>

1. Know exactly where you start and where you arrive.
2. List **every** difference between them: register, timbre, tempo, texture, articulation, then pitch, harmony, rhythm.
3. Change **one item at a time**. Each step should be about 1–2 on the contrast scale.
4. The transition's length follows from the number and size of the changes. Six differences need more time than two.

</Steps>

Things that stay constant fade into the background; attention goes to whatever changes. So a transition can also keep one strong element constant (a pedal note, a cymbal pattern) while everything around it moves. For drama, change two things at once or take a bigger step.

The other routes:

- **Turning point:** build to a climax and use the peak as a pivot. The new idea arrives as the resolution of the build, so a big change feels caused.
- **Interruption:** a sudden event cuts the music off. Even here a tiny preparation (a pickup, a fill, a rising line that still reaches its peak note) stops it from sounding random.
- **Cross-cutting:** alternate blocks of A and B; one gradually wins by getting longer. Belkin's example is Stravinsky. It loses its force if the cuts arrive at predictable times.

## Contrast presupposes coherence

Schoenberg's rule for every middle section: **contrast presupposes coherence** <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. XIII" />. A contrasting section keeps the meter and motive-forms that aren't too remote and gets its contrast from new variations, a new order of the same motive-forms, and above all **harmony**, a different but related region. He demonstrates it by writing ten different middle sections for one opening section, each on another degree. That is a drill worth copying with a riff: ten B sections, one A.

He also notes that a great composer's imagination often starts to bloom near a cadence, "where lesser composers are happy to have arrived."

## Transitions as sections

In larger forms a transition can be a whole section. Belkin's model (Brahms) uses several tools together: the new idea **overlaps** the old one at a high point, counterpoint knits them, motives mutate step by step (a → b → c → d), and harmonic tension builds before the new theme so its arrival feels like resolution. His summary: always new and familiar at the same time <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 12" />.

The contrast scale and the dimension weights are craft judgements, not measurements. They're consistent with Module 3's tension research (Farbood found loudness and pitch weigh most in listeners' tension ratings), but no study has rated section contrasts in rock. Treat the numbers as a shared language for the band, then test them on listeners.

</ListenerSide>

Schoenberg lists four things a transition does, which can overlap <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. XVIII" />: **establish** its own idea by repetition (often in sequence); **modulate**, often in stages; **liquidate** the motivic features; and **arrive on a suitable upbeat chord** that prepares what follows. Short rhythmic figures suit it, and what sets it apart from a theme is a fluid change of region and the prompt removal of characteristic features. It can be built from new material or from the opening idea, reworked at its start or its end. Large forms grow, in his words, "through the generating power of contrasts", and the transition is itself one of them.

## Contrast without a new section

Two devices from the same collection put contrast inside a familiar shape <Source book="moore03" where="Ch. 8 (Moore), Ch. 9 (Covach)" />:

- **Same words, two settings.** Jethro Tull often set the same lyric twice in one song: once as acoustic, folk-like introspection, once as hard-rock declamation ("Minstrel in the Gallery", "Heavy Horses", "Dark Ages"). Sometimes the electric version intrudes on the acoustic one; sometimes it is the arrival point. Moore hears this opposition of two ensembles, not alternate versions of one arrangement, as the core of the band's idiolect.
- **A formal parenthesis.** In Foreigner's "Feels Like the First Time", John Covach shows the second verse heading for the chorus, but its last chord is reinterpreted as the dominant of the relative minor. A minor-key version of the chorus follows, and the chord that ends it is reinterpreted again to lead into the expected chorus. The contrast that compound AABA form normally places after the second chorus arrives before it, as an insertion that leaves the surrounding form intact.
