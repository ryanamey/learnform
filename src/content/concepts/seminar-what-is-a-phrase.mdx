## The question

"Phrase" is the most used word in this course, and the four writers don't mean the same thing by it. The differences change what you count, where you look for endings, and how long you expect a unit to be.

## Schoenberg: a breath, ending with a comma

The phrase is the smallest structural unit, a kind of musical molecule, roughly what can be sung in one breath; its ending suggests a comma <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. II" />. Its length depends on meter and tempo: in a moderate 2/4 or 3/4 typically two bars, in a slow tempo perhaps one, in a fast one four. Endings are marked by rhythmic reduction and melodic relaxation. Phrases don't coincide with barlines. In the practice sentence, a two-bar phrase is stated and repeated.

## Caplin: a grouping, separate from its function

Caplin uses "phrase" as a **grouping** term: a unit of about four bars <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 2" />. What the phrase *does* is described with separate **function** terms: presentation, continuation, antecedent, consequent. He keeps the two vocabularies apart on purpose: "presentation phrase" names the grouping, "presentation" names the function. He also rejects a rule found in many textbooks, that every phrase ends with a cadence: a presentation repeats a beginning and doesn't close <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 2" />. He distinguishes an **end** (a cadence) from a **stop** (motion ceasing) <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 3" />. And he says that where an idea begins and ends can legitimately vary with the listening habits of the musician.

## Belkin: a clause, sized by attention

A phrase is like a clause in a sentence, sized by the attention span, which he describes as quite short: after a few seconds special effort is needed <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 2" />. It is a piece in miniature: a beginning with one or two motives, an incremental progression to one local peak, and **punctuation** of varying strength, which needs at least two dimensions working together <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 5" />.

## Snyder: one short-term memory

The phrase is the largest unit held whole in the present <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 3–4" />. Short-term memory lasts about 3–5 seconds on average. Smaller groupings are usually bounded by a change in one parameter; phrase boundaries by changes in more than one at once (a pause and a longer note, say). He notes the parallel with spoken language, which is cut into intonation units ending with falling pitch.

## Where they agree

All four tie the phrase to a span of a few seconds (breath, attention, short-term memory, four bars at an ordinary tempo). All four treat endings as graded rather than all-or-nothing.

## Where they differ

- **Size:** Schoenberg's phrase can be a two-bar idea; Caplin's is usually four bars.
- **Does a phrase have to end?** Schoenberg and Belkin describe every phrase as ending with some punctuation (a comma at least). Caplin separates grouping from closure: a phrase can end without a cadence.
- **What marks the boundary?** Caplin: harmony (cadence) for ends, while stops are something else. Belkin: two or more coordinated dimensions. Snyder: changes in more than one parameter; and natural closure (falling, lengthening, pause) as distinct from learned syntactic closure.

## Questions to decide

1. In your band's vocabulary, is a "phrase" a length (two bars? four?), a span of time (a few seconds), or a shape (beginning–peak–punctuation)?
2. Do you want separate words for *how long* a unit is and *what it does*, as Caplin keeps them? Riff music has many units that repeat without closing; which vocabulary describes them better?
3. In one of your riffs, where does the phrase end on each writer's criteria? Do the answers coincide?
