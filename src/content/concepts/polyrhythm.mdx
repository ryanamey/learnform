## Two pulse rates at once

So far this course has mostly dealt with layers built from **one** fast pulse: a riff in groups of three sixteenths against a backbeat in groups of four. A **polyrhythm** in the narrower sense is different: two layers that divide the same span into *different numbers of equal parts*, so that they share no fast pulse at the surface. Three against two, five against four and seven against four are the usual names.

Snyder draws the line clearly <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 12" />. In **polymeter**, parts share a pulse but group it differently, and they realign over a longer span; the tension is mild. In **polyrhythm**, the parts run on different basic pulses, and that undercuts the downbeat of both. He compares tempo ratios to pitch intervals: simple ratios (2:3, 3:4) realign quickly and sound relatively stable, while complex ones (like 15:16) keep the tension longest.

Krebs's labels cover the simple cases: 3 against 2 is a grouping dissonance (G3/2) on a shared micropulse of 6 <Source book="krebs" where="Ch. 2" />. Past a certain complexity the shared micropulse is too fast to hear (a 7:4 needs 28 parts per bar), and the two layers really behave as two rates.

## What the listener does with it

London's experiments point one way <Source book="london" where="Ch. 3, 5" />. Listeners don't hold two meters. They either fuse the parts into one composite pattern and find a meter for that, or they follow one part and hear the other as a figure against it. In practice the band decides which by what it makes loud and steady.

The speed limits from *Levels, tactus and speed limits* apply to each layer. At 90 bpm, the 7:4 notes are about 380 ms apart, comfortably countable. At 140 bpm a 13:4 would put them about 130 ms apart, close to the 100 ms floor, where they stop being notes you count and become a texture.

## Magadini's method

Peter Magadini, a drummer who studied with the North Indian tabla master Mahapurush Misra, wrote the standard practical method <Source book="magadini" where="How to use; §1–11" />. It works for any instrument, or none:

- The order: 3:2 (= 6:4), 3:4 (half-note triplets, then quarter-note triplets, 9:4), 5:4, then 7:4, 11:4 and 13:4, then solos combining them.
- **The basic pulse must stay absolutely steady**: a metronome first, then the drummer's feet (hi-hat and bass drum). It must be felt even where it isn't written.
- **Count one rhythm aloud while playing the other, then reverse.** For 5:4 and 7:4, he insists on learning to count four while playing five or seven.
- For 3:4 in eighths: "Do not count 6"; count 1-and-2-and-3-and with accents.
- Memorise the sound of the two layers together, first on one surface, then on two surfaces an octave apart; vary the tempo.
- Melodic players should make scale patterns and melodic ideas on the counter-rhythm.
- His last section proposes time signatures that show both layers at once (6 over 4, 7 over 4), with barlines only where the layers meet.

Magadini credits African drumming and Indian classical music with bringing polyrhythm into Western practice. His book, he says, suggests their ideas rather than imitating them <Source book="magadini" where="More about polyrhythms" />.

<KeyIdea>Polymeter shares a pulse and realigns; polyrhythm runs on different pulses. Listeners follow one layer and feel the other against it, so decide which layer is the meter.</KeyIdea>

## Practice

<Exercise title="Magadini's swap">

1. With a metronome on quarter notes, clap 3 against it for a minute, counting the quarters aloud.
2. Swap: clap the quarters while counting the triplets.
3. Do the same for 5:4 and 7:4, slowly. Record where your counting breaks down.

</Exercise>

<GuitarPro title="A polyrhythm as a section">

1. Over a 4/4 drum groove, write a guitar figure in quintuplets (5 per bar) for four bars.
2. Write a second version where the drums switch to the five and the guitar keeps four.
3. Which version still feels like your song's meter?

</GuitarPro>

If you use a polyrhythm in a song, name the layer that is the meter and make sure the listener can always hear it (a kick, a hi-hat foot, a bass note).

</ApplyIt>
