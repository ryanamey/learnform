## Why music needs to come home

Belkin's arc for a large piece: a problem or question, deeper exploration, then a turn toward resolution that usually brings back earlier material <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 17 Returning" />. Somewhere along the way the music must **breathe**, and the tools for that are familiarity and predictability. Degrees of relaxation can be controlled as finely as degrees of tension: from a solid cadence after some wandering harmony to a huge recapitulation that feels like coming home.

Snyder gives the same mechanism from memory research: the familiar is more closural than the unfamiliar, which is why so many forms put a return at their most important closing points <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 5" />.

<KeyIdea>A return is never the same music. The listener now knows it and arrives at it from somewhere else, so its meaning has changed. Design for that.</KeyIdea>

## Five kinds of return

From least to most prepared:

1. **Literal return, no warning.** The simplest ternary (A B A): the middle section ends and A just comes back.
2. **Linked return.** A transition between the middle section and the return gives advance notice that relief is coming. Subtler, less square.
3. **Return that grows out of development.** In rounded binary, the return isn't a separate section; it emerges from the exploring. It can be a fragment or a nearly complete reprise.
4. **Varied refrain.** Home is not quite as you remember it. The variation can be ornamental (a new surface over the same skeleton) or structural (new transitions, different cadences). Haydn adds **false returns**: four bars of the refrain that turn out not to be the real one <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 14 Rondo" />.
5. **Return with one critical change.** In sonata form the recapitulation repeats the exposition but rewrites the transition so the music stays home. One structural difference, everything else familiar.

A related technique from Covach's reading of The Cars' "Just What I Needed" <Source book="moore03" where="Ch. 9 (Covach)" />: the chorus introduces a faster harmonic rhythm, and from then on the instrumental **interlude returns at the new speed**: one chord per bar the first time, two per bar the second, and the last time with the first interlude's synth melody over the faster rhythm, finally settling the song's question of major key or relative minor. A change made in one section is carried into the later returns of another.

## The thwarted return

Belkin's two favourite examples work by **building an expectation, then breaking it once**. In a Mozart minuet and trio, four main cadences in a row all land in the same key; when a deceptive cadence and a short transition finally appear, the return becomes special. In Beethoven's Eighth, the end of the recapitulation starts out like the beginning of the development, then turns somewhere else <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 17" />. The lesson for a chorus that returns three times: let the listener learn the pattern, then change one thing on the last approach.

## Three kinds of ending

The ending is one of the moments memory keeps, with the beginning and the biggest climaxes and contrasts. Belkin describes three types <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 18 Ending" />:

- **Climactic.** Reaches an extreme: the highest or lowest register, the loudest point, the fastest tempo. It must be **the** biggest climax of the piece; ending on a secondary climax disappoints.
- **Fade-out.** Also an extreme, of quiet. It has to fade further than any internal calm. His Carter example: slashing chords, failed attempts to restart, a final collapse into the lowest register.
- **Rounding-off.** Brings back enough familiar material that the music feels home. The difficulty is enough familiarity to resolve without boring.

For all three: **at the ending, every dimension agrees**. Most weak endings reach an extreme in one dimension while others still need resolving, like a strong cadence with no build in dynamics or texture. And the release is proportional to the tension before it, so the bigger the piece, the longer the preparation.

### Four ways to end a song

Osborn sorts rock endings by two questions <Source book="osborn13" where="Ex. 1" /> <Source book="osborn10" where="Ch. 2" />: is the last section **old or new material**, and is it a **main section** (memorable in its own right) or an **appendage** to one?

| | Old material | New material |
|---|---|---|
| **Main section** | Final chorus or verse. Radiohead's "Knives Out" ends on a last chorus cut one bar short and simply stops | **Terminal climax**: new, memorable, once (see *Where the climax goes*) |
| **Appendage** | **Outro**: an extension of what came before. Jimmy Eat World's "A Table for Glasses" sings a fragment of verse two over tonic pedals instead of the verse chords, with the band stripped down | **Coda**: new material that trails off; can be long (half of Green Day's "Chump") and risks losing the listener |

The distinction matters most between coda and terminal climax, which are both new: a coda is less memorable than the chorus, a terminal climax more. Osborn also notes "cliffhanger" endings, final verses that sound as if they'll lead to a chorus and just stop, and endings on a bridge, which feel anticlimactic (he cites Tool's "Prison Sex"). Tool often combines two types: a terminal climax followed by a very short outro on the opening riff.

### Ending music that doesn't close

Rock adds a problem Belkin's types don't cover. If every unit ends open and resolves only into the next unit's downbeat (see *Degrees of punctuation*), there is no built-in final cadence. Stephenson argues that the **fade-out** is therefore not a lazy way out but the ending most true to the style, and that live bands solve the same problem by choosing a chord and holding it, like a tape stretched across a running track <Source book="stephenson" where="Ch. 1, 3" />. A long fade can even carry meaning: over the unresolved pattern at the end of "I'm So Afraid", it suggests the cycle the singer fears will never end.

His analysis of Pink Floyd's "Eclipse" shows the opposite: a real cadence earned in a looping style <Source book="stephenson" where="Ch. 7" />. The song is a litany of short lines, each ending on a weak downbeat, over a repeating chord pattern. Twenty-three lines in, one line keeps going word by word, with each word demanding the next, until its key word lands on the strong downbeat with the tonic. Then the pattern skips its V and arrives on I **early, at the end of the pattern instead of the start of a new one**, and the rhythm simply stops. In rock, the strongest possible ending is closure placed at the end of a unit, which the style otherwise never does.

For the band: decide whether a song ends in the style (fade, held chord, a loop that stops) or against it (a cadence placed where the loop would otherwise restart). The second only works if the song has avoided closure up to that point.

## The coda as a separate section

In large works the ending can need a whole section. Belkin's examples show what a coda does <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 18" />:

- It can start like a second development, then fail to stay away from home: a giant cadential approach.
- It **avoids the final arrival** repeatedly: the bass steps aside at the last moment, the root arrives under the wrong chord, a sudden drop in volume acts as a deceptive cadence. Each avoidance makes the drive toward the end stronger.
- It can speed up.
- A motive that has been everywhere can **drop out**, then return for the final push.

And a surprise ending (a sudden loud hit after a quiet passage) works only if an earlier climax made it plausible.

## What a coda can make up for

Caplin's view: by the time a coda begins, the main processes of the piece are complete, so the coda is an **after-the-end**. Yet it's often the real conclusion, because it settles business the return couldn't <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 15" />. The return is constrained to replay the opening in order, so the coda gets five **compensatory** jobs:

1. **Circularity**: bring back the opening idea at the very end.
2. **Restore** material the return dropped.
3. **Refer to the middle section**, often at the coda's start (both follow a closing passage, so the parallel is natural).
4. **Shape a new dynamic curve**: a huge build (Beethoven's Third and Seventh Symphonies) or a dissolution (his *Coriolan* overture), when the opening section's ending won't do for the whole piece.
5. **Realise an implication** left hanging, often a "problem" planted in the opening section.

Two cautions from the same chapter. Avoid salient new ideas in a coda: they ask for development and leave the listener wanting more. And a coda usually doesn't sound like it has started; Caplin locates its start where the return stops matching the opening, and its clear beginning may come later.

## How a coda winds down

Schoenberg describes the usual shape of a coda <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. XVIII, XX" />. It often grows out of a last statement of the main idea and opens with elaborate cadences that wander into distant keys. Then the segments get **shorter** and the cadences **simpler**, until the last ones are just two chords, or the home chord alone. In Beethoven's *Appassionata* the final segments run 6, 4, 3, 3, 1 and 1 bars through a crescendo, then six bars of the home chord die away to almost nothing. The reduction is never mechanical. A coda, in his phrase, is there because "the composer wants to say something more", not to confirm the key.

Two details about the return itself <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. XIX, XX" />:

- **The transition gets bigger, not smaller.** Since the return stays home, the passage that used to modulate is now the only contrast left, so composers usually heighten or lengthen it.
- **Vary the return, but not at its entrances.** "Variation, after all, is a merit in itself", but too much change at the start of a returning theme stops it being recognised. In a minor-key piece, staying in minor too long without relief becomes tiresome, so composers move a theme into major first, then back to minor.

## Analysing a return

Caplin's method for any restatement is simple and worth doing on your own arrangements <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 7, Ch. 14" />: compare the return with the original bar by bar, list every change, mark each as **structural** (harmony, motives, grouping, function) or **ornamental** (dynamics, instrumentation, register, texture, figuration), and ask why for each, including why something didn't change. Typical structural changes: dropping repeats the listener no longer needs, adding a short new sequence that throws new light on an old idea, a turn toward darker, flat-side harmony to avoid monotony, and removing the stops the opening had.
