## The balance problem

The moment you use a motive, you have the central problem of all musical form: **repetition versus novelty**. Repeat it unchanged ten times and attention dies. Change everything about it and the listener can no longer follow — the result sounds like a stream of unrelated ideas <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 1, Motivic variants" />.

Schoenberg puts it as a rule: variation means changing *some* features while keeping others <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. III" />. The skill is in choosing which.

## A scale from 1 to 5

Belkin proposes rating each variant by how strongly it still sounds like its source:

| Rating | What it means | Where it belongs |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | Instantly the same idea (e.g. transposition) | Inside the same section; clear reminders later |
| **2** | Close; obviously related | Developing a line while staying on one idea |
| **3** | Medium: you hear the link, and the change | Transitions — turning one idea into another |
| **4** | Distant: the difference is louder than the link | New sections with a hidden family resemblance |
| **5** | New to the ear | Real contrast; save for special moments |

Two points from the book are worth underlining. First, close variants create continuity, distant ones create contrast — so the scale is really a **dial between flow and change**. Second, if you spend strong contrasts too early, they become the norm and you need ever bigger ones later. Big contrasts are a budget.

<KeyIdea>Every variant you write is a choice of distance. Close variants keep the listener oriented; distant ones spend surprise. Decide which one the moment needs.</KeyIdea>

### What the lab shows

- **Rhythm carries identity.** The inversion keeps the rhythm and stays a 2. The even-rhythm version keeps every pitch and drops to a 5.
- **Leap sizes matter less than you think.** Changing where the last note lands barely registers. Belkin notes that the ear treats leaps mostly as "not a step".
- **Time changes are drastic.** Augmentation and diminution keep everything except speed, and still move to a 4. Played at half speed, a riff stops being a riff and becomes something like a melody.
- **Displacement is a 5, and prog uses it on purpose.** Shift a motif by a sixteenth and the accents land on different beats; the ear hears new material. Tool and King Crimson often repeat a figure while moving it against the beat, so the same notes keep sounding new. You'll study this properly in Module 2.

## Context changes the rating

A variant's rating is not fixed. Belkin's own example: a change that would normally count as distant sounds close if it arrives immediately, on the same pitches, as part of an obvious process like a speed-up. The same transformation heard four minutes later, after other material, might not be recognized at all.

Snyder explains why <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 3–4" />. Inside the short-term memory window of a few seconds, the source is still "present", so the comparison is easy. Across sections, the source has to be retrieved from long-term memory, which keeps a sketch rather than the details. **The further apart two statements are, the closer the variant has to be.**

<ListenerSide grade="weak">

The 1–5 ratings are Belkin's craft judgements, not measured results. Margulis's work on repetition (Module 5) supports the general shape: exact and near repetition is what lets listeners orient and participate, and change is heard against what they already hold. Where a variant actually lands on the scale for *your* listeners is something to test with the A/B lab and a listening session, not to assume.

</ListenerSide>

## Practice

<Exercise title="Ten versions of one riff">

From the roadmap's month-2 project, made measurable.

1. Take one of your motifs (or M).
2. Write **two variants for each rating, 1 to 5**: ten in total. Use at least five different techniques: transposition, inversion, rhythm kept with new pitches, fragment, augmentation or diminution, displacement, register, a new tone or tuning.
3. Save them in the Motif bank as children of the source, with their ratings.
4. Play them to someone else without telling them the ratings. Where they disagree with you is the useful part.

</Exercise>
