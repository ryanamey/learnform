## Principles, not moulds

Belkin's conclusion is the reason this module exists. Mature composers don't pour music into standard forms; they think in **formal principles**: presenting, contrasting, connecting, progressing, returning, ending. His chapter titles are verbs because these are things the music has to *do* <Source book="belkin" where="Conclusion" />. The textbook forms are practice examples of those principles. That's what makes the approach usable for a band like yours: the questions (how to begin, how much contrast, how to return, how to end) are the same in any style.

Two consequences:

- **Parts aren't interchangeable.** Even an identical passage changes meaning depending on where it falls, because memory and expectation have changed.
- **Every piece has a frame**: beginning, continuing, ending. Each needs its own design.

<KeyIdea>Design the whole from a few decisions: does material return, where is the climax, how does it end, and what holds it together.</KeyIdea>

## The coordination principle

Belkin's central craft rule: **no single dimension convinces on its own** <Source book="belkin" where="Conclusion" />. A high note with no support from harmony, texture or dynamics sounds like a mistake; a timbre change mid-phrase with nothing else changing sounds like a wrong entry. But coordination is a dial, not a switch:

- Only at a **major climax** or the **ending** should all dimensions point the same way.
- Everywhere else, controlling *how many* dimensions move together is how you fine-tune intensity and mood.
- With no coordination at all, nothing lands.

This is the thread running through the whole module: punctuation strength, contrast size and climax strength are all, at bottom, *how many dimensions agree at once*.

## Two questions that define the shape

Module 3 asked where the climax goes. Add a second question: **does material come back after contrast?** Together they give you a design space. The table is my synthesis of Module 3's climax shapes with Osborn's work, not a table from any one source.

| | Material returns | No return (through-composed) |
|---|---|---|
| **Climax inside** (on a returning section) | Verse–chorus with a telos chorus; rondo-like riff returns | Rare: a peak with no later return leaves the end to fade or stop |
| **Climax at the end** | Final chorus as peak; a stairstepper | **Terminally climactic** form: a new section, once, at the end (Osborn's “Pushit”) |
| **No single climax** | Process pieces, trance-like cycles | Concatenated riffs (much math-metal); post-rock that thins out; moment form |

Osborn's 2011 study gives the through-composed column its own taxonomy <Source book="osborn11" />. He calls a piece through-composed when no clearly identifiable section returns after contrasting material; immediate repetition is allowed, and a section can be developed as long as it keeps going. Two sections share a theme when they use the same melody, chord progression or rhythm (in rock, often the groove). Two questions sort the pieces:

- **One part or grouped?** Is the piece one continuous span, or divided into **section groups** that share a character (texture, rhythm, tonal centre)?
- **One theme or many?** Is the material unified, or do the sections keep changing?

| Type | Shape | What holds it together | His examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| I · one part, one theme | One idea developed through volume, timbre, texture | The single idea, often as a process | Post-rock: Sleepy Eyes of Death's two-chord "Mean Time 'Till Failure" builds then thins out to synths alone; Animal Collective's "Banshee Beat" fills in its groove one sixteenth at a time and only completes its theme near the end |
| II · grouped, one theme per group | Two or three groups, each developing its own idea | Each group's idea; contrast between groups | Mew's "Chinaberry Tree" moves its focus from melody (group I) to rhythm (a 9-beat groove fighting the vocal) to timbre (morphing synth chords); "Pierce the Air" builds two separate layer-by-layer grooves |
| III · one part, many themes | Riffs and grooves strung one after another | Genre logic: groove, intensity, technique | Math-metal and metalcore: Dillinger Escape Plan builds grooves and dismantles them; The Chariot runs 2/4, 5/32 and 17/16 in its first minute |
| IV · grouped, many themes | Groups of contrasting sections | Rhythm, meter, timbre, key, lyrics — not theme | Emery, Hopesfall, Radiohead's "2+2=5" (one key throughout, split in two by the rock-out at 1:54) |

What the analyses add for a writer:

- **Metal and fragmentation.** Osborn links type III to metal's habits: riffs in fifths and octaves rather than chords, screamed vocals that come in short bursts, few repeated lyrics, and songs that work as showcases for grooves. Many such songs end on the same level they started, as if done stringing riffs together. He refuses to call that amateurish: it's a different logic from goal-directed form.
- **The stable groove as a chorus.** Even in a type III song, a passage where the meter settles (rarely into plain 4/4) works like a chorus: a release that listeners remember.
- **Sections keep their function without returning.** In type IV, a section can be an *independent verse* (narrative words over a simple pattern) or an *independent chorus* (thick texture, a repeated hook) that never comes back. Emery's guitarist on their different-sounding verses: if verses can have different words, why not different chords or beats too.
- **Through-composed songs usually end on their climax.** After a climax only a winding-down makes sense, and a four-minute song has no room for one. Emery's "In a Lose, Lose Situation" drops to voice and one guitar for twelve seconds, then lands the loudest, half-time, major-mode section. Hopesfall's "The End of an Era" shows the full skeleton: a group of five riffs in one minor key, a quiet group that moves to the relative major and half-time while the drummer opens his hi-hat wider and wider, and a climactic group whose hook is the only sung melody in a screamed song <Source book="osborn10" where="Ch. 4" />.
- **Or on the quiet part.** Hopesfall's "Dead in Magazines" reverses metal's usual loud–quiet–loud and ends on the quiet clean-guitar group, saving that familiar texture for the end.

His caveat: these are categories for analysis, not recipes, and real pieces blend them. A correction to an earlier version of this page: Tool appears in the article only in a footnote about *Ænima*'s six interlude tracks, most of which are through-composed; Osborn doesn't classify the album's songs that way.

### Endings that are new but not climactic

Osborn's dissertation names three in-between cases <Source book="osborn10" where="Conclusions" />:

- **Terminally new**: the ending brings new words and melody but stays on the same level as the rest, so it isn't a climax.
- **Terminally anti-climactic**: a new, *quieter* closing section, often on an album's last track. In Mew's "Louisa, Louisa" the singer is left alone with synth pads, and live the band leaves him alone on stage before returning for an encore.
- **Nearly through-composed**: a song with no returns except one (often the opening riff coming back briefly at the end). Osborn admits he found these disappointing, but they're a valid shape.

He also notes that the reading depends on what you weigh: a long instrumental ending can be a forgettable coda by theme and a clear climax by loudness. And for singer-songwriters, the closest thing to through-composition is a **through-composed lyric over repeating music**: when the story is what matters, keep the accompaniment simple and let the words do the travelling.

### The standard forms you're departing from

Osborn's through-composed types are defined against a background, and Stephenson's survey describes that background <Source book="stephenson" where="Ch. 6" />. Rock songs mostly fall into four forms:

- **Strophic**: one section (often a 12-bar blues or one chord pattern) repeated, or a verse–chorus pair repeated; a solo often replaces the third verse.
- **Rounded binary** (AABA): a main section and a bridge that must lead back to it. Inherited from pre-rock popular song, and through it from dance forms centuries old.
- **Verse–chorus–bridge**: verse and chorus two or three times, with a new section after the second chorus. The bridge leads either to the chorus repeated to the end, or to a third verse–chorus pair.
- **Compound binary**: a complete song (a "supersection" as long as a normal song) followed by a second part built on several repetitions of **new** material: one melodic line, chord pattern or riff. "Hey Jude" is the model (three minutes of song, then nearly four of the "na-na" section); "Layla" joins two pieces, a song and an instrumental by the drummer; "Stairway to Heaven"'s short second part has its own tempo, important words and the highest notes.

Two of his points matter for long pieces. Bridges in this repertoire are often **instrumental**, and Chicago's early records stretch them into multisection passages longer than the sung parts. And in his reading of Emerson, Lake & Palmer's "The Endless Enigma" (a three-part, ten-minute piece), even ambitious prog borrowed length, motivic unity, texture and instrumentation from classical music while its phrase, melody and harmony stayed rock. The boundaries between its parts are also ambiguous: the "Fugue" could start at three different points. Compound binary is the standard form closest to the terminal climax: the second part is new, repetitive and often the emotional peak.

## Forms for a song about change

Moore adds a criterion that the table above leaves out: **what the song is about** <Source book="moore12" where="Ch. 6, pp. 174–176" />. Verse–chorus suits songs about a *static* situation: describe it, explain it, look at it from different sides, perhaps decide what to do. Songs that live through a *change* of situation tend to take other shapes, and because verse–chorus is so common, any departure from it carries a strong charge.

- **Arch.** Start somewhere secure, move away, come back. King Crimson's "21st Century Schizoid Man" is Moore's prog example: five linked sections, sung outer sections (two verses with refrain, then one), a faster version of the opening riff in sections two and four, and an improvised centre that moves between sax and guitar (A B C B′ A′). An arch can live in a single parameter: in Lil' Louis's "French Kiss" the tempo slows to a halt and gradually recovers. New Order's long "Blue Monday" reverses the proportions: the intro and the final tag expand to more than half the track, and the verses tell their story inside that frame.
- **Bolero** (cumulative): one long growth, perhaps with one drop before the last climb. See *Climax placement*.
- **Medley**: different stretches of music strung together. Jethro Tull's *A Passion Play* links a dozen sections; the Beatles' "A Day in the Life" and Queen's "Bohemian Rhapsody" join what sound like incomplete songs; Moore says extreme metal often joins unrelated stretches (his example is Metallica's "Blackened"). His important observation: in almost all of these, even usually in metal, **something from the beginning comes back at the end** to give a sense of completion.
- **The hijacked section.** A section can start as a repeat and then turn somewhere new. The third verse of "Alfie" (Cilla Black) is taken over by a new chromatic bass line and never finishes as a verse. Diana Ross's "Ain't No Mountain High Enough" has only one verse; its bridge climbs over a rising chromatic bass to a climax where the singer's plea becomes action. Arcade Fire's "Crown of Love" runs through standard verses and choruses, then a rising bass note triggers a sudden tempo change that turns the song desperate. In each case the form changes *at the moment the situation in the words changes*.
- **Two songs in one.** Chris Rea's "The Road to Hell" is two complete forms back to back: a slow AABA over a drone, then an up-tempo band AABA with only loosely related melody.

For a concept-driven band, the question to ask of each song is therefore not just "where is the climax?" but "does the situation change?" If it does, choose a shape that changes with it.

## Memory sets the limits

Snyder's reminder for any large plan: short-term memory holds a few seconds and a few chunks; beyond that, listeners keep a **sketch** — the boundaries, the landmarks, the salient moments — not the details <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 1, Ch. 5" />. Belkin says the same thing from the composer's side: listeners remember the beginning, the biggest climaxes and contrasts, and the ending, so the composer must make the important moments salient enough to be remembered <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 18" />. Module 5's *What listeners actually hear* covers the evidence that proportions and long-range key plans are barely perceived. Design for the ear, scaffold for yourself.

Kramer adds two things to this picture <Source book="kramer" where="Ch. 2, 10–11" />. First, a song doesn't have to be *linear* at all: the design space above assumes direction, but **moment form** (self-contained blocks, cut together, starting and stopping rather than beginning and ending) and **vertical time** (one extended present) are designs too; see *Kinds of musical time* and *Moment form and strata*. Second, proportions are most audible exactly where direction is weakest: in static, discontinuous music, perceived lengths come close to clock lengths; in strongly directed music, momentum and information bend them (*How long it feels*).

## A sketching method

Belkin's appendix on sketching describes how to grow a form from ideas instead of fitting ideas into a mould <Source book="belkin" where="Appendix A" />. Adapted for a band working remotely:

<Steps>

1. **One idea per card** (one file, one riff-board entry). Don't combine yet.
2. **Explore each idea alone**: close and remote variants. Ask how it could appear later in the opposite character — the lyrical idea as a full-band explosion, the heavy riff as a clean whisper.
3. **Plan each idea's career**: for example, heard twice in its first character, then once transformed as a surprise. Some ideas are seeds for a later turning point.
4. **Extend** each idea into a few phrases.
5. **Shuffle the cards.** Lay them out and try different orders; Belkin mentions Stravinsky's table of paper scraps. a → b and b → a can differ enormously in impact.
6. **Decide each join**: sudden contrast or transition, and how big (contrast scale).
7. **Make a rough draft of the whole** and listen straight through. Check: an idea too short or too long, a transition too slow or too sudden, not enough return to balance, a missing intro or coda.
8. **Take a day or two off**, then listen as if it were someone else's.
9. Only then fill in details.

</Steps>
