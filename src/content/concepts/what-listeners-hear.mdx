## The uncomfortable experiments

Two studies should change how you think about large-scale design <Source book="lalitte" />:

- **Cook (1987)** played excerpts from 30 seconds to 6 minutes long, ending either in the home key or elsewhere. Tonal closure affected listeners' ratings of coherence and completion **only for the 30-second excerpts**. Over longer spans, ending "in the wrong key" made no measurable difference.
- **Lalitte & Bigand (2006)** cut six pieces (contemporary art music, plus rock-blues, Ellington and a world-music track) into chunks of about 6 seconds and **scrambled the order**, avoiding obvious jolts at the joins. Musicians and non-musicians were asked to press a key at every incoherent join. They caught only **about 17% of the bad joins in the popular pieces and 13% in the contemporary ones.** Musical training made no significant difference.

But scrambling wasn't invisible. It **lowered how much people liked the popular pieces**, though not the contemporary ones, and listeners more often attributed scrambled versions to an "inexperienced sound engineer". So listeners are weakly but really sensitive to large-scale order, especially in familiar styles. They feel it more than they can point to it.

<KeyIdea>Proportions, key plans and Fibonacci lengths are scaffolding for you. Listeners follow landmarks, contrast and surface energy. Build both, and don't confuse them.</KeyIdea>

## Why: the memory levels

Snyder's model (Module 1) explains this <Source book="snyder" where="ch. 1" />. A phrase of a few seconds is heard *whole* in short-term memory. Anything longer is **reconstructed** from long-term memory, which keeps a sketch rather than the details: salient events, contrasts, recurring material. A key relationship 6 minutes apart, or a section length that's a Fibonacci number, isn't the kind of thing that sketch holds. A riff that returns in a new feel, a sudden silence, or the highest vocal note of the song is.

Proportions have a second problem: remembered length isn't clock length. Snyder's chapter on form summarizes the research: a stretch full of novel events is remembered as longer than an equal stretch of repetition, and the first of two equal stretches is remembered as longer than the second <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 13, Duration" />. A steady pulse and repeated listening make proportions more accurate, which is one reason a strong groove helps the large form read. Module 4's *How a form is remembered* goes further.

## Design rules that follow

<Steps>

1. **Make the form audible through landmarks.** Each major section needs something memorable that marks it: a motif, a timbre (guitar synth patch, folk percussion), a texture change, a vocal register.
2. **Use returns as reminders.** Recognizable returns, even varied ones (1–2 on the similarity scale), are what let the listener rebuild the large shape.
3. **Keep private structure private.** Use golden sections, Fibonacci or numerical schemes to *generate* material and proportions if they inspire you. Tool's "Lateralus" does, by the band's own account. Just don't expect the listener to perceive them, and don't let them override what the ear needs. (Kramer's chapter on proportion adds nuance: proportions are most audible in static, block-built music and with repeated listening; see Module 4, *How long it feels*.)
4. **Care about order anyway.** Scrambling still reduced liking in popular music. Order matters to how the music *feels*, even when listeners can't say why.

</Steps>

<ListenerSide grade="moderate">

Cook's finding on tonal closure is strong and replicated. Sensitivity to section order is moderate: real, but weak. Neither study used prog or metal, or anything as long as a Tool song, so test your own forms (see *Trance, ecstasy and the listening test*).

</ListenerSide>

1. Listen to a Tool song once, then without replaying it, write down every moment you remember, in order.
2. Compare with a timeline of the song. Which sections left no trace? What do the remembered moments have in common (new timbre, highest note, silence, a return)?

</Exercise>
