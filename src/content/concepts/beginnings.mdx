## What an opening has to do

The first seconds of a piece are among its most memorable, because they come out of silence and the contrast is total. Belkin gives a beginning three jobs <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 15 Beginning" />:

1. **Grab attention fast.**
2. **State a clear character**, so the listener knows what world this is.
3. **Leave something open**: a question the rest of the piece will answer.

The mechanism he keeps returning to is **provocation**: one dimension contradicts the others. A held organ note interrupted by plucked chords makes you ask *why?*. A motive with internal contrast proposes a topic. This is the same idea as the motive page in Module 1, scaled up: contrast inside the opening is what makes it stick.

<KeyIdea>A good opening states a character and asks a question at the same time. Clear but not closed.</KeyIdea>

## Seven ways in

Belkin's examples, translated into band terms:

| Opening type | Classical example | What it could be for you |
|---|---|---|
| **Bursts, then go** | Beethoven Op. 106 fugue: short bursts that stop before the nonstop music starts | Two or three hits with gaps, then the riff takes off |
| **Alternating opposites** | Mozart “Jupiter”: loud tutti answered by quiet strings | Full band hit / solo clean guitar, back and forth |
| **Accompaniment first** | Prokofiev Violin Concerto 1: a mechanical figure that announces itself as background | Drum groove or bass ostinato alone, clearly waiting for something |
| **Planes plus pedal** | Bach *St John Passion*: restless figures and suspensions over a long tonic pedal | Drone or feedback under unsettled parts; the pedal promises scale |
| **Slow, unresolved motive** | *Tristan*: a sequence of an unstable motive, slow, with silences, no clear pulse | Rubato guitar figure with gaps, no beat yet |
| **Frustrated cadences** | Beethoven Symphony 1: it seems to cadence, in the wrong key | Start on what sounds like an ending, then swerve |
| **Several characters fast** | Bartók Concerto for Orchestra | Three contrasting ideas in the first minute, as a promise |

What **doesn't** work as a beginning: fade-outs, gestures that sound final, thinning textures — unless another dimension stays provocative.

## Intros as separate sections

A separate introduction signals that a substantial piece follows. Belkin's observations <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 15" />:

- Intros are often **slower**, which makes the main section more vivid when it arrives.
- An intro can use material unrelated to the main section, but it must sound **clearly incomplete**: tense harmony, an upbeat feel, a rising line or crescendo, a long dominant, a thinning texture before the arrival.
- The intro can come back later as an **anomaly**: Beethoven's *Pathétique* brings its slow intro back inside the fast music. An intro that returns gains a second meaning.
- The arrival of the main section can be the intro's resolution: a quiet suspension that resolves *loud*, launching the body of the piece (Brahms 1).

He also analyses the introduction of Beethoven's Seventh as an *exploring* section: it has its own motives, combines them in startling ways, and works as one giant unstable upbeat to the main theme <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 16" />. A long prog intro can be built the same way.

Caplin adds two distinctions <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 5, Ch. 10, Ch. 16" />. A **thematic introduction** is tiny (a bar or two of vamp before the riff, with no melody of its own), while a **slow introduction** is a whole section before the piece. The classical slow introduction combines two moods that seem incompatible: **stately** (slow tempo, fanfares, the full ensemble, dotted rhythms, loud) and **anticipatory** (stops and starts in melody, rhythm, texture and dynamics, unstable harmony, minor mode, chromaticism). The fanfares are kept hesitant so the piece doesn't seem to have begun yet. And a hesitant opening isn't automatically an introduction: many main themes start with sudden changes of texture and dynamics and stops in the rhythm, and the music only really gets going with the more continuous section after them. If the opening has a stable key, a clear landing and solid phrases, it's the beginning, however uncertain it sounds.

<ListenerSide grade="moderate">

Belkin's claim that beginnings are among the best-remembered moments fits what memory research says about salience: what stands out from its surroundings is noticed and kept, and the start of a piece stands out against silence (Snyder). The opening types themselves are craft categories, not findings.

</ListenerSide>

## Practice

<Exercise title="Three openings for one song">

1. Choose one riff you want to use as a main section.
2. Write three openings for it from three different types in the table.
3. For each, write one sentence: what character does it state, and what question does it leave open?
4. Play each into the main riff. Which one makes the riff sound best when it arrives?
