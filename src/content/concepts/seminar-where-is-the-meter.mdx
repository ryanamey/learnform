## The question

When a Meshuggah riff of 25 sixteenths loops over a cymbal in quarters, what is "the meter"? The writers of this course locate meter in different places: in the listener's attention, in layers of the music, in a frame the band counts, or in the notation. The answer decides how you notate, how you count, and what you expect a listener to follow.

## In the listener

**Snyder** treats meter as a schema the listener infers from accents and then maintains; it persists against contradiction for some time, and several streams can compete for the downbeat <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 12" />. **London** starts from the same place: rhythm is the pattern of onsets you can hear, meter is the listener's anticipation of beats, a habit of attention <Source book="london" where="Intro, Ch. 1" />. On both accounts the meter is not in the notes.

## In the layers of the music

**Krebs** defines meter as the union of all the pulse layers in a passage, consonant when they align and dissonant when they don't <Source book="krebs" where="Ch. 2" />. **Biamonte** adapts his terms to rock but separates rhythm from meter and hypermeter, rather than folding them into one union <Source book="biamonte" where="§1–2" />.

## In a frame the band counts

**Pieslak** hears Meshuggah's riffs as fitted to a symmetrical 4/4 frame: an odd last bar makes the riff block land on a four-bar hypermeasure, which he distinguishes from true metric dissonance <Source book="pieslak" where="pp. 222–225" />. **Lucas** accepts the frame but refuses to choose one correct hearing: following the 4/4 and feeling the weirdness against it, or hearing two cycles at once, are both ways of getting the music <Source book="lucas" />. Chancellor describes Tool putting four beats behind an odd riff to make it feel straight <Source book="tool-interviews" />.

## In the interpretation

**Caplin** counts real bars rather than notated ones and says which is which is a matter of interpretation <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 2" />. **Osborn** hears combinations of 3/4 and 4/4 in Meshuggah's *I* where Pieslak writes barless streams <Source book="osborn10" where="Ch. 1, n. 81" />.

## Four answers to "what is meter?"

The writers added in this round give the question a different shape (*What is meter? Four answers*):

- **A schema in the listener** (Snyder, London): meter is where your attention expects beats <Source book="london" where="Ch. 1" />.
- **Rules applied to the surface** (Lerdahl & Jackendoff): the meter is the best-fitting hierarchy of strong and weak beats, kept until strong contrary evidence arrives <Source book="gttm" where="Ch. 4" />.
- **A process** (Hasty): meter is not anywhere you could point to in advance. It happens as each duration is taken up by the next <Source book="hasty" where="Ch. 7" />.
- **One structure among three** (Yust): the meter is in the listener, but the played rhythm, the harmony and the form each make their own structure, and the music lives where they disagree <Source book="yust" where="Intro, Ch. 1" />.

## Hear the question

Return to the frame-and-cycle lab in *Frame and cycle* and try the rotations, where the frame opens in the middle of the riff. Then the two landmark examples in *What is meter? Four answers*. In each, ask: is beat 1 where the drums say, or where the riff says? If the hi-hat stopped, would you still know?

## Where they agree

All accept that a passage can be heard in more than one metric way. All accept that a steady layer (cymbals, a click, a count) changes what the other layers sound like.

## Where they differ

- **Where meter lives:** in attention (Snyder, London), in the sounding layers (Krebs), in a counted frame (Pieslak), or in the analyst's reading (Caplin, Osborn).
- **Is one hearing correct?** Pieslak argues for the frame; Lucas and Krebs accept several; Snyder says music can be designed to be ambiguous.
- **What to notate:** a 4/4 frame with odd riffs inside it, changing time signatures that follow the riff, or no barlines at all.

## Questions to decide

1. When the band writes a riff that doesn't fit 4/4, will you notate it in a 4/4 frame, in its own odd meter, or both?
2. Do you want the listener to follow one meter and feel the other against it, or to hear two at once? Which layer gets the job of the frame?
3. Is a frame something you count in rehearsal only, or something the audience should hear?
