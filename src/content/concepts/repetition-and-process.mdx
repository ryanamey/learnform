## Repetition changes how we listen

Elizabeth Margulis's *On Repeat* is the central study of musical repetition <Source book="margulis" />. Two of its findings (as reported in Albrecht's review) matter directly for long, riff-based music:

- **Attention moves deeper with repetition.** Early on, listeners detect short repeating units easily. With more exposure they start detecting **longer** repetitions, and get *worse* at noticing the short ones. Repetition pushes attention up the hierarchy.
- **Repetition makes complex music sound intended.** When literal repetition was added to atonal excerpts (Berio, Carter), professional theorists enjoyed them more, found them more interesting, and were more likely to believe a human composed them.

The reviewer's caveats: the book has no single unifying theory, and several of the experiments it proposes were never run.

## The carrier: repetition that disappears

Hove and colleagues scanned 15 experienced shamanic practitioners (average age 50, nine years of practice on average) lying still with their eyes closed, listening to a low hand drum at about 4 beats per second for 8 minutes <Source book="hove" />. In one run they were asked to go into trance, in the other not to. They rated the trance runs as a deep journey (about 6 on a 7-point scale, against about 1 for the other runs).

During trance, a hub of the network for inwardly directed thought became more connected with the networks that hold attention on a task, and **connectivity along the auditory pathway dropped**. The authors read this as *perceptual decoupling*: the brain turns down the predictable drum signal while attention follows an inner train of thought. They link it to predictive coding: what the brain can predict needs less processing (the page *Anticipation, chills and groove* says more).

Three details from the methods are useful for composers:

- **The non-trance drumming was the same drum at the same tempo, just a little less steady.** The beats wandered by about 17 ms on average instead of about 7 ms. That small extra jitter was the researchers' way of making trance *less* likely. A machine-steady repeating layer is the easiest one to tune out.
- **The drum doesn't drive the brain.** The old idea is that a 4-per-second beat pulls brainwaves to the same rate. Their EEG showed no such change in either condition.
- **The way out is a rhythm change.** Each run ended with a distinct 20-second "callback" rhythm, as is customary in this practice, and the authors note that an unexpected rhythmic shift is what usually ends the journey. Breaking the pattern pulls attention back outward.

The limits: a small, trained sample entering trance on purpose, with no untrained listeners. The authors cite other studies in which drumming plus instructions changed novices' experience too, and more hypnotizable people went deeper. Listeners at a gig, with their eyes open, standing in a crowd, may behave quite differently.

## Reich: make the process audible

Steve Reich's 1968 essay "Music as a Gradual Process" adds the composer's half <Source book="reich" />. He wants **the compositional process and the sounding music to be one and the same thing**: no hidden structure, everyone hearing the process unfold. The change has to be *extremely* gradual, like watching the minute hand of a watch. You notice it has moved only after staying with it a while. Even with everything audible, Reich says, the mysteries remain: sub-melodies, psychoacoustic by-products, small irregularities that pull the listener in. He also notes that modal, drone-based music, including drug-oriented rock of the time, focuses the ear on minute sound details.

<KeyIdea>Let a repeated layer become the carrier the listener stops noticing; put slow, audible change on top of it. Repetition does the absorbing, gradual change holds attention, and a clear break in the pattern brings the listener back out.</KeyIdea>

## Anatomy of a process piece

Kramer's analysis of Frederic Rzewski's *Les Moutons de Panurge* shows what a process does to time <Source book="kramer" where="Ch. 12.7" />. The rule is simple: play note 1, then 1–2, then 1–2–3, up to all 65 notes of a melody, then take notes away from the front; players who get lost are told to stay lost, so the unison drifts into canon.

- **The rate of new information keeps falling.** Half the notes are new on the second pass; by the 65th pass, one note in 65. That steady decline, not the motion itself, is what makes the long middle feel static.
- **Small linear threads still matter inside the narrow limits:** a regular pulse slowly emerges; one mode is gradually replaced by another; a leading tone is left hanging for about five minutes before it resolves. These keep the stasis alive.
- **Beginning and ending are linear.** The opening is a learning phase; the subtractive end accelerates and gets exciting again. The timeless part is the interior.

Kramer's point for composers: in process music, motion that is constant and predictable is heard as stasis.

## Long-form repetition in dance music

Two analyses in *Analyzing Popular Music* show how club tracks sustain eight to ten minutes on one groove <Source book="moore03" where="Ch. 5 (Hawkins), Ch. 10 (Kennett)" />.

Stan Hawkins maps Lil' Louis's "French Kiss" (1989, 9'53") as about seventy four-bar units grouped into eleven **cellular groove patterns**. Each new pattern is marked by one entering feature: shakers, then filtered brass stabs, then reverbed claps, then a four-note synth motif, then a modulation down a whole step. Something stays constant (kick, organ, hi-hats) while something else always changes, so the listener keeps expecting the next arrival. The track's turning point is a **tempo collapse**: at the break the tempo slows almost to a stop, the drums vanish and only the voice and slowed brass remain; then the groove comes back slow and speeds up, and the final phase pushes the tempo further.

Chris Kennett's drum'n'bass example, Doc Scott's "Shadowboxing" (8'02"), is even barer: one four-bar breakbeat that never changes except for a 30-second snare drop-out, a sample on every fourth repeat that marks 16-bar units, a sub-bass that is simply on or off, and one cello sample that fades in over almost three minutes through a filter sweep. Elements are switched in and out rather than developed.

The craft lesson for a riff band is the same as Reich's: in a long repetitive section, **plan one change per cycle**, keep one layer fixed as the anchor, and treat tempo and filtering as levers, not only adding parts.

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. **Repeat odd or complex material literally before developing it.** It sounds intended, and listeners learn it (see also *Anticipation, chills and groove*).
2. **After a few cycles, change the slow layers, not the riff.** Attention has moved up the hierarchy, so development should happen up there: layering, register, density, a new voice. The riff can stay.
3. **Write at least one "carrier" section** per album: a long ostinato with one gradual, audible process on top (additive melody, slowly opening filter, phasing, a feel change by degrees).
4. **Make the process audible.** If the change is a hidden scheme, the listener just hears repetition.

</Steps>

<Exercise title="Write a carrier section">

1. Choose a one- or two-bar ostinato (bass and drums, or guitar).
2. Repeat it 16 times. Over it, write **one** gradual process: a melody that gains a note each cycle, a filter that opens over 16 bars, a second guitar that slowly shifts by a sixteenth, or a feel that moves from half to normal to double time.
3. Play it to someone. Ask when they stopped hearing the ostinato, and when they first noticed the change.

</Exercise>
