## Four answers to one question

What is meter? The writers of this course give four different answers. This lesson sets them side by side. It doesn't choose between them; the seminars do the arguing.

| Answer | Writers | In one line |
|---|---|---|
| A schema the listener infers and holds | Snyder, London | Meter is an expectation, an attention habit locked on to periodic events |
| Rules a listener unconsciously applies | Lerdahl & Jackendoff | Beats are points in a hierarchy of strong and weak, chosen by preference rules |
| A process that happens while we listen | Hasty | Each duration that ends offers itself as a model for the next ("projection") |
| One of several timespan hierarchies | Yust | Meter is a regular hierarchy of spans; the rhythm actually played is a separate structure that can disagree with it |

## Answer 1: a schema the listener holds (Snyder, London)

Snyder's chapter on rhythm starts from the listener <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 12" />. A **pulse** is a series of equal beats inferred from equally spaced events; once inferred, it is felt to continue even after the sounds stop, and rhythms are reproduced and judged far more accurately against it. **Meter** begins when the beats of a pulse are differentiated in a regular pattern of strong and weak. Like the pulse, meter is inferred and then persists: it takes a long discrepancy to dislodge it. People even group identical, evenly spaced clicks into twos or threes on their own.

He calls meter a **schema**, a framework with slots, and compares it to a scale: pulse and meter are to time what tuning systems and scales are to pitch, a set of reference points that tell you where you are. Rhythmic patterns are perceived and remembered much more efficiently inside a metrical framework.

<KeyIdea>Snyder: meter is a schema the listener infers and then holds on to. The downbeat is its point of greatest closure, and everything else is heard against it.</KeyIdea>

## The first events decide

Two observations from the same chapter bear directly on how a song starts <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 12" />. The most intense beat is first heard as the start of the cycle, and the first event of a piece is usually taken as beat 1, unless it is made weak and quickly followed by a stronger accent. Starting in the middle of a cycle is possible, but only by building that in. Once a downbeat is established, the means of accenting it can change from bar to bar.

## Beats are categories, and feel lives inside them

Snyder treats the beat as a **category** about a quarter to an eighth of the beat wide on each side. Events falling inside it are heard as *that* beat, early or late; this is where **feel** lives: rushing, dragging, laying back. He reports that live players' deviations are systematic (a drummer pushing toward an important downbeat, important events slightly delayed), while a machine's random "humanizing" is unrelated to the structure and doesn't sound natural. Larger deviations, with events on both sides of the category centre, start to sound sloppy. And how wide the category is may be learned: musicians from very rhythm-centred traditions may hear as structure what others hear as nuance.

On tempo he gives three regions: about **100 bpm** sounds neither fast nor slow; the pulse is easiest to feel between about **60 and 150 bpm**; and usable tempos run from about 30 to 300 bpm. How fast a piece *feels* depends on its fastest subdivisions, not on the basic pulse.

On his account, the downbeat is the point of greatest closure, so a group ending on a weak beat closes at a non-closing place and drives forward. The beats just before a downbeat are a **zone of tension**; he names drum fills, more activity on the last beat, as a way to strengthen the downbeat that follows.

## How long can a cycle be?

Snyder's size limits <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 12" />:

- Groups longer than about four beats are heard as compound: **5 is heard as 3+2 or 2+3**, and larger meters pull apart into smaller ones.
- Cycles longer than short-term memory become schemas rather than categories: you know where you are by learning the pattern, not by hearing the whole cycle at once.
- Very long meters, such as Indian talas, use **strict repetition** of their basic pattern, and render each accented beat with the **same drum sound** every cycle. He notes an inverse relation between how long a meter is and how freely its rhythm can vary, and calls these sounds landmarks.

**Polymeter** (a common pulse, parts in different meters that realign over a longer span) gives mild tension. **Polyrhythm**, in Snyder's sense (parts on different basic pulses), undercuts the downbeat of both parts; he compares tempo ratios to pitch intervals, small ratios realigning quickly and large ones like 15:16 holding tension longest. And **free rhythm**, without a pulse, is less stable than metrical music; in North Indian classical music an unmetred opening section is resolved by the metred body that follows.

London says much the same in the language of psychology <Source book="london" where="Intro, Ch. 1" />: meter is **entrainment**, our attention locking on to a periodic series the way we lock on to footsteps. It needs a beat plus at least one other level, and it works only inside a window of speeds (next lesson).

## Answer 2: rules for strong and weak (Lerdahl & Jackendoff)

GTTM separates two things that ordinary talk mixes up <Source book="gttm" where="Ch. 2" />. **Grouping** is how we cut the music into units: motives, phrases, sections. **Meter** is a regular pattern of strong and weak **beats**, and beats are points without duration (the conductor's ictus, a metronome click). What lies between beats is a **time-span**. A beat that is strong at one level is also a beat at the next level up; in classical tonal music, levels relate by 2 or 3.

The listener picks a meter with **preference rules** <Source book="gttm" where="Ch. 4" />: put strong beats where notes begin, where they are stressed or long, where the harmony changes (the strongest cue), where the bass is stable, and parallel music gets parallel meter. When the cues agree, the meter is clear. When they disagree, it is ambiguous. Once a meter is clear, it is given up only against strong evidence, and syncopation is contrary evidence that isn't strong enough to win.

<KeyIdea>Lerdahl & Jackendoff: meter is a hierarchy of beats (points), inferred by preference rules and kept apart from grouping.</KeyIdea>

## Answer 3: a process (Hasty)

Hasty asks you to stop thinking of a grid <Source book="hasty" where="Ch. 6–7" />. When a sound begins, a duration begins. When a new event begins, the first duration is complete, and its length becomes a **projected potential**: an expectation that the new duration will be the same. That is **projection**. If the next beginning arrives where the first duration predicts, the projection is realised. If it arrives earlier or later, we feel *too soon* or *too late*, and we may reinterpret what we heard. In place of strong and weak, he uses **beginning** and **continuation**.

His test: stop the music exactly where the next unit should begin, and feel whether the silence has a definite length <Source book="hasty" where="Ch. 12" />. Try it on the three versions below. The two-bar riff is the same each time; only the moment the next one begins changes.

Hasty doesn't treat meter as a habit that runs on by itself: each repetition is made anew. For him there is no "conflict of meter and rhythm", only projections that are confirmed, denied or reinterpreted <Source book="hasty" where="Ch. 11, 14" />.

<KeyIdea>Hasty: meter is projection. A finished duration becomes the model for the next, and what we feel is the next beginning arriving on time, early or late.</KeyIdea>

## Answer 4: one timespan hierarchy among several (Yust)

Yust keeps the idea of a regular hierarchy but builds it from **timespans**, not points: a bar contains two half-bars, each contains two beats <Source book="yust" where="Ch. 1" />. Some timepoints of the meter may be silent; they exist in the listener's attention, peaks of an internal oscillation. The rhythm actually played has its own **rhythmic structure**, which usually matches the meter but can disagree with it. Syncopation is exactly where it disagrees. He borrows Hasty's projection to explain how we infer the structure, but, unlike Hasty, he keeps meter as a real psychological thing with **inertia**: once established, it persists against some contrary evidence. And he makes rhythm one of three independent structures, with tonality and form. A passage gets its energy where the three don't line up, for example an important chord on a weak beat.

<KeyIdea>Yust: meter is a regular hierarchy of timespans held by the listener; the rhythm, the harmony and the form each make their own hierarchy, and they can disagree.</KeyIdea>

## Where the writers stand

  <Position book="caplin" where="Ch. 2, 5">Counts real bars, not notated ones, and says which is which is a matter of interpretation. Treats hemiola inside a theme as a way of expanding a function, not as a separate layer of form.</Position>

  <Position book="london" where="Intro, Ch. 1–2">Meter is entrainment: an attention habit, a beat plus at least one more level, inside a window of speeds.</Position>
  <Position book="gttm" where="Ch. 2, 4">Meter is a hierarchy of beats (points), separate from grouping, chosen by preference rules and kept against weak contrary evidence.</Position>
  <Position book="hasty" where="Ch. 6–7, 9">Meter is projection: beginning and continuation, not strong and weak; it is not a habit and doesn't conflict with rhythm.</Position>
