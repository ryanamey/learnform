## What this app is for

Learn Form exists so the band can design long, robust forms of its own, not to make anyone a theorist. Every page ends in something you can write or test. If a page doesn't change what you do in the rehearsal room, skip it.

The app has five working parts, and the method is about moving between them:

| Part | What it's for | When you use it |
|---|---|---|
| **Learn** | One idea per page, with sources, labs and an exercise | Start of the week |
| **Motif bank** | Every riff, motive and sketch, named, with its meter and count | Whenever something is written |
| **Journal** | Five lines after each session; a form principle once a month | End of the week |
| **Form plan and riff board** | The shared language for designing a song before the material exists | Whenever a song is being shaped |
| **Listening test** | Checking on real listeners whether a design works | Once a month, or before a song is locked |

<KeyIdea>Read one idea, hear it in one track, write one sketch with it, note what happened. Small, every week, for a year.</KeyIdea>

## The weekly loop

<Steps title="One week, about 5 to 7 hours">

- **Read (1–2 h).** One page from Learn, in module order. Read its sources only where the page sends you.
- **Analyze (1–2 h).** One track, and only what the page asks you to hear. Draw one of the three maps below.
- **Write (2–3 h).** A sketch of 1 to 3 minutes in your own sound, using the idea. It goes in the motif bank with a name and its count.
- **Reflect (15 min).** Five lines in the journal: what worked, what bored you, what you'd steal again.

</Steps>

Two rules keep the loop honest. The piece you analyse this week is never in the style you write this week, so you take the principle and not the surface. And every idea gets tried on one of the band's real songs before you call it learned.

## How to analyse a track

Start general and get specific; drop any question that doesn't apply. Moore, who wrote the longest list of such questions, points out that a "no" is often as useful as a "yes" <Source book="moore12" where="ch. 11" />. A short version for this band:

1. **Layers.** Which instruments carry the beat, the bass, the harmony and the melody? Does anyone change layer during the track?
2. **Space.** Where is each sound in the stereo picture and in depth? Does that change between sections? Does anything get masked, then revealed?
3. **Groove and meter.** What's the basic kit pattern? Where does the groove or meter change, and is anything heard at half or double time? Are two meters running at once?
4. **Phrase and hypermeter.** Are phrases regular? Where are they cut, extended or overlapped, and does that follow the words?
5. **Tonic and harmony.** What's home in each riff, and how can you tell (the longest note, the one that starts the pattern, the accented one)? Where a pattern repeats, is the join smooth?
6. **Form.** Write the outline. Is it a known pattern, a verse-chorus, an arch, a cumulative build, a medley? Where is it on the line from open-ended groove to closed, phrase-by-phrase form?
7. **Friction.** Where does the track do something the style doesn't lead you to expect? Does the opening or the end use an unusual texture? What does the surprise do?
8. **Voice and persona.** Who is speaking, to whom, from how close? Does the voice change character across the track?

Then draw **one** of the three maps:

- **Material map:** which ideas exist, where they return, how they change.
- **Energy curve:** loudness, density and register together, scored every 15 to 20 seconds.
- **Meter map:** written meter, felt pulse, grouping and cycle length, layer by layer.

## How a song gets designed

Ali and the drummer design most forms, often at a distance; the rest of the band writes material to fit. That makes the form plan the handover document, so it has to be readable by someone who wasn't in the conversation:

1. **Name and count every unit** on the riff board, with its meter and length (see *The riff board and the form plan*).
2. **Write the plan as a table:** section, riffs, bars × count, feel, energy, job, notes for the band.
3. **Give every section a job** (present, establish, build, cue, arrive, release) and every seam a reason to be heard.
4. **Mark what the listener has to learn before you disrupt it.** Complexity has to be felt before it's broken.
5. **Test it** before it's locked: a demo, a listener, the listening-test kit.

## How a band talks while writing

Cohen followed bands through months of rehearsal, and Stokes summarises what she found <Source book="moore03" where="ch. 11 (Stokes)" />. The same habits show up in every room:

- **Rehearse "the hard bit".** The difficult part of a song is usually the *change* between two riffs, not the riffs. Spend the time there.
- **Count the repeats out loud** before a change, and agree who calls it.
- **Cue the change with a fill**, and keep the same fill for the same change, so it works as a signal.
- **Build shared words.** Bands borrow vague words ("chunky", "punchy", "too choppy") and argue past each other. Use the names from the riff board and the app's terms (cue, arrival, liquidation, counter-meter) so a comment means one thing.
- **Watch for theory as a weapon.** In one of Cohen's bands, a member proposed one riff in 3/4 against another in 4/4 because the theory said it would contrast. The others found it "too choppy" but couldn't say why. Test a theory-driven idea on the band's ears before defending it.

## Who we are, across styles

A band can cross styles and still sound like itself. Moore calls the band's own consistent habits its **idiolect**, as against the **style** it works in <Source book="moore03" where="ch. 8 (Moore)" />. His example is Jethro Tull, who moved through blues, hard rock, prog, folk rock and metal and stayed recognisable, largely because so many songs set an acoustic, folk-like ensemble against an electric, hard-rock one. Keep a short list of what makes the band recognisable whatever it plays: a sound, a way of building, a vocal stance, a kind of meter. Check every new design against it. Stealing a principle from Tool shouldn't cost the band its own voice.

1. Pick your first page in Learn and block out the five to seven hours this week.
2. Choose a track for the analysis that is **not** in the style you'll write in this week.
3. Answer the eight questions you can answer, skip the rest, and draw one map.
4. Write and name one sketch in the motif bank.
5. Write five lines in the journal. At the end of the month, turn the best of them into one form principle.

</Exercise>
