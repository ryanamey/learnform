## What listeners actually track

Morwaread Farbood built a model of how listeners feel musical tension moment to moment, tested with over 2,600 online participants and a lab group moving sliders in real time <Source book="farbood" />. The parameters were weighted by how strongly they drove tension:

| Lever | Weight | In your band |
|---|---|---|
| **Loudness** | strongest | dynamics, gain, number of players |
| **Pitch height** (melody) | strongest | vocal register, guitar register |
| **Tempo** | next | actual tempo, or felt tempo via time feel |
| **Onset density** | next | notes per second: subdivisions, cymbal rate, riff busyness |
| Harmony; bass and inner-voice register | weakest | chord tension, low-end register |

Two more findings matter for composing:

- **Trends create momentum.** The model works on *direction of change*. When a parameter keeps moving the same way, listeners expect it to continue, so a steady build pulls forward on its own.
- **Parallel beats conflicting.** When several levers rise together, tension clearly rises. When they move against each other, responses become ambiguous.

The limits: excerpts were short, mostly synthesized, and the model looks at roughly 3-second windows. Harmony mattered more to trained musicians, pitch height more to non-musicians.

<KeyIdea>Write builds as curves of loudness, register, density and tempo moving together. Harmony alone won't carry a build.</KeyIdea>

## Build devices: the prechorus toolkit

Jay Summach's study of the prechorus, the section whose whole job is building toward the chorus, gives a checklist that works in any genre <Source book="summach" />:

<Steps>

- **Faster harmonic rhythm:** chords, or riff roots, change more often.
- **Shorter units:** phrases shrink, so new things arrive faster (Schoenberg's fragmentation again).
- **Rising melody and register:** the vocal climbs, sometimes into a higher or falsetto range.
- **Added layers:** backing vocals, a fuller kit, a new instrument entering.
- **Busier texture:** steady streams of 8ths or 16ths replace sparse accompaniment.
- **Fragmented lyrics, fewer rests, enjambment:** lines break into one- or two-bar units and run on into the next section.
- **Crescendo.**
- **The pregnant pause:** a held breath before the arrival. It can also be used to *withhold* the arrival, which creates uncertainty.

</Steps>

## How to use it

1. For every build in a form plan, write which levers rise and over how many bars. Aim for **at least three levers in parallel**.
2. Save at least one lever for the arrival: if register peaks during the build, the arrival needs something else, such as a feel change, a new layer or a drop.
3. To make an arrival *ambiguous* on purpose, move one lever against the others.

## A composer's rules for progressions

Belkin's chapter on progressions <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 13 Progressing" /> adds four rules the experiments don't give you:

- **Climaxes need a hierarchy.** If every peak is the same height, they seem to get *weaker*, because the listener gets used to them. Each major peak should top the last, and the final one should be the strongest.
- **Rising beats falling.** A long descent in the middle of a piece kills curiosity. Keep falling stretches shorter than rising ones and end them with a small surprise.
- **Simplify before the last climax.** Thin the material and speed up its rate of change, so the final push is clear and fast.
- **Hold something back.** Keep a register, an instrument or a texture unused so its arrival can mark the peak (he makes the same point about orchestration in Ch. 4).

<ListenerSide grade="moderate">

The lever weights come from controlled experiments, with strong evidence for short excerpts. Whether they hold over a 10-minute prog build is untested; your listening tests (Module 5) are where you find out.

</ListenerSide>
