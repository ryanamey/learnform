## Justin London, *Hearing in Time*

London is a music theorist and music psychologist at Carleton College. *Hearing in Time* (Oxford, 2004; second edition 2012) brings rhythm research together with music theory. Meter, for him, is a form of **entrainment**: our attention locking on to periodic events, as with footsteps.

**How it was read for the app:** the first edition from the introduction through Ch. 9; Ch. 6 (Beethoven's Fifth) in full; Ch. 8 through the 8- and 12-cycles, the rest skimmed. From the second edition, only the excerpt in our copy (front matter, introduction, contents).

## His question

**What are the limits and habits of human attention that make a meter possible, and which meters do they allow?**

## His toolkit

- **Rhythm vs meter**: rhythm is the pattern of onsets you hear; meter is your anticipation of beats (Ch. 1).
- **The metric envelope**: subdivisions down to about 100 ms; beats from about 200 ms to 2 s, strongest around 600 ms; a measure up to about 5–6 s (Ch. 2).
- **A meter needs a beat plus at least one other level**; a different subdivision makes a different meter (Ch. 1, 4).
- **Tempo-metrical types**: the same meter feels different at different tempos (Ch. 5).
- **Thick and thin meters**: levels come and go while the beat stays (Ch. 6).
- **Non-isochronous (NI) meters**: uneven beats of 2s and 3s on an even fast pulse, with **maximal evenness** (Ch. 7–8).
- **Rotations and families**: the bell pattern 2-2-3-2-3, 3-3-2, 2-2-2-3-3 ("America") (Ch. 8).
- **Many meters hypothesis**: listeners store many context-specific timing patterns, so a band's feel is part of its meter (Ch. 9).

## Where he agrees with the others

- With **Snyder** on time limits and schemas.
- With **Lerdahl & Jackendoff** that uneven Balkan meters are real meters with their own rules.
- With **Krebs** that metric states change through a piece. He describes the changes in metric *consonance* (levels thickening and thinning) where Krebs describes changes in dissonance.

## Where he differs

- **One meter at a time.** Meter is a single state of attention, so two conflicting *meters* at once are, for him, not possible. Yust reports this as a critique of Krebs. See *Seminar: Is metric dissonance a conflict?*
- **Odd groupings as meters** (against Yust's "rhythmic topoi").
- **Meter vs hypermeter**: no essential difference, but entrainment weakens at slow periods.
- **Habit.** Meter as a practised habit, against Hasty.
