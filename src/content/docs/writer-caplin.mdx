## William E. Caplin, *Analyzing Classical Form*

Caplin is a music theorist at McGill University. His earlier book, *Classical Form: A Theory of Formal Functions* (1998), set out the theory; *Analyzing Classical Form: An Approach for the Classroom* (Oxford University Press, 2013) is the textbook version. It covers instrumental music by Haydn, Mozart and Beethoven only. A companion website has scores and recordings of the examples.

**How it was read for the app:** all twenty chapters' prose (basics, more details, finer points, text boxes, example commentary) and the notes. The review question lists and the captions of the "examples for analysis" were skimmed. Music examples are images; the app re-notates the ones it plays.

## His question

How do the parts of a piece express **where they are in time**: beginning, being in the middle, ending, before the beginning, after the end? He calls these **formal functions** and follows them at every level, from a two-bar idea to a whole movement.

## His toolkit

- **Function versus grouping.** "Phrase" says how long a unit is; "presentation" or "continuation" says what it does. Keep the two apart (Ch. 2).
- **Type versus function.** A sentence is a type; a main theme is a function. A type can serve different functions (Ch. 3).
- **The basic idea**: two bars, a whole texture, characteristic rather than conventional (Ch. 2).
- **Presentation, continuation, cadential**: the sentence's functions. Continuation shows fragmentation, faster harmony, busier surface rhythm or sequence, not necessarily all (Ch. 2).
- **Antecedent and consequent**: the period, with weak and then stronger cadence (Ch. 3).
- **Hybrid themes**, his own category, between sentence and period (Ch. 4).
- **Only three cadences** (perfect authentic, imperfect authentic, half); the plagal cadence is a myth in Classical style. **End versus stop.** **Syntactical versus rhetorical strength**: a loud cadence is not a stronger close (Ch. 2–3).
- **Real versus notated bars** (Ch. 2).
- **Tight-knit versus loose** organization, credited to Schoenberg (Ch. 7, 9 onward).
- **Framing functions**: introductions, closing sections, standing on the dominant (Ch. 5).
- **Retrospective reinterpretation** (one function becoming another) and **form-functional fusion** (Ch. 2).
- Large forms: sonata, rondo, slow-movement forms, minuet, concerto (Ch. 9–20).

## How he works

Bottom up when a passage is unusual: harmony, then surface grouping, then higher grouping, then functions. **Recomposition**: rewrite a passage the "normal" way to see why the composer avoided it. **Accept more than one reading**, provided you state your criteria; he writes that the goal of analysis is to raise and relish reasonable alternatives, not to fix a single interpretation.

## Where he agrees with the others

- With Schoenberg: the sentence, the period, liquidation, tight and loose, and the deletion test. He credits Schoenberg with discovering the sentence.
- With Belkin and Snyder: closure is graded and hierarchical.
- With all three: openings tend to be stable, later sections looser.

## Where he differs

- **Harmony decides.** For him the cadence is harmonic and syntactic; Belkin wants at least two dimensions; Snyder separates natural from syntactic closure (Course II).
- **Derivation.** Function doesn't depend on where material comes from. → *Seminar: Does everything grow from one motive?*
- **Terms.** No contrasting period (hybrid instead), no double period (compound period), no plagal cadence in Classical style. → *Seminar: Sentence and period*; *Riff harmony*
- **With Schoenberg** on codas, the scherzo, and whether looseness is a weakness. **With Hepokoski and Darcy** on the medial caesura and sonata theory (Course IX).
- **Scope.** He writes about one style. Applying his terms to rock is the app's experiment, not his claim.

## Where the app uses him

Course I (basic idea, sentence and period, hybrids); Course II (cadence, end versus stop); Course IX (formal functions, tight and loose); Course X (beginnings, transitions, developments, codas).

## How to read him

Chapters 1–4 for themes (the harmony review in Ch. 1 can be skimmed if your harmony is solid), then 5 for deviations and framing, then 7 and 9 for tight and loose and the overview of sonata form. Each chapter is laid out the same way: basics first, then details, then finer points; the basics alone give the whole theory.
