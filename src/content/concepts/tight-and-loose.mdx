## Stability is a dial for every section

Schoenberg noticed, and Caplin turned into a working tool, that sections of a piece differ in how **tight-knit** or **loose** they are <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 7, More Details" />. Tight-knit organization makes a section feel stable and self-contained; loose organization makes it feel unstable, open, driven. Caplin breaks the impression into seven criteria:

| Criterion | Tighter | Looser |
|---|---|---|
| **Tonality** | Stays in the home key | In another key; looser still if it modulates |
| **Harmony** | Prolongs the home chord; diatonic | Sits on the dominant; sequences; chromatic, borrowed chords |
| **Cadence** | Strong landing | Weak landing (on the dominant); an evaded landing; no landing at all |
| **Grouping** | Symmetrical in twos (4+4, 8+8) | Symmetrical in other sizes (6+6); asymmetrical (4+3+5) |
| **Functional efficiency** | Each job done once | Redundant (extensions, repeats); ambiguous |
| **Motives** | Uniform material and accompaniment | Many ideas, changing accompaniment |
| **Conventionality** | A standard type (period tightest, then sentence) | A one-off design |

The criteria can agree or pull against each other. When they conflict it may be impossible to say which of two sections is looser; Caplin's advice is then to describe the specific loosening devices rather than rank.

<KeyIdea>Looseness is relative. The opening section of a piece sets the standard of tightness for that piece, and every later section is heard against it, not against other songs.</KeyIdea>

For a riff band the table translates directly. Tonality and harmony become the riff's home note and the bass. Grouping becomes bar counts and cycle lengths. Efficiency is how many times a riff repeats beyond what its job needs. A section that keeps one riff on the home note, in clean 4- or 8-bar units, and lands it hard is tight. A section in odd groups, over shifting bass notes, that never quite lands is loose.

## Loosening is not weakness

Caplin is emphatic about this, because his analytical language ("absent presentation", "functional inefficiency", "cadential obscurity") sounds like criticism <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 9, text box" />. It isn't. Each loosening device suits a section's job, and **deft handling of loosening is a sign of compositional skill.** In the classical sonata the plan is clear: a tight main theme, a looser transition that destabilises it, a much looser subordinate theme that dramatises the arrival of a new key, and a development that is the loosest of all.

## How each kind of section loosens

**A transition** (Caplin's three jobs: destabilise the home key, loosen the form, and **liquidate** its motives to "clear the stage" for what comes next) <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 11" />:

- skips a full beginning, or starts directly in the middle with a sequence;
- extends its middle, stretches its approach to the landing;
- ends on the dominant, often with a passage that holds that chord (a "standing on the dominant") and then a gap. Hepokoski and Darcy call the gap a **medial caesura**; Caplin treats it as a common cue, not a requirement.

Transitions are often short, four to eight bars. Much of what older theory called transition is really the start of the next section.

**A subordinate theme** (the section that must confirm the new key) <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 12" />:

- repeats its basic idea a third time, or repeats its whole opening;
- weakens its opening stability (starting on an inverted chord, or over a held bass note from the transition);
- **delays fragmentation**, keeping the long units of the beginning while the harmony speeds up;
- gives the middle and the ending **separate phrases** instead of fusing them;
- stretches its closing progression over several phrases;
- and above all, makes the final landing **hard-won** through failed attempts (see below).

Its typical arc: tuneful and soft at first, then increasingly energetic and aggressive as the landing approaches. A group of several such themes is the main way classical composers made expositions longer, and the interest lies in how the loosening devices are **distributed**: evasions in one theme, a huge closing progression in another, sometimes one tight theme placed first, in the middle as relief, or last to give a sense of closing.

**A development** uses Caplin's **pre-core / core** plan <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 13" />:

1. **Pre-core**: softer, discontinuous, hesitant: "the calm before the storm". Often the opening idea in a new key, or a closing figure; often incomplete.
2. **Core**: a relatively large **model** (4–8 bars) repeated in sequence, then **fragmented**, leading to a landing on a dominant and a passage that holds it. Loud, restless, continuous accompaniment ("chugging"), often imitation between parts.
3. Optionally a second core, or a separate **retransition** that leads home.

When there is no model-and-sequence process, a **pseudo-core** can do the job: the core's loudness, density and restless rhythm without the sequence, sometimes held together only by a long rising or falling bass line.

## Reaching the landing

The most dramatic loosening device is the failed landing. Caplin distinguishes three kinds, and the difference is about **grouping**, not just chords <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 5, Ch. 12" />:

- **Deceptive**: the music lands, but on the wrong chord. The wrong chord is still the goal of the phrase; it groups backward. Then the approach is tried again.
- **Evaded**: at the moment of landing, something starts instead. The event at the arrival point groups **forward** with the next attempt, so nothing ends. Often the music backs up and runs the approach again: Schmalfeldt's **"one more time"** technique. It can even happen on the right chord, if a change of texture, register or dynamics makes it sound like a beginning; a performer can help with a breath before it.
- **Abandoned**: the approach itself goes wrong (the bass leaves the dominant's root, or the dominant never comes) and the music wanders before a new approach.

Their effects differ. A deceptive landing gives a temporary goal. An evasion is the most dramatic: closure is snatched away at the last second and immediately attempted again, which builds a strong expectation and makes the eventual landing feel earned. Abandonment is less dramatic but loosens strongly.

## The return tightens again

A return of the opening material usually restores tightness, but rarely all of it. When a return is shortened or changed, Caplin finds it usually becomes **looser** than the original. The exception is a return that stays home where the original wandered off: it is **tighter** <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 7" />. Two further habits are worth stealing: returns often remove the stops and gaps the opening had, to keep momentum after an intense middle, and an idea worked hard in the middle section can fittingly be left out of the return <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 14" />.

<ListenerSide grade="moderate">

Tight-knit and loose are analytical categories, but most criteria map onto things with listening evidence: harmonic stability and closure, symmetric grouping (easier to hold in memory, see Module 1), and repetition. The aesthetic claims (evasions dramatise, loosening suits middles) are Caplin's readings of a classical repertoire. Treat them as hypotheses for your style and check them in the listening test.

</ListenerSide>

## Practice

<Exercise title="Loosen a tight section">

1. Take a tight section of yours: one riff on the home note, 4+4 bars, a hard landing.
2. Make three looser versions, each changing one criterion only: (a) odd grouping, (b) the riff moved in sequence over a moving bass, (c) the landing evaded once, then achieved.
3. Play each version after the original. Which feels most like a middle? Which feels most like a struggle to arrive?

</Exercise>

<ApplyIt>

Draw a tightness line for one of your songs: a 1–5 rating per section. Make sure the opening sets the standard, the middle is the loosest, and the final arrival is earned by at least one failed attempt.

</ApplyIt>
