## Two ways to build a theme

Schoenberg describes two standard ways to build a complete theme, the **period** and the **sentence**, and calls the sentence the higher form, because it starts developing almost immediately <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. V, VIII" />. Its basic scheme (8 bars in the practice form) is:

1. **Presentation, bars 1–4.** A basic idea (2 bars), then its immediate repetition, exact or moved to another degree. Repetition makes the idea stick.
2. **Continuation, bars 5–6.** The idea is broken into fragments, units get shorter, sequences push upward. New energy, no new material.
3. **Liquidation and cadence, bars 7–8.** The characteristic features are gradually removed until only neutral residue remains, which no longer asks to be continued. Then the arrival.

Schoenberg's own advice on the presentation: the repetition is often **complementary**. The idea is stated on the tonic and answered on the dominant, keeping its rhythm and contour, like a question and answer (I–V, then V–I) <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. V" />. For a riff that means stating it on the home note and answering it on another degree with its rhythm intact. He warns that an idea with too many chords can't be answered this way; a simple harmonic shape transposes cleanly.

Caplin's later terms, *presentation*, *continuation* and *cadential function*, describe the same stages, and he reads them as **beginning, middle and end** <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 2" />. Two refinements from his chapter on the sentence matter for writing:

- **The continuation has four signs, not one**: shorter units, faster chord changes, busier surface rhythm, sequence. They can stand in for each other. A continuation that keeps the two-bar units can still sound like a middle if the chords change twice as fast. See *Formal functions*.
- **Why the presentation doesn't end**: repeating a beginning strengthens the sense of beginning. Only a contrasting idea with closing support can end anything.

## Liquidation: how to make something end

Schoenberg's definition: liquidation gradually eliminates the motive's characteristic features until only uncharacteristic ones remain, ones that no longer demand continuation. Its purpose is to stop the music from extending forever. It usually comes with **shortening**. Two-bar units become one bar, then half a bar.

Belkin links this to **neutral lines**: scale fragments, simple figures, clichés <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 1, Neutral lines" />. A cliché is familiar, so it releases attention. That's why it works at endings, and also why music needs occasional neutral passages to breathe. Wall-to-wall motivic density is exhausting.

In the M demo, listen to bars 7–8: the stutter and the tritone are gone, leaving a falling line and a power-chord hit. That is liquidation in Schoenberg's sense: the characteristic features are removed before the arrival. Caplin adds a detail: liquidation is strictly any elimination of characteristic motives, immediate when the continuation is new material, more audible when it's gradual <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 2" />.

## Sequences create obligations

Schoenberg's chapter on the scherzo puts the continuation in a sharper light. The emphasis a sequence gives (it is a form of repetition) **creates obligations**: once you've pushed a pattern up three times, the listener expects it to go somewhere. Liquidation is how you pay that debt <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. XVI" />. Reduce the pattern to half its length, then less, dissolve it into scales or broken chords, and the music reaches two states at once: **repose** (the harmony stops moving) and **suspense** (the return is anticipated). A pedal point, a held figure, or an ostinato of residues holds that state. His summary of the pedal point is worth keeping: it's a **retarding device**, for moments when the harmony must be stopped from moving too far or too fast.

Two more devices from his analyses:

- **Chain-like construction.** The end of one motive-form is the start of the next, so the links overlap and nothing stops between them (Beethoven op. 10/3) <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. V" />.
- **Dovetailing.** When Mozart extends a four-bar unit to five or six, he often brings in the next unit a bar early to compensate, so the extension costs no time <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. XVIII" />.

## The period

The period postpones the repetition. Schoenberg: the first phrase is followed by more remote motive-forms, and only the second half returns to the beginning; the practice form is 4 + 4 bars with a caesura in bar 4 <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. VI–VII" />. Caplin's terms: an **antecedent** (basic idea + contrasting idea, ending with a weak cadence, usually a half cadence) and a **consequent** (the basic idea returns, and the contrasting idea now closes with a stronger cadence) <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 3" />. His model is the Romanze of Mozart's *Eine kleine Nachtmusik*.

Caplin points out that you can't tell from the basic idea whether a sentence or a period is coming: the next two bars decide. And he gives the two types different characters, citing Erwin Ratz: the period has repose in itself, the sentence strives forward <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 3" />. Schoenberg ranks them; Caplin describes them without a ranking. That difference, and several disputes about what counts as a period, are laid out in the seminar.

## Without a tonal cadence

Classical sentences end with a harmonic cadence. Music that doesn't use the V–I formula raises the question of what ends a phrase instead, and the writers answer differently:

- **Caplin** keeps the cadence harmonic: a formal **end** is not the same as a **stop**, and music can stop (a rest, a break) without a cadence, or cadence while the motion continues <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 3" />.
- **Belkin** prefers the word punctuation: it needs at least two dimensions working together, and outside tonal harmony rhythm, register and texture can do it <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 5" />.
- **Snyder** separates **natural** closure (falling intensity, longer durations, a pause, return; found across cultures) from **syntactic** closure (learned formulas such as cadences) <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 5" />.

Course II (*Punctuation*, *Cues and arrivals*) takes this up in full.

## At other time scales

The scheme can be stretched. Caplin counts in **real** bars, not notated ones: in a slow piece one notated bar can hold two real bars, and in a fast one two notated bars can make one <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 2, Finer Points" />. Caplin also says that whether real bars match notated bars is a matter of interpretation. For a band that moves between half-time and double-time feels, this raises a question to test: does a sentence still read as one when its "basic idea" is a long riff cycle and its continuation spreads over a minute? Snyder's memory limits (*Three time levels*) suggest where the scheme stops fitting in one short-term memory and starts to be remembered instead.

Pop history shows the sentence growing exactly this way. Summach found that in the early 1960s the sentence became the most common verse shape in hit songs, and that songwriters kept stretching its continuation until it split off as a section of its own: the **prechorus** <Source book="summach" where="sentence origins" />. A verse, prechorus and chorus can be heard as a sentence's presentation, continuation and arrival spread across a whole strophe. The page *Building tension* has the details.

## Beyond the scheme

Schoenberg is clear that real pieces rarely match the practice form exactly. Extensions come from:
- remote variants that need extra repetition to be understood
- units of unequal size
- insertions

His test is worth stealing: **try removing bars**. If the music still makes sense without them, they're an insertion or extension, and now you know what job they do.

Liquidation drains distinctiveness. Snyder's account of habituation says that what is fully predictable leaves the focus of attention, and the prediction view (Vuust & Witek, Course XII) says the same. Belkin's point that clichés help endings is in line with both, from practice. Whether rock listeners perceive the sentence shape itself as a unit has not been tested in the sources here.

1. Use a motif from your bank.
2. **Sentence A**, 8 bars in 4/4, following the scheme exactly, like the demo.
3. **Sentence B**, same functions, but each stage longer: a 2-bar idea repeated, then 4 bars of continuation that fragments further each bar.
4. **Sentence C**, same functions in an odd meter or with a displaced repetition.

<Exercise title="Schoenberg's self-check">

From his chapter of advice for self-criticism <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. XII" />. Run it on one of your sentences.

1. **Play the melody and the harmony (or riff and bass) separately.** One can hide the other's weakness. A good line should still be smooth and balanced alone.
2. **Are the significant features of your motive being developed, or only the neutral ones?** Find any empty bars: no melodic movement, no rhythmic movement, no harmonic change.
3. **Remove what isn't essential**: too much variation, decoration, remote variants, sudden register jumps.
4. **Watch the highest note** and the phrase endings. Is the climax near the end? Do the endings vary?
5. **Watch the bass line** as a second melody.
6. **Make many sketches** and join the best parts. He calls methodical variation no substitute for invention, but good training, like an athlete's.

</Exercise>
