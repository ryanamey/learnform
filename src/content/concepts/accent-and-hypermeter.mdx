## Three kinds of accent

"Accent" means three different things, and confusing them causes most arguments about where the downbeat is. Kramer follows Lerdahl and Jackendoff <Source book="kramer" where="Ch. 4.2" />:

| Accent | What it is | Can it move the meter? |
|---|---|---|
| **Stress** (phenomenal) | Emphasis on one note: a hard attack, a loud hit, a silence just before it | Almost never. A heavy stress on an offbeat stays an offbeat, it just sounds louder |
| **Rhythmic** | The focus of a rhythmic group, a point of stability. Often near the **end** of the group (a cadence) | It shapes groups, not meter. It can sit anywhere in a group |
| **Metric** | A point of **initiation** on the grid of strong and weak beats. A hypermeasure starts with its strongest one | It *is* the meter. It's felt, not heard; it can even fall on a rest |

Meter, in Kramer's account, is a series of timepoints of different strength that the music establishes, reinforces and sometimes redefines. Once established, it runs on by itself until something contradicts it decisively. That's why a riff can stress the "wrong" beats for a long time without moving the downbeat: stress isn't metric accent.

<KeyIdea>Phrases and hypermeasures are different things. A phrase is a rhythmic group with its weight wherever its goal is; a hypermeasure is a metric unit with its weight at the start. When they line up after disagreeing, the moment hits hard.</KeyIdea>

## Hypermeasures

Bars group into larger "measures": usually 2 or 4 bars, sometimes 3, each starting with a strong bar. We feel our position in a 4-bar group without counting, because we track strong and weak on several levels at once (1 2 at the level of bar pairs, 1 2 3 4 inside the bar, and so on) <Source book="kramer" where="Ch. 4.7" />.

Kramer's important claim for anyone writing in odd groupings: **irregularity is judged level by level.** A 5-bar group among 4-bar groups is irregular because one level suddenly has three beats where it had two, not because of the extra seconds. And an irregularity on one level can disappear on the next level up (a 10-bar span and a 9-bar span can still be two "beats" of a larger unit). So meter can be irregular and still deeply hierarchic.

## Stretching, shrinking, overlapping

Three operations change hypermeasures <Source book="kramer" where="Ch. 4.8" />:

- **Extension**: a group runs longer than expected (a bar repeated, a cadence held).
- **Contraction**: a group is shorter than expected (a bar dropped, harmony compressed).
- **Overlap**: one bar is both the weak end of a group and the strong start of the next. Kramer stresses that this only counts when a bar heard as weak is *re-heard* as strong. Overlaps often coincide with a rhythmic arrival meeting a metric downbeat after the two have been out of phase, which is why they're such powerful moments.

Kramer's lesson: by alternating 2- and 3-beat groups at various levels and using these three operations, **meter becomes expressive**. And unequal spans can balance: a 12-bar group (an extended 8) and a 6-bar group (a contracted 8) can feel like a matched pair, even though one is twice as long.

## What creates a metric accent

There's no algorithm, but the cues are known <Source book="kramer" where="Ch. 4.9" />. Lerdahl and Jackendoff's preference rules, simplified: repeated patterns keep the same metric reading; metric accents fall early in groups and on note onsets; strong beats coincide with long notes, new harmonies, changes of dynamics or articulation; the bass is more stable than the upper parts; a cadence shouldn't contradict the meter; two-beat groupings are the default. Lester and Berry add registral leaps, texture and timbre changes, new entries, contour changes and surprise.

For riff music that gives practical levers: if you want the listener to re-hear where "one" is, change several cues on the new downbeat at once (bass note, crash, new riff, new harmony). One cue alone (a stress) won't do it.

Kramer also makes a point about deep levels: at large scale there are fewer cues (in tonal music mainly harmony, plus texture and timbre), so those few cues carry more. Without tonal harmony, as in much modern music, there may be too few cues for a deep hierarchy. In moment forms there are only a few metric levels at all.

## Humans aren't sequencers

From Kramer's chapter on technology <Source book="kramer" where="Ch. 3.5" />: performers never play exact ratios. In 38 performances of a folk song, the half–quarter rhythm averaged about 1.75:1, not 2:1, and even players trying to be metronomic keep a scaled-down version of their expressive timing. Physically simple timing sounds psychologically unnatural; natural timing is physically complex. Performers project meter by timing (Clarke): the same melody in different metric settings is played with different note lengths.

His advice to composers working with machines is relevant to a band using guitar synths and sequenced parts: don't waste effort faking human timing. Use machines for what humans can't play (Nancarrow), or move deliberately between human and machine feel. And note the reverse: a human audibly fighting a very hard rhythm has a kinetic intensity that a sequencer playing the same notes lacks. Difficult to play is not the same as difficult to hear.

<ListenerSide grade="moderate">

The accent distinction and the preference rules come from music theory (Lerdahl and Jackendoff); later perception research on meter, such as London's *Hearing in Time* (in your library, not yet worked into the app), takes them up. Kramer's view that meter can be irregular and still hierarchic is his theoretical position, not a finding. The timing studies (Seashore, Bengtsson & Gabrielsson, Clarke) are experimental.

## Practice

<Exercise title="Move the downbeat">

1. Play a 4-bar riff cycle three times as regular hypermeasures.
2. Version A: add only heavy stresses on an offbeat. Does "one" move?
3. Version B: on a new bar, change the bass note, add a crash, start a new riff and change the harmony at once. Does "one" move now?
4. Version C: write an overlap: make the last bar of a cycle a cadence that is also the downbeat of the next.

</Exercise>

<ApplyIt>

Take a song you know with odd groupings. Write out its hypermeasures and mark each irregular group as an extension, a contraction or an overlap. Is it irregular at the deepest level, or does it even out?
