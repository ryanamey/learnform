## The question that organises modern song form

Recent theory of rock and pop form asks **where the energy goes and whether material returns**, more than what the sections are called. Two sources give you the main shapes.

**Schwitzgebel's climax strategies** <Source book="schwitzgebel" /> describe how a song reaches its peak through texture:

- **Release:** a bridge builds through cues, often ending in near silence, then an upbeat launches the full final chorus. The contrast is the climax.
- **One more time:** the first chorus after the bridge arrives *sparse*, defying expectation. A cue then repeats the chorus in full texture. The climax is the deferred payoff.
- **Stairstepper:** each chorus adds texture, a terraced climb, and the last step completes it.
- **Hybrid:** a combination, usually release and stairstepper.

**Osborn's terminally climactic form** <Source book="osborn13" /> describes songs whose most memorable moment is **not** a repeated chorus but a **single, new section at the end**:

- The **terminal climax** brings in new material (melody and lyrics not heard before), happens **once**, and works as a real main section, not a short coda. Often it's where the tonic is finally stated clearly, the vocal reaches its highest note, and the groove goes half-time or anthemic.
- The verse and chorus are deliberately **understated**, so they don't compete with the ending.
- Archetypes: **two-part** (verse–chorus group → climax), **three-part** (verse–chorus group → a multi-section bridge group that builds → climax), and **extended** (the bridge group splits into contrasting loud and soft sub-groups).

## Tool's "Pushit" as Osborn reads it

Osborn's extended example is Tool's "Pushit" (*Ænima*):

- The verse and chorus share **the same guitar-and-drum groove**. The contrast between them is mostly sonic: overdrive and crash and ride cymbals in the chorus.
- The first two minutes work as **one growing arc** of texture and dynamics.
- The middle is three dynamically distinct groups, **loud–soft–loud**, with the bass groove developed under a guitar solo in the second loud group.
- In the climactic group the vocal reaches the song's **highest note** in a more operatic tone, and the meter loosens.
- The **terminal climax** (around 9:06) sits over a 6/8 groove. Instead of repeating a hook, the lyrics keep developing, and only repeat a line at the very end for closure.
- A brief unison outro stops on a **non-tonic chord**, leaving the song unresolved.

Osborn also notes how terminal climaxes are prepared: mainly by **adding and removing layers** rather than playing louder, by **saving register** for the end, by **saving harmonic clarity** for the end, and by repeating hooks more at each level (verse, then chorus, then climax).

## A third shape: cumulative build

Mark Spicer's "(ac)cumulative form" (summarised in the Master source list; the article is paywalled and not yet read here) describes songs built by **adding layers one at a time** over a stable base until the full texture arrives. It's the post-rock model, and the natural partner of Reich's gradual process (Module 5).

Moore, who calls the song-length version **bolero form** after Ravel, adds the details that make it work <Source book="moore12" where="Ch. 6, pp. 175–176; Ch. 3, p. 85" />:

- **Growth with one cessation.** Doves' "Satellites" grows across four verse–chorus units (the third without voice), not quite steadily, then strips everything away except the voice for the last verse, and grows the final chorus to the maximum: louder, richer, denser, with more reverb. The single drop before the end is what makes the last climb hit.
- **Sections matter less than the sweep.** In a bolero form, the repeats of verse and chorus aren't the point; the listener is meant to feel gradually swept away. Texture changes are the form, but each one has to point towards the whole.
- **Cumulation most often opens a track.** Deep Purple's "Smoke on the Water" introduces guitar, hi-hat, snare and kick, bass, and finally voice and organ; New Order's "Blue Monday" works the same way. Building the intro this way is a standard move. Building the whole song this way is the rarer, larger decision.
- **It can be combined with an arch.** Deep Purple's "Child in Time" grows its opening section to a climax (texture and the singer's range), breaks into an improvised middle, then repeats the whole directed growth. See *Form design* for arch forms.

## An additive build across a whole song

Rob Bowman's analysis of Otis Redding's 1966 "Try a Little Tenderness" is the most detailed account of a song-length build in the reading so far <Source book="moore03" where="Ch. 6 (Bowman)" />. The song is a 1933 Tin Pan Alley standard; Redding keeps the melody, chords and words within a recognisable range and builds a different piece out of the performance:

- **A1** is almost empty: guitar and bass on beat 1, single fills from piano, organ, guitar and sax.
- **A2**: the drummer enters with only a rim click and closed hi-hat.
- **B**: the piano fills more often, the organ brightens, ninth chords appear.
- **A3**: the guitar plays a continuous comp, organ and piano both comp, the horns hold pads for the first time.
- **The full kit arrives on the pick-up to bar 65.** Holding it back that long is delayed gratification, and bringing it in at the end of a strain avoids a cadence and drives straight into the next section.
- **C**, a new section Redding added, repeats an eight-bar tag: four bars of tension (chords every two beats rising chromatically, horns on every off-beat, call and response at close range) and four bars of release (descending, the title line, attacks on the beat). The third time through, a two-bar break of voice, kick and hi-hat is the climax.

Meanwhile, the drummer raised the tempo from about 96 to 114 bpm so evenly that the beat length shrinks steadily across the whole track, and Redding's vocal rasp is applied the same way: light, then heavier, withheld for half the bridge, peaking at the break. Every parameter rises together, which is the condition Farbood found gives the clearest rise in felt tension (see *Building tension*). Bowman also notes a change of logic: the written A and B strains are linear, while the added C section is circular, a repeated cell rather than a story.

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. For each song, choose **one** primary shape: telos chorus, stairstepper, release, one-more-time, terminal climax, or cumulative.
2. Write down what you're **saving** for the peak: the highest vocal note, the clearest tonic, the full band, a new groove (half-time or a new meter), or new lyrics.
3. Check the sections before the peak. Do any of them spend what you're saving?
4. Across a record, use **different shapes**. Four telos-chorus songs in a row is one shape repeated four times.

</Steps>

## The ending must be the biggest event

Belkin's chapter on endings <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 18 Ending" /> adds a warning that applies to every shape above. A loud ending has to be **the** climax of the whole piece: ending on a secondary climax disappoints. A fade-out ending has to fade further than any internal calm. And at the very end, **every dimension should agree** (register, loudness, harmony, texture). If only one dimension arrives, the ending sounds like a stop sign, not a conclusion. Module 4 takes this further.

Salimpoor's study (Module 5) found reward activity rising in the *anticipation* window before a peak, roughly 15 seconds, distinct from the peak itself. That supports long, audible approaches to climaxes. The sample was tiny and selected, so this is a direction, not a rule. Osborn's forms are analytical observations, not perception experiments.
