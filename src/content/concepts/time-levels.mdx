## Before the motive: three ways of hearing time

Course I begins one step below the motive, with a question Bob Snyder puts at the start of *Music and Memory*: what can a listener actually take in, and at what speed <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 1" />? His answer is a simplified model with three processes, each working on its own time scale. He calls them three **levels of musical experience**:

| Level | Memory process | Time scale (approx.) | What you hear as one unit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Event fusion** | echoic memory and early processing | faster than about 1/16 s per event | single sounds: a pitch, a hit, a change in loudness |
| **Melodic and rhythmic grouping** | short-term memory | from about 1/16 s up to 3–5 s (8 s at most) | patterns, figures, phrases |
| **Form** | long-term memory | beyond about 8 s | sections, movements, whole pieces |

Snyder stresses that the three are processes, not places in the brain, and that they interact; the borders between them are zones, not lines. He also notes that the idea of three time levels comes from Stockhausen, who did not tie them to memory, and that the match between levels and memory processes is not exact <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 1, notes" />.

<KeyIdea>Snyder's model: the same physical thing, events per second, is heard in three different ways depending on how fast and how long it is: as sound, as pattern, or as form.</KeyIdea>

## Level 1 — when rhythm turns into sound

Snyder's clearest demonstration is a train of clicks played faster and faster <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 2, Ch. 10" />. Slowly, you hear separate clicks and can count them. At around **16 per second** they blur into a grainy buzz. At around **20 per second** the buzz becomes a **pitch**. Nothing changed but the rate, yet the words you use change: below the threshold you say *faster* or *slower*, above it you say *higher* or *lower*. In his words, what is a rhythmic pattern below the fusion threshold becomes "a timbre or tone color" above it.

He gives intermediate thresholds too <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 10" />:

- up to about **5 events per second**, the order of different events is clear;
- around **10 per second**, a melodic pattern can still be told apart from another one but no longer followed note by note; it is heard as a whole, like a texture;
- around **20 per second**, the events fuse into one higher-level event.

Try it. Each button plays a click train at a fixed rate; the sweep goes from 2 to 200 clicks per second in sixteen seconds.

<FusionLab />

For a band this is concrete arithmetic. At 120 bpm, sixteenth notes are 8 per second, sextuplets 12, thirty-seconds 16. Snyder's thresholds say where a guitar run, a tremolo figure or a double-kick pattern sits on that continuum. Whether that is a problem or a resource is a compositional decision; Snyder only describes what the ear does.

## Level 2 — the present

Between about a sixteenth of a second and a few seconds, events are grouped **in the present**: you hear a figure or a phrase as one thing, all at once, and can compare it with the one before <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 1, Ch. 4" />. This is the level of motives, riffs and phrases, which is the rest of Course I. Snyder puts short-term memory at **3–5 seconds on average**, sometimes longer, and its capacity at a handful of chunks; he treats the phrase as the amount that fills short-term memory once: the largest unit we can hold whole.

The other writers in this course work at this level without Snyder's vocabulary. Schoenberg's phrase is roughly what can be sung in one breath <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. II" />; Belkin sizes the phrase by the attention span <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 2" />; Caplin's four-bar phrase falls within the same range at ordinary tempos <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 2" />. *Phrase, grouping and memory* takes this level apart.

## Level 3 — form is remembered, not heard

Anything longer than short-term memory can't be grasped in the present. Snyder's point is strong: at the formal level the **order of events is not kept automatically**; it has to be **reconstructed** from long-term memory, and long-term memory keeps a schematic outline plus a few vivid moments rather than the details <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 1, Ch. 6" />. He notes that we speak of form with metaphors of moving through space: being *in* a section, *getting lost*, *coming back*.

Two consequences he draws, which later courses build on:

- **Sections** are held together by things that stay constant and separated by changes in several parameters at once <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 13" />. (Course II and IX.)
- **Remembered duration is not clock duration.** Novelty makes a span seem longer in memory; repetition and a steady meter make judgements more accurate <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 13" />. (Course VII.)

## What the other writers assume

Snyder states his model from the listener's side. The three composition writers mostly work from the score and from practice:

<Positions title="Who works at which level">
  <Position book="schoenberg" where="Ch. I">
    Form means organization that makes a piece comprehensible, and "one can comprehend only what one can keep in mind". Subdivision is how a piece fits the mind. He builds upward from phrase and motive.
  </Position>
  <Position book="caplin" where="Ch. 1–2">
    Analyses form as functions at every level, from idea to movement, measured in bars (real, not notated). Perception is not his subject; he treats large tonal plans as structural.
  </Position>
  <Position book="belkin" where="Introduction, Ch. 2">
    Asks what music must do so an attentive listener stays engaged. Uses attention span and memory as working limits, from experience rather than experiment.
  </Position>
  <Position book="snyder" where="Ch. 1, 13">
    Starts from memory research. Questions whether listeners hear large-scale tonal plans, citing studies that challenge it, while noting that experienced listeners may differ.
  </Position>
</Positions>

That last difference, whether long-range tonal form is perceived, is a real disagreement between the writers. It comes back in Course IX; here it's enough to notice it.

<ListenerSide grade="strong">

The thresholds (order at about 5 per second, holistic hearing around 10, pitch fusion near 20) and the few-second span of short-term memory are well established findings that Snyder summarises from psychoacoustics and memory research. The neat mapping of three memory processes onto three musical levels is his teaching model, and he says himself that it is a simplification.

</ListenerSide>

## Practice

<Exercise title="Map one song onto the three levels">

1. Pick one long song you know well.
2. **Level 1:** list two sounds that work as single events (a particular hit, a chord colour, a fast figure heard as texture).
3. **Level 2:** time three of its phrases or riff cycles in seconds.
4. **Level 3:** without listening, write down the order of its sections from memory. Then listen and check. What did memory keep, and what did it lose or reorder?

</Exercise>

<GuitarPro title="Where does your fastest figure sit?">

1. In Guitar Pro, write your fastest riff or fill at its real tempo.
2. Work out its events per second: (bpm ÷ 60) × notes per beat.
3. Make two versions: one slowed until it falls under about 5 per second, one at full speed. Listen to both. Which features survive at full speed: the notes, the contour, or only the colour?

</GuitarPro>

<ApplyIt>

Take one of your own songs and mark which moments you intend the listener to *hear* (level 2: riffs, hooks) and which you expect them to *remember* (level 3: section changes, returns, a climax). Is each level-3 moment marked by something distinct enough to survive in memory?

</ApplyIt>
