## Three clocks in every listener

How long a section *feels* is not how long it *is*. Kramer's chapter on perception pulls together the psychology and adds his own informal experiments <Source book="kramer" where="Ch. 11" />. Three things set perceived duration:

1. **Information**: how much new, hard-to-compress material the stretch contains. More information means more memory space, and more remembered time (Ornstein's storage-size model).
2. **Meter**: music's internal clock. A clear hierarchy of strong and weak beats lets us feel elapsed time without counting.
3. **Tempo**: both the beat rate and the rate of information. The same piece at a slower tempo feels and is remembered as longer, even with identical notes.

Over a few seconds, meter tends to dominate. Over longer stretches, information takes over. And they can disagree: two 8-bar phrases are equal metrically and unequal in information.

<KeyIdea>Repetition, pedal points and literal returns shorten a section in memory. New material, fast harmonic change and complex patterns lengthen it. Plan lengths by how they'll be remembered, not by bar counts.</KeyIdea>

## What the research and Kramer's tests show

- **Patterned is shorter.** A 16-note pattern repeated for 62 seconds was drawn about 15% *shorter* than 55 seconds of the same notes in random order (Kramer's class test). Ornstein's tapes of 200 everyday sounds: the randomly ordered tape was judged a third longer than the grouped one. Kramer adds that past about a minute, monotony could flip this, because boredom lengthens time while it's happening.
- **Repeats shrink.** A literally repeated exposition seems shorter; a known piece feels shorter; a florid passage over one harmony feels shorter than one with fast harmonic rhythm.
- **Pauses help when they match the grouping.** Deutsch found rests at natural chunk boundaries aid memory; rests against the grouping hinder it.
- **Counter-grouping adds length.** Dynamics that support the grouping add little information; dynamics that imply a different grouping add a lot (and performers slow down to let it register).
- **Approaching a goal stretches time.** Perceived duration expands as a goal-directed task nears completion (Meade). In linear music, the approach to a cadence or a climax feels longer than the clock says; in moment time, with no goals, it shouldn't.
- **Empty and overfull both drag.** Moderately filled intervals feel shortest. A texture so dense it can't be sorted becomes one undifferentiated sound: low information, static.
- **Attention to time lengthens it** (the "watched pot" studies).
- **Discrimination is coarse.** For spans of about 4–30 seconds, people need differences of roughly 8–16% to tell lengths apart, although doubling and halving are judged surprisingly well.

## Meter as a clock: a test

To isolate meter, Kramer played students the 20-bar opening of Brahms's *Haydn Variations* (built in 5-bar phrases) and a recomposed 16-bar version in 4-bar phrases <Source book="kramer" where="Ch. 11.9" />. By the clock the ratio is 5:4. By hyperbeats (each phrase is one "big beat") it's 1:1. The students' judgements fell **between** the two. Where meter is weak and irregular (Stravinsky's *Symphonies*), measured and perceived proportions should be close, because only one clock is running.

He also recomposed the exposition of a Mozart sonata from 93 bars down to 54 by removing repeats, extensions and pedals. The short version didn't feel like 58% of the original, because almost all the structural information was still there.

## Golden sections and Fibonacci numbers

Kramer's chapter on proportion surveys golden-section analyses of Bartók (Lendvai), Debussy (Howat) and others, and Stockhausen's use of Fibonacci series <Source book="kramer" where="Ch. 10" />. Points that matter for a band that has read about "Lateralus":

- What makes the ratio attractive to composers: it's the only ratio that **nests** (a golden section of a golden section is the same ratio again), consecutive Fibonacci numbers share no divisor (balanced but not periodic), and sums are audible.
- Howat's Debussy analyses show something more useful than the ratio itself: **different processes (dynamics, motives, tonality, texture) can each follow their own proportional plan, out of phase, and the moment they coincide creates a large-scale arrival.**
- Kramer's verdict: proportions matter, especially in static, discontinuous music, but **no single ratio is the source of balance**, and in strongly directed music, momentum distorts perceived length so much that bar-count proportions are mostly private. He found few golden sections in Stravinsky but other consistent ratios. What makes proportions work is **context**: a teacher can hear that a passage is too long *for its material*, whatever the numbers say.
- Familiarity helps. Proportions become more perceptible with repeated hearings, and composers, who know their material in every order, judge them better than first-time listeners.

## The length of the present

The perceptual present has a limit: up to about 8–10 seconds (20 at an extreme) <Source book="kramer" where="Ch. 11.12" />. That's the longest phrase we can take in without chunking it. Everything in a phrase stays available and revisable until a cadence closes it and sends it to long-term memory, which is why the end of a phrase can change the meaning of its beginning. Kramer calls the **phrase** the fundamental perceptual unit of music, more basic than motives or sections. (Snyder's figures in Module 1 agree.)

## Two ways of listening at once

Kramer ends the chapter with a duality worth keeping <Source book="kramer" where="Ch. 11.10" />:

- **Active, linear listening**: timing, expectation, following where the music goes.
- **Cumulative listening**: building a memory image of the whole ("the trail left by a jet"), which is where proportions, consistencies and nonlinear structure are grasped.

Context shifts the balance: a strong groove pulls attention to timing; dense harmonic or motivic information pulls it to encoding; static music leaves little but the information to attend to.

<ListenerSide grade="strong">

The duration findings (information/storage size, filled vs. empty intervals, goal approach, attention, discrimination thresholds, the present) are from the psychology Kramer reviews and are well supported for laboratory stimuli. His own experiments (the chunked/random sequences, the Brahms and Mozart recompositions) are informal classroom tests, and he flags them as such. How they scale to a 10-minute song is extrapolation.

## Practice

<Exercise title="Same length, different time">

1. Write two 30-second passages at the same tempo: one built from a two-bar riff repeated, one through-composed with a new idea every bar.
2. Play them to someone in both orders on different days and ask which felt longer.
3. Then write a third version of the repeated passage with a counter-grouping in the accents. Does it feel longer than the plain repeat?

</Exercise>
