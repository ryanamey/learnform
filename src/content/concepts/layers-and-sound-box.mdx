## Texture is jobs, not instruments

Allan Moore starts his analysis of recorded songs with texture, because the sound of a track is usually what draws a listener in first. He describes the norm of popular music as **four functional layers** <Source book="moore12" where="Ch. 2 Shape (preview)" />:

| Layer | What it does | Usually |
|---|---|---|
| **Explicit beat** | States the beat pattern; nominally unpitched | Drum kit |
| **Functional bass** | Gives the groove its other half; connects root-position harmonies | Bass guitar |
| **Melodic** | Carries the tune and the words; primary and secondary lines | Voice, lead instrument |
| **Harmonic filler** | Fills the register between bass and melody | Rhythm guitar, keys, pads |

Three points in his account matter most for writing:

- **Layers are defined by function, not by instrument.** One instrument can change layers within a track: in The Who's "My Generation" the bass becomes the lead during the instrumental section; a piano can move between filler, bass and fills.
- **An absent layer is still implied.** A solo guitar or piano supplies the beat and bass by implication, or plays against an imagined beat. Listeners hear what's missing as missing.
- **Changes of texture mark structure.** Moore found that students who couldn't follow themes could follow form by listening for texture changes (a whole band switching to stop time for a chorus, for example). The filler layer is also the one listeners use most to identify a style, and much metal does without it.

<KeyIdea>Before asking what a part plays, ask what job it does. A riff band's form is partly a plan of which layers are present, which are absent, and which instrument holds which job in each section.</KeyIdea>

## The fifth layer: novelty

Megan Lavengood adds a fifth layer to Moore's four <Source book="lavengood20" where="Parts 2–3" />. The **novelty layer** is melodic but isn't the melody: hooks, fills, colour, often answering the voice. A mixing engineer would call it "fills"; organists call the equivalent "toy stops"; a writer in 1607 called such instruments ornaments that season the ensemble.

Studying 1980s hits that used the same synthesizer's factory sounds, she found a consistent split:

- **Core layers blend.** Bass, melody and filler sounds were mostly *full*, *rich* and *harmonic* (all the overtones, many of them, in whole-number ratios). Sounds like that fuse into one band sound.
- **Novelty sounds refuse to blend.** They were mostly *hollow*, *sparse* and *inharmonic* (bell-like), often with a noisy attack. Sounds like that stay separate, so they're heard as events.
- **Novelty follows form.** Core layers run through every section. The melody owns verses, choruses and bridges. The novelty layer takes intros, interludes and outros, and inside the main sections it speaks only in the gaps the melody leaves.

Two cautions from the same article. "Marked" (attention-drawing) depends on context: against pop's clean-guitar norm, distortion is marked, but **in metal the reverse is true**, and a clean guitar is the marked sound. And in 1980s pop the novelty layer was often filled with "world instrument" presets used for exotic colour. If your drummer's folk percussion goes into this layer, give it a structural job, not decoration.

## When a sound changes jobs

Because layers have norms, crossing them can carry the form. Lavengood's analysis of Band Aid's "Do They Know It's Christmas?" shows a two-part song (verses and choruses, then a long accumulating coda) whose division is marked by two sounds changing roles <Source book="lavengood20" where="Part 3" />:

- A synth voice enters at the end of a chorus with a new melody, **drops it after one phrase**, and becomes a chordal pad instead. The broken-off melody sets up an expectation.
- In the coda, that sound **takes the melody back** and completes the phrase.
- The bells, a novelty sound throughout, first answer the singers' "Feed the world" call; once the full texture has built up, they **play the same melody as the singers**, moving from novelty into melody as everyone joins in.

For a band this is a form tool that costs nothing: plan which instrument holds which layer in each section, and save a role change (the guitar synth taking the melody, the bass becoming the lead, the vocal dropping into the texture) for a structural moment.

## Background planes

Belkin's last chapter describes the same idea from the composer's side <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 20 Refinements" />. **Enriching** planes are background layers of *movement* (repeated notes, arpeggios, trills) or *resonance* (sustained sound). They must not compete with the foreground, so they differ from it in timbre, register, speed and rhythmic motive, and each one on its own should be uninteresting. In Moore's terms they're filler; in Lavengood's, they're the opposite of novelty.

## Heaviness from clashing layers

One more layering device, from Adam Krims's study of 1990s rap production <Source book="moore03" where="Ch. 7 (Krims)" />: producers stacked loops that **agree on the meter but not on the harmony**, and detuned them against each other by about a quarter tone. No combination of layers ever resolves, and listeners heard the result as menacing and hard. Krims doesn't discuss metal, but the principle (heaviness from layers that won't fuse) is worth testing against the heavy guitar stacks you already use.

## Timbre analysis in practice

Lavengood's toolkit is a set of ten opposite pairs you can check by ear or on a free spectrogram (Sonic Visualiser, Audacity) <Source book="lavengood20" where="Part 1" />. For each sound, decide which side it's on, relative to the norm of your genre:

| Group | Pairs |
|---|---|
| Sustain | bright / dark · pure / noisy · full / hollow · rich / sparse · beatless / beating · harmonic / inharmonic |
| Attack | percussive / legato · bright / dark |
| Pitch | low / high · steady / wavering |

The attack matters more than most people assume: listeners identify a sound's source far more accurately when they hear its onset. Like Roman numerals, the table is raw data; it becomes analysis when you ask what job each sound's profile suits it for.

## Practice

<Exercise title="Layer map">

1. Pick a 4–6 minute song. Make five rows (beat, bass, melody, filler, novelty) and one column per section.
2. Fill each cell with the instrument holding that job, or leave it empty.
3. Mark every place an instrument changes job. Does each change line up with a section boundary?
4. Rate each sound you hear on three pairs: full/hollow, harmonic/inharmonic, percussive/legato attack. Do the novelty sounds share a profile?

</Exercise>

<ApplyIt>

Draw a layer map for one of your own songs before arranging it. Decide which section has no filler, where the novelty layer speaks, and which single role change you're saving for the biggest structural moment.

</ApplyIt>
