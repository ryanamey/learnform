## Meter is something the listener does

London's starting point is that meter isn't in the notes <Source book="london" where="Intro, Ch. 1" />. **Rhythm** is the pattern of time between note onsets, which you can hear. **Meter** is the listener's anticipation of beats, a habit of attention that locks onto regular events the way we lock onto footsteps or a dripping tap (psychologists call it *entrainment*). Two consequences matter for a band that writes in odd meters:

- **Listeners hold on to a meter once they have it.** That's what makes syncopation and hemiola possible: the meter is kept against the surface. In experiments, re-hearing a pattern with a different downbeat was much harder than shifting a pattern against a steady meter.
- **Meter needs more than a beat.** A meter is a beat plus at least one other level, usually a subdivision below and a bar above. If you change the subdivision (straight to triplet), London counts it as a different meter, even if the time signature stays the same.

## The window your meters have to fit in

Meter only works inside a range of speeds, and London collects the research on it <Source book="london" where="Ch. 2, 4" />:

| Level | Range | What happens outside it |
|---|---|---|
| Fastest countable note | about **100 ms** (a sixteenth at 150 bpm) | Faster notes blur into a trill, a texture, or grace notes |
| Beat | about **200–250 ms to 2 s** (roughly 240 bpm down to 30), strongest around **600 ms** (100 bpm) | Faster beats are heard as subdivisions; slower ones as bars, or not at all |
| Bar or cycle | up to about **5–6 seconds** | Longer spans aren't felt as one unit; they're counted or remembered instead |

Within that window there's a second boundary at about **250 ms**. Shorter values are heard as a group of quick notes; longer ones are heard as durations you can feel passing. A beat also needs room to be subdivided: about 200 ms is the shortest beat with a duple subdivision and about 300 ms with a triplet one.

Two practical consequences:

- **Tempo changes the meter.** The same notes at a different tempo can put the beat on a different level. A riff in sixteenths at 70 bpm and the same riff at 140 bpm aren't the same meter in London's sense; at the faster tempo the old beat becomes a subdivision. London calls the result a different **tempo-metrical type**, and tempo is a compositional choice, not a performance detail.
- **Slow tempos leave room for subdivision, fast tempos for bigger groups.** At fast tempos there's no room for subdivisions above 250 ms; at slow ones the bars run up against the 5-second ceiling. That's one reason a slow 7 and a fast 7 feel like different kinds of music.

## Odd meters: beats of two and three

London's chapters on **non-isochronous meters** (NI meters) are the theory behind every 5, 7, 11 and 13 your band plays <Source book="london" where="Ch. 7–8" />. In an NI meter the fastest pulse is steady, but the beats are uneven, made of 2s and 3s: 7 as 2-2-3, 9 as 2-2-2-3 (Brubeck's "Blue Rondo à la Turk"), 11 as 3-3-3-2. His rules for which ones work:

- **The pulse has to be there.** The uneven beats are measured in pulses, so in nearly all music with NI meters the pulse keeps sounding: a hi-hat, a bass, a guitar chug. Without it, a 3 next to a 2 sounds like a slightly late beat (an expressive push), not a long one. Also, NI meters tend to live at faster tempos, where the running pulse helps keep the energy up.
- **Beats as even as the length allows.** London's rule is **maximal evenness**: the beats should be spread as evenly as the number of pulses allows. Nine pulses in four beats gives 2-2-2-3; nine in three beats must be 3-3-3. A pattern like 2-3-4 doesn't work as a meter, because the 4 just splits into 2+2.
- **2:3 is the natural ratio.** Long and short beats almost always relate as 3 to 2 (sometimes 4 to 3). A 2 next to a 5 splits into 2+3; ratios like 4:5 sound like an even beat with a push; very long beats break in half. Three different beat lengths in one bar are hard to hear and tend to reduce to two.
- **Rotations are different meters.** 2-2-3 and 3-2-2 have the same parts, but the long beat lands in a different place relative to the downbeat. London treats them as distinct meters, and they feel different: long-last leans into the next bar, long-first lands heavily.
- **Prime lengths force unevenness.** 5, 7, 11 and 13 can't be split into equal beats: 5 is 2-3, 7 is 2-2-3, 11 can be 3-3-3-2 or 2-2-2-2-3, 13 can be 3-3-3-2-2 or 3-3-3-4. Lengths like 8, 10 and 12 can be heard either way: 3-3-2 is the tresillo, which you can hear as three uneven beats *or* as 4/4 with a syncopation, depending on what the band does. The 12-cycle offers the most options of any length (seven beat patterns), which may be why it's so common across cultures.
- **Uneven beats come with a tempo window.** With the pulse much faster than about 150 ms, a 3+2 starts to sound like one swung beat; with the pulse slower than about 250 ms, the long beat itself becomes a slow beat. London thinks NI meters are more tempo-sensitive than even ones.

### Which beat gets the missing note

A long note inside an odd meter raises a question: where does the beat inside it go? London borrows an answer from scale theory: the listener fills the gap in whichever way gives the most even, least ambiguous meter, as a listener fills in a missing note of a scale. In 12 pulses, the durations half note, dotted quarter, quarter, dotted quarter (4-3-2-3 eighths) are heard as a five-beat meter of 2s and 3s, with a silent beat splitting the half note into 2+2; a split into 3+1 or 1+3 isn't possible, because a single pulse can't be a beat. The point for writing: the durations you choose decide what meter is heard, and repeated durations in an odd meter tend to *become* beats. If a figure you mean as a syncopation keeps repeating, the band may hear it as a meter change instead.

### When nobody agrees where 1 is

In West African music such as Gahu, the bell pattern 3-3-4-4-2 can be heard with its downbeat in five different places, and listeners do re-hear it during a long performance (a "gestalt flip"). London points out that the people who hear it differently still share the bar length and beat pattern, so they can still clap and dance together. For a band, a long repeated riff in an odd grouping can be heard starting in more than one place. That can be the design (a riff that shifts its apparent 1 as the band changes) or a problem (the audience is on a different 1 from the drummer). The cue that settles it is whatever marks the cycle: the kick, a chord change, the vocal.

## One meter at a time

London disagrees with the idea, common in theory and in this app's sources, that listeners hear two meters at once <Source book="london" where="Ch. 3, 5" />. In experiments with polyrhythms, listeners either fuse the parts into one composite pattern and find a meter for that, or follow one part and hear the other against it. So on any one hearing, a 3-against-4 is one meter with a figure set against it, never two meters. Two consequences:

- **"Metric dissonance" is fine as a metaphor** (he compares it to hearing a dissonant chord in a key, not two keys), but the listener is always in one meter, feeling the other layer as tension against it. Krebs's and Biamonte's terms on *Grouping and displacement* still work; just keep in mind that the listener holds one layer and feels the other.
- **When two strong layers really compete, the bar can collapse.** If the notated downbeats and a displaced set of accents are both strong, the combined pattern can accent every beat, and the sense of bar disappears. Sometimes that's what you want.

London's ambiguity scale is useful when you write: **unambiguous** (one reading even in a flat performance), **latently ambiguous** (performance decides it), **truly ambiguous** (different readings on different hearings), and **vague** (no beat can be found at all, e.g. very long notes with very short ones in between). His other local effects are worth naming too: hemiola, syncopation, and the **loud rest**, a downbeat that everyone expects and nobody plays.

## Many meters: why your band's feel is part of the meter

London's last chapter proposes the **many meters hypothesis** <Source book="london" where="Ch. 9" />. Real performances are never evenly timed, and the deviations are systematic: listeners expect the lengthenings and rushings typical of a style, and a perfectly even performance sounds wrong to them. So London argues that what we know as listeners isn't "4/4 at 120" but hundreds of specific timing patterns: Basie's swing and Goodman's, a march and a galop, one drummer's feel and another's. Each is, in his terms, a different meter.

For a band this means:

- **Your feel is part of the composition.** Where the snare sits, how long the long beat in 2-2-3 really is, how much the bass leans: these are part of the meter the audience learns, not ornaments on top of it.
- **Tempo changes need practice in steps.** Patterns don't scale evenly with tempo, because notes cross the thresholds above; that's why players practise at gradually increasing metronome settings.
- **Complex isn't the same as hard.** London's closing point: a rich meter is easy to follow when the texture states each level, and a simple one at an extreme tempo can be hard. Difficulty comes from how the figure sits against the meter, not from the meter alone.

<ListenerSide grade="strong">

The timing limits (the 100 ms floor, the 200–2000 ms beat range, the preference around 600 ms, the 2-second limit on hearing events as connected) are among the best-replicated findings in rhythm research, though every value shifts with context and listener. London's well-formedness rules and the "no polymeter" claim are theory built on that research, not direct findings. In the second edition (only the front matter is in your library) he revises the rules himself: in some music the fastest level can be uneven too, and in experiments the shortest playable pulse was much slower for uneven meters than for even ones.
