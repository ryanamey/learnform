## The question

A rock phrase ends on V, or on ♭VII, and the riff starts again. Is that a half cadence? The Classical theory of Course II and the rock writers in the library answer differently, and the choice of words changes what you expect to happen next.

## Caplin: the half cadence and its implication

In Classical style, a half cadence is a cadential progression ending on the dominant <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 2–3" />. It is the weakest of the three cadences, and in a period it is answered by a consequent that closes more strongly. IV–I is not a cadence in that style, and the final bars of a piece are usually codettas after the real cadence.

## Stephenson: open cadences promise nothing

Stephenson's chapter on rock cadences says a rock phrase can end on almost any chord, and that many songs end every phrase on V and never close <Source book="stephenson" where="Ch. 3" />. He advises against calling these half cadences: the term implies a full cadence to come, and in rock it usually doesn't come. Units typically begin on I and end on V, so the V–I happens *across* the seam and the resolving I is also a new start. A fully coordinated cadence is rare, and so it is an event when it happens.

## Moore: loops and periods

Moore places songs between open-ended repetition (a loop with no closing phrase) and period structure (an open phrase answered by a closed one) <Source book="moore12" where="Ch. 3, pp. 85–89" />. On his account an open ending is normal in a loop, and closed–open pairs are almost unknown.

## Lilja: function in the root progression

Lilja hears metal's commonest cadence, Aeolian VI–VII–I, as more plagal than authentic, and finds whole songs resting on Tonic–Subdominant–Tonic <Source book="lilja" where="Ch. 3.4, 6" />.

## Snyder: syntax is learned

Snyder calls cadences syntactic closure: relatively arbitrary patterns that close because a style has used them that way <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 5" />. On his account the same ending can imply continuation in one style and not in another.

## Hear the question

Play example 6 of *Natural and learned closure* (VI–VII–I) and stop it after the VII chord. Then play a loop of your own that ends on V or ♭VII. Does the open ending make you expect a closed answer, or just the next repeat?

## Where they agree

All of them treat an ending on a chord other than the tonic as open. All of them treat the strength of an ending as relative to the style.

## Where they differ

- **Does an open ending imply a closed one?** Caplin (Classical style): a half cadence is answered. Stephenson: in rock it usually isn't. Moore: in a period yes, in a loop no.
- **Plagal motion.** Caplin: not a cadence in Classical style. Lilja: the default function in classic metal.
- **Which style's syntax applies?** Snyder: whichever the listener has learned.

## Questions to decide

1. Will your band use "half cadence" for a phrase ending on V? If so, does it promise an answer?
2. For each of your main riffs: is it a loop (open, no answer expected) or the first half of a period?
3. Is a closed ending something your songs use rarely, as an event (Stephenson's view of rock), or regularly?
