## Gestures carry their own time

Some musical shapes announce their function wherever they appear. A closing gesture sounds like an ending even out of context; a build sounds like a transition; an explosion sounds like a climax. Kramer argues that this lets music create a second kind of time <Source book="kramer" where="Ch. 6" />:

- **Absolute time**: the order in which things actually happen.
- **Gestural time**: the order implied by what each gesture *is*: beginning, middle, ending.

Usually the two agree. When they don't, the result can be anything from a joke to the main drama of the piece. His evidence that gesture function is real out of context comes from composition seminars: students composed fragments, and everyone could hear which fragments could serve which functions and which couldn't, without any context.

<KeyIdea>An ending gesture placed early doesn't stop the music. It changes how everything after it is heard: as coming after an end.</KeyIdea>

## Ending as process and as product

An ending can be defined two ways <Source book="kramer" where="Ch. 6.2" />:

- **Process**: the point where the piece's tensions are resolved and its issues settled.
- **Product** (profile): a gesture we recognize by convention as an ending shape.

Most endings need both. When the profile appears without the process, audiences applaud in the wrong place (Kramer's example: a false ending in Tchaikovsky's Fifth). When the process completes without a convincing profile, the piece just stops.

He also describes how stock endings work. After the goal is reached, it must be **held** long enough to dissipate the momentum that brought the music there, and the material is **liquidated**: simplified step by step from what's specific to this piece toward the generic, so nothing implies a future (Beethoven's Fifth ends this way). And an ending has to announce itself in time; if the listener needs the silence afterwards to know it's over, the ending failed. (Module 1's *sentence and liquidation* and Module 4's *Returning and ending* cover the craft side.)

## Beginnings: more procedures, fewer formulas

The reverse holds for beginnings: there are many ways to begin, but few stock beginning gestures. Some openings start "in the middle" — off the home chord, in the middle of establishing it. A piece can even start with a gesture that sounds like the **close of an introduction** (Kramer cites Beethoven's *Prometheus* overture), or start, stop, and **go back to the slow introduction it forgot** (Berlioz's *Le Corsaire*): two beginnings.

## Witty and serious misplacement

Kramer's examples run from wit to profundity:

- **Too many endings**: Haydn ends a quartet with at least five closing formulas in a row, the last one built from an opening figure, so we only know it's over when nothing follows.
- **Withheld arrival**: Mozart's *Jupiter* Symphony establishes strong home-key downbeats in the first movement, then avoids them for nearly three movements (even building a trio out of an ending cadence used as an opening), so their return in the finale feels like triumph. The tension spans whole movements.
- **An ending at the start**: Haydn and Beethoven open movements with archetypal closing gestures. The disorientation becomes a force that drives the movement.

## Beethoven's three endings

Kramer's main case is the first movement of Beethoven's String Quartet Op. 135 <Source book="kramer" where="Ch. 5, 6.4" />. Ten bars in, after a struggle, comes a cadence so final it has the shape of the movement's ending. Kramer hears it as exactly that: in gestural time, the movement **ends at bar 10**, and then (in absolute time) we learn the content of the movement that has just "ended." The same closing gesture returns twice more, at the recapitulation and at the real end. Each time we know more about what is being ended, and the gesture itself carries less new information.

Two more multiply-directed features in the same movement:

- The central climax is **prepared by three separate upbeat passages**, one directly before it, one far earlier and one far later. In gestural time, all three lead to it.
- Two different continuities run through the exposition, intertwined, each heading to its own goal.

His caution is useful for composing: an "unscrambled" version of the movement would be duller. Reordering a simple linear prototype rarely makes a good piece. What matters is **how** the misplacements are paced.

## A problem stated at the start

Kramer's reading of the same Beethoven movement shows where its forward drive comes from, since it isn't only harmony <Source book="kramer" where="Ch. 5" />. Three things are "wrong" at the start, and the movement spends its length putting them right:

- **Texture.** The opening is fragmented: blocks, silences, no transitions. By the development the motives are combined in counterpoint, and in the recapitulation the block boundaries blur into continuity.
- **A gesture in the wrong place.** The too-early final cadence at bar 10 doesn't make sense where it is; later events justify it, and it returns at the false recapitulation, the reprise and the coda.
- **Groupings.** Irregular hypermeasures (an implied 5/8 cross-pattern, 7-bar units) gradually settle into regular 4-bar units.

The discontinuous opening is heard, in retrospect, as the cause of what follows. For a riff band this is a usable plan: **state a problem in the first minute** (fragments that won't join up, a grouping that won't sit in the meter, an ending gesture that comes too soon) and make the song the process of solving it. The solution then needs no announcement; the listener has been waiting for it since the start.

## Past–present–future vs. earlier–later

Kramer uses a philosopher's distinction to say why this works. Earlier/later is fixed by the order of events. Past/present/future can be set by gesture: an ending gesture is the music's *future* even when it's heard first. Music can separate the two, which is why a piece can feel like it's remembering something you haven't heard yet <Source book="kramer" where="Ch. 6.5" />.

<ListenerSide grade="weak">

This is interpretation, not experiment. The one empirical claim (that gesture functions are recognizable out of context) rests on Kramer's informal seminar experience. It's consistent with Snyder's *subjective set* (style-learned expectations, Module 1) and with Module 3's cues: listeners do know an ending profile when they hear one. Whether a rock audience hears a mid-song "ending" as an ending depends on how conventional the gesture is in the genre.

## Practice

<Exercise title="Start with the ending">

1. Write a closing gesture for a song: a broadening, descending figure that lands on a long final chord.
2. Put it at the very start, then follow it with the song's opening riff.
3. Bring it back twice more: once halfway through and once at the end, changing one or two details each time so the last is the most final.
4. Listen to the whole. Does the opening ending change how the riff sounds?

</Exercise>

Pick a song you know with a strong ending. Write down the ending gesture as process (what gets resolved) and as product (what the gesture is). Then imagine the gesture at the start of the song. What would the rest of the song have to do to justify it?
