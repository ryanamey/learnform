## The question

When does a phrase or a section *end*? Is it a harmonic formula, a coordination of several dimensions, or a drop in intensity that memory treats as a boundary? The answer decides what you count as an ending in music without Classical cadences, and what you do when the band simply stops.

## Caplin: a harmonic, syntactic event

In Classical style a cadence is a cadential progression arriving on V or on I, with a falling melody; there are three kinds (perfect authentic, imperfect authentic, half) <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 2–3" />. An **end** is not a **stop**: the music can keep moving through a cadence, and it can stop where nothing has ended. Melodic and textural markers are less reliable than cadences for finding boundaries, and when harmony and texture disagree, harmony prevails. He writes about one style and says so.

## Schoenberg: harmony plus liquidation

A cadence delimits harmonically and liquidates what demands continuation <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. VII–VIII" />. His list of cadence types is longer than Caplin's (Phrygian and plagal included).

## Belkin: coordination of dimensions

Belkin avoids the word cadence because its definition isn't agreed, and speaks of **punctuation** <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 5" />. Harmony alone never makes one, otherwise every V–I would; at least two dimensions must coordinate, and rhythm is almost always one of them. Outside tonality, punctuation comes from reducing tension. Silence works as punctuation only after something incomplete; after something complete it just raises the question whether the piece is over.

## Snyder: natural and syntactic closure

Snyder separates **natural** closure (falling intensity, longer durations, pauses, repetition, return to a central pitch) from **syntactic** closure (learned, style-specific formulas such as cadences) <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 5" />. He treats only the natural kind. Patterns with no organised motion don't close; they simply stop. For sections, he points to change in several parameters at once rather than to a cadence <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 13" />.

## Hear the disagreement

Listen again to the six closings in *Natural and learned closure*, especially these two:

- **1 · It just stops.** Caplin: a stop, not an end. Snyder: an interrupted pattern stops without closure. Belkin: silence after something incomplete creates suspense, so it can work as a powerful *internal* punctuation. Here the three agree on what it isn't, and differ on what it's good for.
- **5 · Thinning out.** No harmonic formula at all. Caplin's theory has no cadence to point to. Belkin: texture thinning plus rhythmic lengthening is a two-dimension punctuation (he names the pop fade-out). Snyder: natural closure by falling density.

## Where they agree

Endings are graded and hierarchical. A stop is not automatically an ending. Melodies that fall tend to close.

## Where they differ

- **What is necessary?** Caplin: a cadential progression. Belkin: two coordinated dimensions, with harmony neither necessary nor enough. Snyder: falling intensity or a learned formula.
- **What decides when cues conflict?** Caplin: harmony. Belkin: the number of dimensions agreeing. Snyder: the number of parameters changing.
- **Scope.** Caplin's claims are about Classical style; Belkin's and Snyder's are meant to hold across styles.

## Questions to decide

1. In your band's music, what has to happen for you to call something an ending? Write it as a rule you could check.
2. When the band stops dead before a new section, is that, for you, an ending, a stop, or punctuation? Does it depend on what came before?
3. Do you want one word for all of these (Belkin's punctuation), or separate words for harmonic cadences and other closings?
