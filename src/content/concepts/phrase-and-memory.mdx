## Why music comes in phrases

A phrase is a complete small thought, roughly what can be sung in one breath. Schoenberg compares it to a unit ending with something like a comma <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. II" />, and Belkin to a clause in a sentence <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 2" />. Both explain *why* phrases exist in the same way: our attention span is short, and ideas that are clearly bounded are easier to remember and compare.

Snyder gives the mechanism <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 3–4" />:

- **Short-term memory holds 3–5 seconds** of material on average. With simple material it can stretch to 10–12 seconds; novel, complex material shortens it. Without rehearsal or repetition it fades.
- It holds a **limited number of different elements**: the famous 7 ± 2 with rehearsal, more like 3–4 without. Repeated elements don't count twice, so a pattern built from a few elements can contain up to about 25 events and still fit. Elements can be chunks, and chunks can contain chunks.
- The **focus of awareness** is smaller still: about three items at most, usually one. Everything else in the present is held just outside it.
- The **phrase is the largest unit you can hold whole** in the present. Anything longer, such as sections or songs, has to be *reconstructed* from long-term memory, which keeps a sketch rather than the details.

<KeyIdea>A phrase is the biggest thing a listener can hear all at once. Everything larger is remembered, not heard, and memory simplifies.</KeyIdea>

This has a direct consequence for prog. Long odd-meter cycles, phrases over 8–10 seconds, and constantly changing material all push past that window. That can be a deliberate effect (disorientation, trance), but past that point the listener is no longer hearing your structure. They're reconstructing it, and only the most distinctive features survive the trip.

## Grouping: how boundaries get drawn

Before memory gets to work, perception has already cut the sound into groups. Snyder lists the main forces, which he calls *primitive* grouping because they work automatically:

- **Proximity in time.** A slightly longer gap starts a new group. This is the strongest force.
- **Similarity.** Notes alike in register, timbre, loudness or articulation group together.
- **Change in intensity.** An accent tends to mark the start of a group.

When these forces agree, grouping is clear. When they **conflict**, listeners hear it differently, or hear it flip back and forth. That ambiguity is a huge resource.

Snyder adds three higher-level forces <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 3" />:

- **Intensification.** When several boundary forces coincide (a long note *and* a pause *and* a change of register), the boundary moves up a level. This is what turns a group into a phrase, and a phrase end into a section end. It's the perceptual side of Belkin's punctuation scale (Module 4).
- **Parallelism.** Once a pattern is known, later material is grouped so that it matches the earlier pattern, even against the local cues. Repetition of a shape teaches the listener where to cut.
- **A return to the tonal centre** can mark a boundary, because long-term memory knows that pitch is home.

He also separates two kinds of learned expectation: **objective set**, what this piece has taught you so far (its themes, its riff), and **subjective set**, what the style has taught you across many pieces (that a chorus is coming, that a breakdown drops to half time). Forms and genre conventions work through the second; your own motives work through the first.

Pay attention to the last scenario. Gaps every four notes and accents every three, over the same pitches, give two valid ways to hear one stream. A large part of the sound of Tool, Meshuggah and *Discipline*-era King Crimson comes from deliberately building in this kind of conflict and making you live inside it. Module 2 builds a whole lab around it.

## Chunking: repetition reduces load

Any repetition works as a kind of automatic rehearsal: it keeps the recent past active and lowers the memory load, which is why music with many levels of repetition can carry so much information <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 4" />. And a striking, unexpected event gets *replayed* in the mind on its own — it's literally remarkable — which makes it more likely to be remembered later.

## Inside the phrase

Belkin describes a phrase as a miniature piece <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 2" />:

1. **Beginning:** one motive, sometimes two. More than about three different ideas in one phrase blurs the focus.
2. **Middle:** some incremental change, so the intensity doesn't stay flat. Peaks rise, harmonic rhythm speeds up, the motive fragments, dissonance increases. The local peak usually happens once.
3. **End:** punctuation, of varying strength. Outside tonal cadences, rhythm and texture can do it: notes slow down, a long note, a gap, a drop in register.

He also notes that later in a piece, phrases can be more fragmentary and unstable on purpose. Fragments that build towards something more stable make the arrival feel like a resolution. Beginnings want clarity; development can afford instability.

## How a phrase ends, and how a line moves

Schoenberg's description of the phrase is about its edges <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. II, IV" />. Its ending is marked by **rhythmic reduction** and **melodic relaxation**: a drop in pitch, smaller intervals, fewer notes. Its length depends on tempo, from half a bar in a very slow piece to eight bars or more in a very fast one, and it doesn't care about barlines. He also asks for variety in the endings themselves: too many strong-beat endings, or too many weak-beat ones, makes the phrasing monotonous <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. V" />.

Inside the phrase, a good line moves **in waves**. The high point is approached through lesser high points interrupted by recessions; a leap is usually balanced by steps in the other direction; and a line seldom goes far in one direction <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. IV, XI" />. His self-check adds two warnings: watch the **highest note** (the climax normally comes toward the end, and repeating or exceeding it needs caution), and beware a compass that's too narrow <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. XII" />.

## Odd lengths are not the problem

For a band that writes in 7 and 11, Schoenberg's chapter on irregular construction is reassuring and demanding at once <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. XIV" />. Odd lengths come from three causes: the length of the basic units, their number, or a mix of different lengths. Brahms writes whole sections in three-bar phrases; a six-bar period can combine bars of 3/4 and 2/4 and still be symmetrical at another level; Beethoven's Ninth Symphony scherzo switches between three-bar and four-bar groupings. Some composers change meter constantly so that the barlines follow the phrases. Masters, he says, use these irregularities freely when the idea demands it, and they add fluency and spontaneity. "But they are neither arbitrary nor casual": an odd length should have a reason you could name.

## Patterns inside patterns

Moore points out two things rock songs do with simple phrase shapes <Source book="moore12" where="Ch. 3, pp. 57–60" />.

- **Embedded patterns.** A small shape is replayed at a larger scale. The Beatles' "A Hard Day's Night" has a 12-bar verse made of three 4-bar groups (A A B); the song then runs verse, verse, middle eight, verse, which is the same A A B A shape one level up. Bowie's "Life on Mars?" builds a verse from six 4-bar groups with the same rhythm, each starting one note higher, so the whole verse becomes a slow-motion rising scale, broken only at the end to set up the next section. The listener doesn't need to notice the embedding for it to make the song feel coherent.
- **Phrases that cut across the layout.** Procol Harum's "A Whiter Shade of Pale" has a 16-bar verse in four regular 4-bar groups, but the melody uses only three phrases arranged a+b, c+a, b+c, a′+b′, so the tune's units never line up with the four groups. The steady descending bass hides the mismatch. Moore calls the result a comfortable, almost transparent disorientation.

His conclusion fits this whole module: because simple, regular norms are so easy to perceive, they allow **more subtlety, not less**. A plain four-square grid is what makes a melody running against it audible. For a band that writes complex riffs, the reverse also holds: a melody that cuts across a complex grid needs the grid to be very clear.

This is the best-supported idea in Module 1. Snyder's synthesis of memory research puts short-term memory at a few seconds and a handful of chunks; phrases and riff cycles that fit that window can be heard whole and compared. Longer units are grasped only through their boundaries and their salient moments. Belkin and Schoenberg reached the same rule from practice.

## Practice

<Exercise title="Same notes, three groupings">

1. Write a riff of 12–16 even notes.
2. Make three versions using only the grouping forces: (a) gaps, (b) accents, (c) register or picking changes. Keep pitches and order identical.
3. Make a fourth version where two forces conflict.
4. Record all four over the same drum loop. Which one would you build a section on?

</Exercise>
