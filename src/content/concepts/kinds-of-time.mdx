## Two forces in every piece

Kramer's book rests on one distinction <Source book="kramer" where="Ch. 2" />:

- **Linearity**: something in the music is determined by what came **earlier**. Each event implies later events; later events are consequences. It's the music of expectation, progression, becoming.
- **Nonlinearity**: something is determined by a **principle governing the whole piece or section**. It's there from the start and doesn't grow: the instrumentation, a constant texture, a groove that never changes, the proportions of the sections. You learn it rather than follow it.

Every piece has both, in different amounts and **on different levels**. A song can be linear on the surface (riffs leading to riffs) and nonlinear in its overall design (blocks in an order that could be different), or the reverse. Kramer stresses that neither force is the same as continuity or discontinuity: a surprise is linear (it only works because of what it breaks), and a link can span minutes.

<KeyIdea>Ask of every passage: is this happening because of what came before, or because of what this piece always is? Most of the interesting music happens where the two answers conflict.</KeyIdea>

## Five kinds of time

From the mix of those two forces Kramer derives five ways music can shape time <Source book="kramer" where="Ch. 2, 6, 8, 12" />. He insists they're listening suggestions, not boxes: they overlap, and one piece can use several.

| Kind | What it feels like | How it's made |
|---|---|---|
| **Goal-directed linear** | You know where it's going and wait for it | Clear implications and a destination (in tonal music, the return home); suspense is about the route and the timing |
| **Nondirected linear** | Constant motion, but you can't predict the goal | Each event leads to the next; no destination is defined in advance |
| **Multiply-directed** | Strong direction, scrambled order | Processes interrupted and completed elsewhere; recognizable gestures (endings, climaxes, transitions) placed in the "wrong" place |
| **Moment time** | A mosaic of self-contained blocks | Static or self-completing sections, cut without transitions; the piece starts rather than begins and stops rather than ends |
| **Vertical time** | One extended present | No phrase hierarchy, no build, no ending; the piece defines its world early and never leaves it |

## How the in-between kinds arise

Kramer gives a neat way to tell the middle three apart when a directed piece starts breaking up <Source book="kramer" where="Ch. 2.14" />:

- If what each section implies is frustrated next but **fulfilled somewhere else**, the time is **multiply-directed**.
- If **nothing** in a later section follows from anything earlier, it's **moment time**.
- If each section leads to the next but the larger implications never pay off, it's **nondirected linear**.

He also notes how fragile non-tonal direction is: put a few big discontinuities into atonal music and it turns into moment time almost by itself. The same passage can be heard as continuous in one context and as a string of moments after the listener has heard it fragmented earlier. Context decides.

## Where heavy, riff-based music sits

None of Kramer's examples are rock, so this is application, not his claim. Riff music has strong **nonlinear** forces built in: the tuning, the guitar sound, the drummer's vocabulary, the fact that a riff repeats. Its **linearity** comes from builds, cues, metric dissonance resolving, the climax plan (Modules 2–3). Moving between the five kinds is a form decision you can make deliberately:

- A **build to a terminal climax** is goal-directed.
- A long section of **riffs that keep transforming without arriving** is nondirected.
- A **riff cut off and resumed later**, or an **ending gesture placed in the middle**, is multiply-directed (next page).
- A song of **contrasting blocks with hard cuts** and no returns that feel prepared is close to moment time (page after next).
- A **long drone or trance groove** with no phrase hierarchy is vertical (Module 5, *Trance*).

The levels matter most. Kramer's model case is Stravinsky's *Symphonies of Wind Instruments*: moment time on the middle level (blocks), linear voice-leading underneath, and a mix on the surface. The strange tension of the piece comes from those levels disagreeing.

<ListenerSide grade="moderate">

Kramer's categories are an analyst's and composer's framework, not experimental findings; he says so repeatedly. The listener side he does argue for: the same music can be heard in different temporal modes depending on familiarity and style knowledge (a piece he first heard as directionless became linear once learned; a random piece memorized from a recording started to sound implied). Snyder's *linearity* and *memory strategies* (Module 4, *How a form is remembered*) give the memory mechanism behind it.

</ListenerSide>

## Practice

<Exercise title="One riff, five times">

1. Take one riff from your bank.
2. Sketch five 30-second treatments of it, one per kind of time, using the lab as a model.
3. For each, write one sentence: what is determined by what came before, and what is determined by the whole?

</Exercise>

<ApplyIt>

Map one long song you know well in three rows: the surface (riff to riff), the sections, and the whole song. In each row, write which kind of time dominates. Mark any row where the answer differs from the others: that's where the song's tension lives.

</ApplyIt>
