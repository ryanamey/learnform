## The question

Should a piece grow from one motive? Is coherence something that comes from derivation, from function, or from what the listener remembers? The four writers of Course I give different answers. This page sets them side by side. It doesn't decide.

## Schoenberg: the motive as germ

For Schoenberg the motive is the **germ** of a piece. It is both its smallest common multiple (it contains elements of every later figure) and its greatest common factor (it is contained in every later figure). Homophonic music is a style of **developing variation**: a succession of motive-forms derived from the basic motive <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. III" />. Logic comes from common content, rhythmic similarity and coherent harmony <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. IV" />. His closing warning is against form built by laying bricks together or pouring material into ready-made moulds <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. XX, Conclusion" />.

He is not absolute about it. Remotely related motive-forms may endanger comprehensibility, and the treatment of the motive matters more than its primary form: "everything depends on its treatment and development".

## Belkin: a useful method, a debatable generalization

Belkin uses derivation constantly: double motives reordered, motives wandering through remote forms and returning, an ornamental variant held for a whole section <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 1" />. But in a note to his chapter on contrast he calls Schoenberg's belief that all ideas derive from one basic motive very debatable as a generalization <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 11, note" />. Contrast, on his account, can also be made by changing several dimensions substantially at once, and what matters is continuity the listener can follow.

## Caplin: function doesn't depend on origin

Caplin's theory asks what a passage *does* (begin, continue, end), not where its material came from. His clearest statement is about fragmentation: it concerns only the length of the units, whether their contents derive from the basic idea or are new <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 2" />. A continuation can bring new material and still be a continuation; a contrasting idea is simply "not a repetition" of the basic idea. He also separates **characteristic** material (typical of beginnings) from **conventional** material (scales, arpeggios, cadential formulas), which is not derived from anything in particular.

## Snyder: what the listener can hold

Snyder doesn't discuss compositional derivation. He describes what makes patterns sound related: keep the rhythmic contour and change the pitch or timbre, and the ear hears a family <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 12" />; close in time, even loose variants link; far apart, only close ones do <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 3" />. A theme meant to be transformed is usually no longer than a phrase, so that it fits short-term memory. On this account a derivation the listener can't remember is not heard as one, whatever the score shows.

## Where they agree

All four accept that repetition is needed for coherence and that unvaried repetition alone becomes monotonous. All four, in their own terms, describe shortening of units toward a goal. They differ on how much of a piece's coherence should come from one source.

## Questions to decide

1. Is monomotivic derivation, for your band, a **rule**, a **method among others**, or **irrelevant** if the functions and the listener's memory are taken care of?
2. Caplin's continuation may use new material. When you build energy in a riff section, do you want it to come from your basic motive, from new material, or from either?
3. Snyder's test: would a derivation you are proud of be audible to someone hearing the song twice? If not, does it still matter to you?
4. Belkin's alternative source of contrast is to change several dimensions at once. Name one section of yours where contrast comes from derivation and one where it comes from changing dimensions.

Write your decision in the notes below. It can change later; the point is to know which position you are composing from.
