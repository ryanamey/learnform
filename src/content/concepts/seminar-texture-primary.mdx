## The question

Is texture (with timbre and dynamics) a structural element of music, able to carry form by itself? Or is it secondary: colour that underlines what pitch, rhythm and harmony are doing? Your answer decides where the band starts when it designs a section: from riffs and chords, or from sound, layers and density.

Course I already met one version of this question in *Seminar: Which dimensions carry a motive's identity?*, about motives. Here it is about form.

## Meyer, as Snyder and Moore report him: secondary means statistical

Leonard Meyer divided musical parameters into **primary** (pitch, rhythm, harmony) and **secondary** (dynamics, timbre, texture, tempo, rate of activity). Both Snyder and Moore report the distinction (Meyer's own book was not read for this course) <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 13" /> <Source book="analysing-rock" where="whole article" />. Primary parameters can form **syntax**: patterns built from distinct categories that can be recognised again and that create specific expectations, like a cadence. Secondary ones can only work "statistically", as more or less.

## Snyder: secondary is not less important

Snyder adopts Meyer's terms but warns against reading "secondary" as "minor" <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 13" />:

- Secondary parameters can't form recognisable melody-like patterns: you can't re-identify a loudness or a tempo after other music has intervened. So they work in **increasing and decreasing progressions** and **large contrasts**, and they reinforce primary patterns.
- Large changes in loudness, timbre and number of instruments **stand out most in first hearings**, especially for less experienced listeners.
- But a single change in a secondary parameter (only louder, only a new tempo) is usually heard as articulation *inside* a section; a change in the primary pattern is often stronger.
- New secondary parameters can always be invented: anything that can be "more" or "less".

## Belkin: "primary" means strongest for contrast

Belkin uses the same word for the opposite group <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 4" />. He calls **tempo, timbre, register and dynamics** the *primary dimensions*, because they make the strongest contrasts: refinements of harmony alone rarely make a contrast dramatic. The clash is partly in the words. For Snyder, "primary" means able to form syntax; for Belkin, it means perceptually strongest. Both agree that these dimensions drive large contrasts.

## LaRue: secondary for motion, primary for joints

LaRue splits the question <Source book="larue" where="Ch. 2" />:

- For **Movement**, Sound usually seems secondary: it underlines but doesn't direct the motion of melody, harmony and rhythm. He allows that large-scale alternations of colour may affect our deepest sense of motion, "responses which we often feel but can rarely explain".
- For **Shape**, Sound is primary: its changes are the most easily observed articulations, and when other elements are ambiguous, a change of colour or dynamics decides where the joint falls. Over long spans, a change of scoring every sixteen bars can make a paragraph structure audible.

## Caplin: harmony decides closure

Caplin keeps harmony central <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 3" />. A cadence's **syntactical** strength comes from its harmonic type; loudness, texture and metric position give only **rhetorical** impact. A huge texture can mark a weak ending. Texture can tell the listener where a boundary is, but for Caplin it can't make a passage close.

## Moore: rock analysis should start from texture

Moore argues the opposite for rock <Source book="analysing-rock" where="whole article" /> <Source book="moore12" where="Ch. 2" />. The sound of a track is usually what draws listeners first; students who can't follow themes can follow form by its texture changes; the filler layer is how listeners identify a style. He begins his analyses with layers and the sound-box. But he is candid about the objection: he cites Meyer's distinction and the view (from David Butler) that pitch and duration are the pattern-forming qualities in all cultures, and admits that his call for texture-based analysis is not yet backed by psychological or ethnographic research.

## Lavengood and Harrison: timbre as structure, timbre as distraction

**Lavengood** treats timbre as structural in 1980s pop: core sounds blend, novelty sounds stand out, and the novelty layer follows the form, taking intros, interludes and outros <Source book="lavengood20" where="whole article" />.

**Harrison**, on the Beach Boys' *Smiley Smile*, describes a case where timbre and reverb take over the listener's attention: with little harmonic or formal development, focus moves to instrumentation, and little attention is left for form <Source book="covach" where="Ch. 2 (Harrison)" />. Texture can be so absorbing that it replaces form rather than carrying it.

## Where they agree

Everyone agrees that changes of timbre, texture and dynamics are easy to hear, that they mark large contrasts and boundaries, and that they are strongest when they coincide with changes in pitch and rhythm.

## Where they differ

- **Can texture carry form by itself?** LaRue: it can make joints and even long units audible. Moore and Lavengood: in rock, largely yes. Caplin: it can mark boundaries but not close. Snyder: a single secondary change is articulation within a section, not a new section.
- **What does "primary" mean?** Able to form syntax (Meyer, Snyder) or strongest for contrast (Belkin).
- **Is attention to texture good for form?** A tool for following it (Moore's students) or a rival to it (Harrison).
