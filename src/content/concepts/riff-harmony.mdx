## A riff is harmony, even with two notes

Esa Lilja's dissertation on classic heavy metal harmony (Sabbath, Purple, Zeppelin, Priest, Maiden, Dio, 1968–1985) starts from a simple claim: in metal, **any two different notes are a chord**, and sometimes one note is <Source book="lilja" where="Ch. 3.1" />. A power chord is a chord. A riff in parallel fourths is a harmonised melody. A single low E under a Locrian riff (Sabbath's "Symptom of the Universe") is heard as a tonic chord. The bass usually decides what the harmony is: in Maiden's "The Trooper" the guitars play thirds and fourths, and the bass note underneath tells you whether it's I or VI.

Three points from Lilja's analysis:

- **Read the lowest note as the root.** Lilja finds that metal builds chords from the bass upward, the way Renaissance and figured-bass musicians did, not as inversions of stacked thirds <Source book="lilja" where="Ch. 4.4" />. A C under C–A♭ is still a C chord with a sixth; put A♭ in the bass and it becomes a VI chord. Same guitar shape, different harmony.
- **Name chords by mode, not by key.** Lilja uses Allan Moore's modal labels: the degree number on the mode in use, plus a prefix when a chord is borrowed from another mode (dorIV, phrII, locV, hmV for harmonic minor) <Source book="lilja" where="Ch. 1, 3.2" />. In E Aeolian, C–D–E is VI–VII–I, not "♭VI–♭VII–i".
- **Two guitar shapes the analysis has to name.** The **minor-sixth dyad** (C with A♭ above, written I(5)6) is a neighbour or passing chord between power chords ("Metal Heart", "Highway to Hell", "The Sentinel"). The **fourth dyad** is either an inverted power chord, where the top note is the root ("Smoke on the Water"), or a suspension that wants to fall to a third (the verse of "Crazy Train"). A fourth on the strong beat sounds like a suspension; a fourth that moves in parallel with the riff melts into the melody.

## The modes metal uses

Lilja's table of metal modes: Ionian, Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian, Aeolian, Locrian, harmonic minor, melodic minor and Hungarian minor <Source book="lilja" where="Ch. 1 Table 1.1; Ch. 5.2" />. Aeolian and Dorian are the most common, and thrash leans on Phrygian and Locrian. Four uses of mode matter for form:

- **Different modes in different sections.** Maiden's "Murders in the Rue Morgue" has a Mixolydian verse and a Dorian refrain on the same tonic, like a major/minor switch.
- **Different modes at the same time.** In "War Pigs" the guitar riff is E Aeolian (minor third) while the voice sings E Mixolydian (major third). In "Symptom of the Universe" Lilja hears the riff in E Locrian and the vocal in A Phrygian. The clash between riff and voice is part of the sound, not a mistake.
- **Pseudo-oriental modes.** Hijaz (Phrygian with a major third) and Hungarian minor turn up in Rainbow, Dio and Maiden as colour ("Gates of Babylon", "Powerslave"), usually mixed with ordinary Phrygian.
- **The blues third.** A minor third sung over a major or power chord is a blues habit that runs from Robert Johnson through Hendrix's 7♯9 to "Paranoid". Lilja's point is that a power chord isn't neutral: under distortion it has a faint major third in its sound (see *Heaviness*), so a minor melody over it gives the same mixed third as the blues.

<KeyIdea>Lilja's finding: in classic metal, the riff and the vocal can sit in different modes at the same time, and the clash is part of the sound.</KeyIdea>

## Functions without voice leading

Classical harmony explains chord function through voice leading. Lilja argues that function survives without it <Source book="lilja" where="Ch. 3.4" />. Play an authentic and a plagal cadence as triads, as power chords and then as bare root notes, and they still sound like the same cadences. In metal, harmonic function rides on the **root progression**.

He uses three functions after Riemann and Daniel Harrison: **Tonic** (T), **Subdominant** (S) and **Dominant** (D), with the Subdominant as an equal partner, not a lead-in to the Dominant. Each has strong and weak forms, decided by the degree:

| Function | Strong | Weak |
|---|---|---|
| Tonic | I in any form (power chord, major, minor) | III (t) |
| Subdominant | IV as a power chord, Aeolian IV, **Aeolian VI** (S) | Dorian IV, Dorian VI (s) |
| Dominant | V as a power chord, Ionian V, Ionian VII (D) | Aeolian V, **Aeolian VII** (d) |

The reasoning: the minor sixth degree pulls hardest towards the fifth, so ♭VI is a strong Subdominant; the major seventh pulls hardest towards the tonic, so the Aeolian ♭VII is only a weak Dominant. That changes how the most common metal cadence reads:

- **Aeolian VI–VII–I** (the Aeolian cadence, Maiden's trademark) is S – d – T: strong Subdominant, weak Dominant. Lilja hears it as **more plagal than authentic**, even though the Dominant comes last.
- **The blues cadence V–IV–I** is plagal too: the Subdominant comes last before the Tonic.
- **III–VII–I** ("Paranoid") reads III as a secondary Subdominant leading to VII.
- **II rarely has a function.** In metal it's almost always a passing chord between I and III or a chromatic step down to I ("War Pigs"), not the II of II–V–I.

## Grounds: the patterns metal repeats

Lilja compares metal's repeated chord patterns to the **grounds** of Renaissance and Baroque music, patterns that a whole section is built over <Source book="lilja" where="Ch. 5.4" />. The ones worth knowing by name:

- **The Aeolian pendulum** (VI–VII–I and its rocking back and forth), which works in metal the way IV–V–I works in classical music.
- **The "Rising Sun" pattern**, I–III–IV–VI with a Dorian (major) IV (from "The House of the Rising Sun"), often with VII added. Its roots fit a pentatonic scale, and Lilja suggests it's popular because it skips the Aeolian degrees that would be minor or diminished triads (V and II).
- **The "Saints" line**, 8–♭7–♮6–♭6–5, as a top line or a bass line ("White Room", "Snowblind"); the ♮6 to ♭6 step is the Romantic major-then-minor Subdominant.
- **The twelve-bar as three phrases**: a Tonic phrase, a Subdominant phrase and a Dominant phrase, each of which metal bands stretch, double or re-harmonise ("Hells Bells", "Mistreated").

He also notes Middleton's observation that riff-based structures often have **little sense of leaving home and returning**: time goes round instead of forward. Some riffs are open loops ("Egypt", "Gates of Babylon"); others close on the tonic in miniature ("Paranoid", "Smoke on the Water").

## From riff to song: plagal frameworks

The last chapter scales the functions up <Source book="lilja" where="Ch. 6" />. Schenkerian theory expects a piece to rest on I–V–I. Lilja finds that the classic metal songs he analyses rest on **Tonic–Subdominant–Tonic** instead, at every level:

- **"Smoke on the Water"**: the riff is T–S–T over four bars, and the whole song is too: riff and verse on the Tonic, chorus on the Subdominant (with the Phrygian II as its strong form), riff again.
- **"Heaven and Hell"**: the whole song is a prolonged T–S–T–S–T. The bridges modulate to A Aeolian, which is the Subdominant region of E.
- **"Aces High"**: tonal centres keep shifting, linked by Aeolian cadences. Lilja maps the centres up to four levels deep and admits that the deepest levels may not be heard at all.

Two more findings about melody and material:

- **Melodies rarely close 2̂–1̂.** They end on the minor third, on 5̂, or avoid the tonic altogether ("War Pigs"). The final note of a section (the *finalis*) is often not the tonic.
- **One riff can carry a whole song.** In "Heaven and Hell" most of the material comes from the opening riff: squeezed into half the length over a tonic drone (diminution), stretched so each chord lasts two bars at the end (augmentation), and handed from the guitar to the bass. Lilja notes that **no section is repeated without some change**.

<KeyIdea>Lilja: the classic metal songs he analyses rest on Tonic–Subdominant–Tonic at every level, where the Classical theory he compares them with expects I–V–I.</KeyIdea>

## Is a plagal close a cadence? The writers disagree

<Positions title="Plagal motion: who counts it as a cadence">
  <Position book="caplin" where="Ch. 2">The plagal cadence is a myth in Classical style: IV–I is not a genuine cadence there, but usually a codetta after the real cadence. Only three cadence types: perfect authentic, imperfect authentic, half.</Position>
  <Position book="schoenberg" where="Ch. VIII">Lists the plagal cadence among the cadence types.</Position>
  <Position book="lilja" where="Ch. 3.4, 6">In metal, function rides on the root progression; the Aeolian VI–VII–I and the blues V–IV–I read as plagal, and whole songs rest on T–S–T.</Position>
  <Position book="belkin" where="Ch. 5">Prefers "punctuation" to "cadence" because the term isn't agreed; harmony alone never makes one, at least two dimensions must coordinate.</Position>
</Positions>

The two kinds of claim are different: Caplin's is about Classical style, Lilja's about classic metal. Whether a plagal close *ends* a section in your music is a question about the style you are writing in. Course II returns to it.

<Exercise title="Name the functions in one of your riffs">

1. Pick one of your riffs. Write its root notes and decide the mode (look at the third, sixth and seventh degrees the riff actually uses).
2. Label each root with its degree (VI, VII, I…) and its function and strength from the table: S, s, D, d, T, t.
3. Say whether the riff's cadence is plagal or authentic, and whether the riff closes on the tonic or loops open.
4. Write the vocal line's mode separately. Agreement or clash? Mark it on the form plan's notes.

</Exercise>

<Exercise title="One riff, a whole song">

Take one riff and write four versions without adding new material, as "Heaven and Hell" does:

1. **Diminution**: fit the four-bar riff into two bars, keeping its outline, over a drone on the tonic.
2. **Augmentation**: give each chord two bars, and let the bass carry the riff's line while the guitar holds.
3. **A Subdominant-region version**: the riff's contour moved to IV or ♭VI as a new tonal centre, for a bridge.
4. **The return**: the original, with one change (register, voicing, or a missing chord).

Put them in a form plan in the order T – T′ – S – T″ and check whether the song still sounds like one piece.

</Exercise>
