## Trance is invited, not caused

The research converges on one point (see *Listener psychology sources*): **music facilitates trance; it doesn't cause it.** Context, intention, learned listening habits and each listener's capacity for absorption do most of the work. Fachner's review calls the mechanism unsolved. Rouget's comparative study rejects the idea that drumming triggers trance physiologically <Source book="rouget" where="ch. 4" />, and the old claim that drum tempos drive the brain into "theta" states has never been replicated. Hove's trance study checked it directly and found no such effect <Source book="hove" where="EEG result" />.

What music *can* do is make absorption easier for willing listeners: repetition that fades into the background (Hove), gradual audible change (Reich), anticipation (Salimpoor), and a ritual frame that says "this is the kind of listening to do now".

## Rouget: what music does and doesn't do

Gilbert Rouget's *Music and Trance* is a comparative study of trance across Africa, the Mediterranean, Asia and the Americas <Source book="rouget" where="Introduction; ch. 1, 4, 8" />. His central claim is in the introduction: almost everyone who writes about trance assumes music has a secret power to trigger it by its own properties, and the assumption doesn't survive the evidence. Music matters a great deal, but mainly because it **socializes** trance: it makes something individual and unpredictable predictable, shaped and shared by the group.

What that means in practice, from his chapters:

- **The same music doesn't produce the same result.** In Morocco the same violent drumming, the same accelerating tempo and the same packed, swaying dance are used every day in a festive dance that never leads to trance. Accelerando, crescendo and loud drums go *with* trance in many cultures; that doesn't make them its cause. Observers who watch a frightening trance tend to call the music frightening too.
- **The trigger is often a learned signal.** In possession cults each spirit has its own tune, a **musical motto**. The adept recognizes it even when a quiet lute or flute is nearly buried under drums and castanets, and that recognition is what sets the trance off. In one Moroccan brotherhood the melody is played by a loud oboe; in Algeria, the same ritual uses a soft flute. Rouget's conclusion: the instrument's power is in its meaning, not its sound.
- **Music then holds the state.** In Sufi listening sessions the sung poetry triggers the trance and the rhythm keeps it going through the dancing. Accelerando and crescendo are used to raise the emotion.
- **The person has to want it.** A shaman's accelerating music leads to trance only if he has decided beforehand that it will.
- **Two famous rhythm claims are wrong.** One writer claimed trance rhythms are always odd (in 5, 7 or 11) and that music in four or eight has no hypnotic effect; Rouget calls this pure fantasy, contradicted by countless examples. Andrew Neher's theory that drumming drives brainwaves into trance rests on a lab drum at 4 beats per second that produced only eye-blinks. His proposed range of about 4 to 12 beats per second covers almost every tempo, so, as Rouget puts it, half of Africa would be in trance all year. Hove's modern EEG study found no such driving either.

### The devices trance music does share

Rouget's survey of what possession music actually sounds like <Source book="rouget" where="ch. 2, What Music?" /> finds a few things that recur, always with exceptions:

- **Accelerando and crescendo** are close to universal where trance comes with a crisis (trembling, falling). In one Senegalese healing rite the whole ceremony is a single long crescendo: a rattle, then a bell rung at the patient's ear, then the drums, then gourds, until the drums suddenly stop to wait for an answer. A crescendo can also mean playing *closer*: bells, rattles or singers moved right next to the person's ear.
- **Rhythmic breaks.** Haitian drummers break against the beat to interrupt the dance; an Indian account describes dancers settled into an easy rhythm, then a few violent drum strokes and a new, more complex rhythm. Rouget says such breaks recur very often but can't be called universal.
- **Calm trance, calm music.** Where trance is gentle (his example is the Mundang), the music keeps the same tempo and loudness throughout and renews itself through small expressive variations in the same register. The music, the dance and the trance match.
- **And the counterexamples.** Trances also start when the music slackens or has stopped. The same weekly ceremony with the same climactic drumming produced trances one week and none the next. A small bell shaken quietly at someone's ear has succeeded where full drumming failed.

Most of the candomblé rhythms he cites are in duple meter with triplet subdivisions, at a wide range of tempos.

For a band that writes in odd meters, the second-to-last point matters: **an odd meter is not a trance device**. What Rouget's evidence supports is a frame the listener has agreed to, a signal they've learned to recognise, and music that keeps a state going once it has started.

## Trance and ecstasy: a polarity, and its limit

Rouget separates two kinds of altered state <Source book="rouget" where="ch. 1, Trance or Ecstasy?" />:

| Ecstasy | Trance |
|---|---|
| stillness | movement |
| silence | noise |
| solitude | in company |
| no crisis | crisis (trembling, falling) |
| sensory deprivation | sensory overstimulation |
| remembered afterwards | not remembered |
| visions | no visions |

The two are the ends of a continuum, with many states in between. And here is the limit: **ecstasy, in his sense, never uses music**. Silence and stillness rule it out. A concert that "sent you into ecstasy" is, in his words, verbal inflation: if anything, it shows as a wish to prolong the moment in silence, and the applause ends it.

So a quiet section in a song isn't Rouget's ecstasy pole; it's still music, heard in company. Treat the table below as our own design axis, borrowed from his contrast, not as his finding:

| | Toward the crowd | Toward stillness |
|---|---|---|
| Density | maximal, layered, loud | sparse, few sounds |
| Rhythm | driving ostinato, drums forward | rubato, drone, or silence |
| Body | invites movement | invites stillness |
| In a song | climax, cumulative build | intro, breakdown, aftermath |

Alternating the two across a song, a record or a live set is one way to shape a long arc that people experience rather than count.

<KeyIdea>Design the frame, the signal and the polarity; let the listener do the rest. Then test whether they actually dropped in.</KeyIdea>

## Vertical time: boredom or timelessness

Kramer calls music with no phrase hierarchy, no build and no ending **vertical time**: one extended present <Source book="kramer" where="Ch. 2.12, 12" />. Two of his observations are directly useful for trance-oriented sections:

- **The world must be set early and never left.** A vertical piece defines its limits soon after it starts. Any step outside them is heard as a major event and breaks the spell.
- **Two listeners, two experiences.** Listeners still waiting for something to happen experience *slowed* time: boredom, acute awareness of time passing. Listeners who give up expecting experience *timelessness*, and their attention goes inside the sound (its edges, overtones, tiny variations). The music can't force the switch; it can only make it possible.

His own account of three hours of Satie's *Vexations* (one short piece repeated 840 times by relay pianists) follows that arc: linear listening, information exhausted, boredom, then a switch into a mode where he became intensely sensitive to the smallest performance differences, and the pianists' changeovers felt like huge contrasts. Three hours felt like forty minutes. In the opposite case, a dense multimedia happening with constant overload, 25 minutes felt like two hours. Both felt timeless; low information shrank remembered time and high information stretched it. Kramer's informal tests put the threshold for hearing a sustained sound as truly static at about two to three minutes.

For a band, this suggests a design question rather than a rule: when you build a long repetitive section, are you inviting the switch (a stable world, small variations worth noticing, no false promises of change), or are you keeping listeners in expectation? The listening test below can tell you which happened.

## Hypnosis ideas: useful analogies, not evidence

Erickson's papers on inducing trance describe structures a composer can borrow <Source book="erickson" where="papers 7, 8, 10, 16" />. **No research shows these induce anything in listeners**, and Erickson was working one to one, with people who had come to be hypnotized. Treat them as labelled craft analogies and test each one:

- **Utilization.** Erickson's rule was to accept what the person brings and use it, instead of trying to replace it with a set routine; a frightened patient was told to get stiff *first*. For a band: start from the state the room or the previous track left people in, and fold it into the material.
- **Don't belabour the obvious.** Once the listener has understood what's happening, he warns, further repetition produces only fatigue. Repetition absorbs only while it still has something to give (see *Repetition and process*).
- **Confusion, then something to hold on to.** His confusion technique sets up a moment that calls for an obvious response, then, just before the response, adds something that makes sense in itself but is irrelevant. He keeps the flow going, leaving almost, but never quite, enough time to answer. The need to respond builds, and the first clear, simple statement is seized on like a rock. He found it worked better one to one than in groups. The musical analogy: interrupt where a cadence or downbeat is expected with material that is coherent in itself, keep moving, then give one unmistakable pulse. It fits the prediction research but is untested.
- **Matching, then leading.** "Pacing and leading" is later writers' name for this. Erickson's own account (an unpublished fragment) describes matching a person's breathing and then gradually changing it. Musically it's Reich's gradual process and the "state, repeat, then displace" rule.
- **Fractionation:** go in, come out, go back deeper. This one comes from Elman's book, not read for this page.

Keep lyrics' "embedded suggestions" at the level of motif and theme. Covert persuasion is the weakest and least ethical part of the analogy, and Erickson's breathing anecdotes are exactly that kind of covert influence. If you ever talk about this publicly, claim only what's supported: music can deepen absorption for willing listeners; it doesn't hypnotize or entrain brainwaves on command.

## The listening-test kit

No study has tested prog or metal, odd-meter groove, or music longer than about six minutes. **Your listeners are the only data that fits your music.**

<Steps title="Protocol (about 1 hour)">

1. **Listeners:** 3–5 people who don't know the material, and ideally aren't musicians. Use one at a time, so they don't influence each other.
2. **Play once, uninterrupted,** at a decent volume, with nothing to look at.
3. **Then ask, with the song's timeline in front of you:**
   - Where did your attention drift? (mark times)
   - Where did you find yourself counting or "working it out"?
   - Where did you "drop in", stop thinking and just go with it?
   - Which moments do you remember without replaying? (landmarks)
   - Where did you want to move? Where did you feel something build?
4. **Play it again** and ask what they noticed the second time (Margulis: attention moves deeper with repetition).
5. **Record the answers** in the journal against the form plan's section numbers.

</Steps>

Then compare with your *intended* energy curve and landmarks. Where the listeners and the plan disagree is where to revise.
