## A form with no climax

Stockhausen named it, but Kramer traces moment form back through Debussy, Webern, Varèse, and above all Stravinsky and Messiaen <Source book="kramer" where="Ch. 8" />. Stockhausen's description of the aim, paraphrased: no climax, no build, none of the usual introductory, rising, transitional and fading stages. The music is intense from the first moment and stays at that level, as if it had **always already started** and could go on forever. Every moment is centred in itself rather than being the consequence of the last and the upbeat to the next.

Kramer's definition of a **moment**: a self-contained section, set off by discontinuities, heard more for itself than for its part in a progression <Source book="kramer" where="Ch. 2.10" />. It's self-contained in one of two ways:

- **Stasis**: one harmony, one texture, a figure permuted rather than developed.
- **A process that finishes inside the moment**: if the process leads to another section, it isn't a moment.

<KeyIdea>Moment form trades the drama of progression for the drama of juxtaposition. It needs blocks that are complete in themselves, contrasts between them that dwarf the contrast within, and a hidden logic that makes them belong together.</KeyIdea>

## Start and stop, not begin and end

Stockhausen separates **beginning** (a process that rises) and **ending** (a process that dies away) from **starting** and **stopping** (cuts that frame a stretch of an endless continuum). A moment form starts as if we'd tuned in mid-stream and breaks off as if it continued somewhere else. Kramer notes that this makes starting and stopping gestures more alike than tonal beginnings and endings ever are. Closing such a form is hard: Messiaen's *Turangalîla* ends by uniting earlier moments (more closure, less moment time); Stravinsky's *Symphonies* ends with a chorale moving to a cadence; Stockhausen's *Mixtur* simply stops.

## The rules the good examples follow

From Kramer's chapters on moment time and on Stravinsky's *Symphonies* <Source book="kramer" where="Ch. 8, 9" />:

1. **Contrast between moments must dwarf contrast within them.** Stasis is relative: a moment counts as static when its internal activity is far lower than the jumps between moments.
2. **Something must hold them together nonlinearly**: shared timbre, texture, pitch world, a consistent way of treating material. Too much discontinuity destroys context; Kramer reports many student moment forms failing for this reason.
3. **Returns are allowed, but not prepared.** An old moment can come back as long as nothing builds up to it like a recapitulation. And if nothing ever returned, constant newness would itself become predictable.
4. **Make planned order sound arbitrary.** Truly random order is less striking than order that only *seems* arbitrary. The craft is hiding the coherence in the background. Stockhausen describes choosing, moment by moment, how much material each one shares with the past and the future, from none to nearly all.
5. **Proportions do the formal work.** With no climax and no progression, balance comes from the relative lengths of the moments and their order. Moments of 4 seconds and 2 minutes can have equal rank. Stravinsky's *Symphonies* uses roughly 3:2 relations between related blocks; Messiaen's *Chronochromie* places one long static block after many short ones, late in the piece, as its centre of weight.
6. **Keep the levels few.** Moments, maybe submoments, maybe groups of moments. A rich hierarchy would bring back linearity.

In the *Symphonies*, Kramer finds the moments marked by three parameters changing together: **tempo, harmony and melodic cells**. Submoments change some but not all. Listeners learn as they go how much change means "new moment"; where the inside of a block is very narrow, two parameters are enough.

## Strata: blocks that continue in your head

Edward Cone, writing on the same piece, describes a technique Kramer builds on: **stratification, interlock, synthesis** <Source book="kramer" where="Ch. 9.12" />.

- **Stratification**: the music cuts between different strands; when one is suspended, we wait for it to resume.
- **Interlock**: in A1 B1 A2 B2 A3 B3, the A sections form one continuing line and the B sections another, even though we never hear either continuously.
- **Synthesis**: strands that have alternated are finally brought together.

Kramer's warning: a strand is held together more by identity (same tempo, material, harmony type or colour) than by continuous voice leading, and long-range links are only heard if the strand's register or timbre is kept unused in between.

## The case against, and the paradoxes

Elliott Carter rejected moment form: music happens in order, every moment should be led up to and led away from, and a string of interesting passages isn't a piece. Kramer accepts this for the linear side but answers that memory isn't only ordered: we reassemble a discontinuous piece as a whole after hearing it (**cumulative listening**) <Source book="kramer" where="Ch. 8.2" />.

He then names three paradoxes <Source book="kramer" where="Ch. 8.7" />:

1. **Moment time uses the linearity of listening to destroy the linearity of time.** We still hear in order, so a long block after many short ones, or an old block among new ones, has an immediate effect. We even form expectations about lengths (after blocks of 5, 10 and 20 seconds, we half-expect 40).
2. **It tries to defeat memory.** Nothing is developed, so nothing is rehearsed; this is also why sonata expositions are remembered better than developments.
3. **Yet its form depends on remembered lengths.** It works because it separates duration from content: we may forget a block's details and still keep a feeling for how long it was. That's why composers of moment forms often planned durations before sounds.

## Is there room for this in riff music?

Kramer's list of moment-time examples includes Frank Zappa's *Lumpy Gravy*, so the idea isn't confined to concert music. Prog's block-cut tradition (sudden switches of riff, tempo and sound) is close to moment time on the middle level, usually with linear riffs inside the blocks and often a linear climax plan over the top. The useful lesson is the set of conditions: complete blocks, big contrasts between them, a shared sound world, unprepared returns, and lengths that balance.

<ListenerSide grade="moderate">

The core claims are Kramer's analyses and Stockhausen's aesthetics. Two listener points are better grounded: memory for a piece without development or return is weaker (Snyder: hierarchy and rehearsal drive long-term memory), and static, discontinuous music makes perceived proportions closer to clock proportions (Kramer, *Perception* chapter, using the information model). His threshold for hearing a sound as static (two to three minutes) comes from informal classroom tests.

</ListenerSide>

## Practice

<Exercise title="A five-moment piece">

1. Write five blocks, each self-contained: three static (one sound world each), two with a process that completes inside the block.
2. Give them lengths in a consistent ratio (for example 3:2 between related blocks), from about 5 to 60 seconds.
3. Cut them together with no transitions, bring one block back once without preparing it, and stop rather than end.
4. Choose one shared element (a tuning, a timbre, a rhythmic cell) that runs through every block.
5. Listen through twice. Which cut feels wrong, and is the problem the content, the contrast, or the length?

</Exercise>
