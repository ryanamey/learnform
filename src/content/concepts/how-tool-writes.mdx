## Form comes out of a process

No book explains Tool's architecture. The closest thing to a primary source is the band's own account of how they work, and it shows that **much of their form comes from a disciplined process**, not a theory applied from outside. Here is that process, pieced together from interviews with Justin Chancellor, Danny Carey and Adam Jones <Source book="tool-interviews" />.

<KeyIdea>Generate freely, record everything, then select, name, count and assemble. Vocals come last, over a structure that's already settled.</KeyIdea>

## The six steps, as the band describes them

<Steps>

1. **Jam in short, regular blocks.** For *Lateralus*, Chancellor, Jones and Carey met Monday to Thursday while Maynard was touring with A Perfect Circle.
2. **Record every session.** The best takes went home on CD to be reviewed overnight. Carey describes listening back to find the "jewels" that turned up along the way, then assembling them the next day.
3. **Bring building blocks, not songs.** Chancellor calls the ideas "very basic building blocks." Riffs often come to him away from the bass, on a walk or in the car, get recorded on a phone, and are counted afterwards. They usually turn out to be in odd meters.
4. **Name every riff and count it.** On *Lateralus* each riff got a name on a whiteboard, with its beat count beside it. Each member counted differently, so the band needed a shared language for its sections.
5. **Simplify, then rebuild.** When Chancellor brought something strange, Jones and Carey often asked to hear it straight, for example in 4/4. The method was to start from the unusual groove, subtract toward something conventional, then build back toward the complex idea. Jones describes the group tearing ideas apart "like wolves" to find the strongest paths.
6. **Vocals last.** Maynard wrote once the music was largely set, stitching his phrases carefully into the shifting meters, as Chancellor puts it. The band stayed flexible when a strong lyric idea needed a section changed or extended.

</Steps>

## What this means for form

Three things in that process are formal decisions, even though they're described as working habits:

- **Selection is composition.** The form is assembled from the strongest moments of many hours of material. Most of what was played is thrown away.
- **Counting and naming turn sound into units you can arrange.** Once a riff has a name and a length, you can sequence it, vary it and argue about it. That's the step that makes large-scale design possible.
- **Equal voices make the dynamics.** Chancellor credits the album's wide dynamics to each member insisting on being heard, not to compression. Jones says the opposite matters as much: **not playing** can be as powerful as playing, and he chooses not to fill every gap.

Jones also describes the songs as **journeys that lead the listener through states**, and says album sequencing came *after* writing, not before. Remember both when you plan the EP order (Module 7).

<ListenerSide grade="moderate">

The "establish it, then move away from it" habit Chancellor describes (make a riff recognizable, then see how far you can push it) matches two findings you'll meet in Module 5. Listeners learn unusual meters through repetition (Hannon & Trehub). Variation is heard as development only when the original is remembered (Snyder; Belkin's similarity scale). The interviews don't cite any research. The match is ours.

</ListenerSide>

<Exercise title="One week of the Tool process">

1. Agree on two 90-minute sessions this week, in a room or remote, where you record everything.
2. After each session, everyone listens back alone and writes down the timestamps of their three favourite moments.
3. Pick the moments at least two people chose. Give each one a name and count it (beats or sixteenths).
4. Take the strangest one and run it through the simplify-then-rebuild loop: play it straight, then add back one irregularity at a time. Stop where it's strongest, not where it's strangest.

</Exercise>
