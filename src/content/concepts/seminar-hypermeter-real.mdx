## The question

Is a group of four bars a "measure" in the same way a bar is a measure, with felt strong and weak? Or is it something else: grouping, a memory of a pattern, a habit of composition? Your answer decides whether you plan sections in counted units of 4, 8 and 16, or in phrases that can be any length.

## Lerdahl & Jackendoff: meter is local

Meter is regular at the tactus and a level or two above it <Source book="gttm" where="Ch. 2, 4" />. At four bars, it is often open to interpretation; in Mozart's Symphony 40, the four-bar level has little meaning for them. At larger levels we hear grouping, parallelism, cadences and prolongation. Where groups are irregular, a preference for binary regularity remains.

## Hasty: definite, then less and less

A duration feels definite up to about two or three seconds <Source book="hasty" where="Ch. 12" />. At the phrase level, "first" and "second" replace strong and weak, and a second section promises a commensurate length, not an equal one. He won't fix a limit, and he leaves open whether large-scale "meter" differs from small-scale meter in degree or in kind. He rejects the model of irregular phrases as regular ones with a bar stretched (Rothstein's expansions).

## London: entrainment fades

London ties meter to entrainment, which weakens for slow periods <Source book="london" where="Ch. 2" />. A measure felt as one unit can't exceed about five or six seconds. Otherwise, he sees no essential difference between meter and hypermeter.

## Yust: rhythmic structure at four bars

Yust agrees that entrainment fades above the bar, but argues that **rhythmic structure** (built by projection and parallelism, not by entrainment) is regular at the four-bar level and sometimes eight <Source book="yust" where="Ch. 6" />. Unlike meter, it can contain irregularities: deletions, pauses, extended beginnings. It is how composers mark sections. He criticises both sides: theories that stop at two bars miss these techniques, and Schenkerian theories (Schachter, Rothstein, Samarotto) that allow unlimited expansion turn hypermeter into a version of tonal structure.

## Kramer and Stephenson: hypermeasures are real

Kramer treats hypermeasures as metric, with irregularity judged level by level <Source book="kramer" where="Ch. 4" />. Stephenson finds that rock is built in four-bar units marked by repeating instrumental patterns <Source book="stephenson" where="Ch. 1" />.

## Where they agree

Everyone agrees that felt strong and weak weakens above the bar, that composers and bands nevertheless build in units of two and four, and that departures from those units are felt.

## Where they differ

- **Is a 4-bar unit metrical?** Rarely (L&J, Hasty); as rhythmic structure (Yust); yes (Kramer).
- **What makes an irregular phrase?** A stretched regular one (Rothstein, as reported by Yust and Hasty); a new rhythmic structure (Yust); a contraction or expansion of projective activity (Hasty).
- **Triple hypermeter**: Yust's Haydn shows a long run of 3-bar groups that never feels like the norm; Cohn's Mozart lets triple win at one level.
