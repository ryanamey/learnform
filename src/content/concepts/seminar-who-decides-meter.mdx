## The question

When a passage can be heard in two meters, who decides which one it is: the **notation** (the time signature the band reads), the **performer** (what the band emphasises), or the **listener**? For a band that composes on paper or in Guitar Pro and then plays live, the answer matters.

## Notation: Magadini and Webern

Magadini proposes time signatures that show both layers of a polyrhythm, with barlines only where they meet <Source book="magadini" where="§11" />. Hasty shows the opposite problem in Webern's Quartet op. 22 <Source book="hasty" where="Ch. 14" />. The changing time signatures (3/8, 5/8, 4/8, 3/8) mark the phrase's pieces, not its meter. Webern's notation can't both show the meter and give players a grid to count on. A good performance, Hasty concludes, needs playing by ear rather than counting. Webern's sketches show him reworking mainly the rhythm, trying the same phrase in several meters.

## The performer: Lerdahl & Jackendoff, Yust, London

GTTM: in ambiguous passages, a slight stress or a small change of articulation can tip the listener's hearing <Source book="gttm" where="Ch. 2–3" />. The variations of Mozart's K. 331 alternate between two groupings of the theme.

Yust: in the Tempo di Menuetto of Bach's G-major Partita, the notes suggest 6/8 but Bach writes 3/4 <Source book="yust" where="Ch. 1.3" />. Only the performer can make the written meter heard; played flat, the cadence sounds like a sudden change of meter.

London: performers may count one meter while listeners hear another, as in a 3-3-2 bass line read in 4/4 but heard as three uneven beats <Source book="london" where="Ch. 8" />.

## The listener: Hasty and Cohn

Hasty: analysis is a guess about possible experiences, not the experience itself. He writes "we" to invite the reader to test his hearing <Source book="hasty" where="Ch. 10" />. Cohn: some ambiguities can't be decided by argument. Two listeners can hear the same piece with opposite norms, and a third can hear the tension itself as the norm <Source book="cohn" where="§IV" />.

## Hear the question

Play the hemiola example in *Grouping and displacement* twice: once tapping the guitar's groups, once tapping the drums'. Did the meter change, or only your attention?

## Where they agree

All agree that notation is not the meter, that performance can change what is heard, and that some passages are genuinely open.

## Where they differ

- **Should notation show the meter or help the players count?** Magadini wants both layers shown; Hasty's Webern shows the conflict.
- **How much can a performer decide?** GTTM and Yust: enough to tip an ambiguity. Hasty: some readings exist only if the performer creates them.
- **Is the listener's choice free?** Cohn: sometimes, and it can't be settled by argument. London: one meter at a time, steered by the texture.
