## Repetition changes how we listen

Elizabeth Margulis's *On Repeat* is the central study of musical repetition <Source book="margulis" />. Two of its findings (as reported in Albrecht's review) matter directly for long, riff-based music:

- **Attention moves deeper with repetition.** Early on, listeners detect short repeating units easily. With more exposure they start detecting **longer** repetitions, and get *worse* at noticing the short ones. Repetition pushes attention up the hierarchy.
- **Repetition makes complex music sound intended.** When literal repetition was added to atonal excerpts (Berio, Carter), professional theorists enjoyed them more, found them more interesting, and were more likely to believe a human composed them.

The reviewer's caveats: the book has no single unifying theory, and several of the experiments it proposes were never run.

## The carrier: repetition that disappears

Hove and colleagues scanned 15 experienced shamanic practitioners listening to repetitive drumming (about 4 beats per second), entering trance or not <Source book="hove" />. During trance, a network linked to inwardly directed thought became more active, and **connectivity along the auditory pathway dropped**. The authors read this as *perceptual decoupling*: the brain suppresses the predictable, repetitive drum signal while attention turns inward. The sample was small and trained, and they entered trance on purpose, so ordinary listeners at a gig may behave differently.

## Reich: make the process audible

Steve Reich's 1968 essay "Music as a Gradual Process" adds the composer's half <Source book="reich" />. He wants **the compositional process and the sounding music to be one and the same thing**: no hidden structure, everyone hearing the process unfold. The change has to be *extremely* gradual, like watching the minute hand of a watch. You notice it has moved only after staying with it a while. Even with everything audible, Reich says, the mysteries remain: sub-melodies, psychoacoustic by-products, small irregularities that pull the listener in. He also notes that modal, drone-based music, including drug-oriented rock of the time, focuses the ear on minute sound details.

<KeyIdea>Let a repeated layer become the carrier the listener stops noticing; put slow, audible change on top of it. Repetition does the absorbing, gradual change holds attention.</KeyIdea>

## Anatomy of a process piece

Kramer's analysis of Frederic Rzewski's *Les Moutons de Panurge* shows what a process does to time <Source book="kramer" where="Ch. 12.7" />. The rule is simple: play note 1, then 1–2, then 1–2–3, up to all 65 notes of a melody, then take notes away from the front; players who get lost are told to stay lost, so the unison drifts into canon.

- **The rate of new information keeps falling.** Half the notes are new on the second pass; by the 65th pass, one note in 65. That steady decline, not the motion itself, is what makes the long middle feel static.
- **Small linear threads still matter inside the narrow limits:** a regular pulse slowly emerges; one mode is gradually replaced by another; a leading tone is left hanging for about five minutes before it resolves. These keep the stasis alive.
- **Beginning and ending are linear.** The opening is a learning phase; the subtractive end accelerates and gets exciting again. The timeless part is the interior.

Kramer's point for composers: in process music, motion that is constant and predictable is heard as stasis.

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. **Repeat odd or complex material literally before developing it.** It sounds intended, and listeners learn it (see also *Anticipation, chills and groove*).
2. **After a few cycles, change the slow layers, not the riff.** Attention has moved up the hierarchy, so development should happen up there: layering, register, density, a new voice. The riff can stay.
3. **Write at least one "carrier" section** per album: a long ostinato with one gradual, audible process on top (additive melody, slowly opening filter, phasing, a feel change by degrees).
4. **Make the process audible.** If the change is a hidden scheme, the listener just hears repetition.

</Steps>

<Exercise title="Write a carrier section">

1. Choose a one- or two-bar ostinato (bass and drums, or guitar).
2. Repeat it 16 times. Over it, write **one** gradual process: a melody that gains a note each cycle, a filter that opens over 16 bars, a second guitar that slowly shifts by a sixteenth, or a feel that moves from half to normal to double time.
3. Play it to someone. Ask when they stopped hearing the ostinato, and when they first noticed the change.

</Exercise>

<ApplyIt>

Find a very repetitive passage in a piece you know. Is it repetition the listener *settles into* (a carrier with something moving on top), or repetition that's simply waiting? Write down the one gradual, audible process you would add to it.
