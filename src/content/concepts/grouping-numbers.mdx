## Rhythm written as numbers

*Odd meters* (London) explains why 5, 7 and 9 are heard as beats of 2 and 3. Dafnis Prieto, a Cuban drummer and composer who teaches at the University of Miami, turns that into a working language for composing and rehearsing <Source book="prieto" where="Level 3" />. He calls it **rhythmic numerology**: write a rhythm as a string of 2s and 3s, where each number is that many subdivisions, and clap the first note of each number.

- **5** is 3-2 or 2-3.
- **7** is 2-2-3, 2-3-2 or 3-2-2.
- **9** is 2-2-2-3, 2-2-3-2, 2-3-2-2, 3-2-2-2 or 3-3-3.

Two things make this more than a counting trick:

- **Reordering is variation.** The same numbers in a different order give a different riff in the same meter. A 5-cycle played as 3-2 3-2 2-3 2-3 already changes its feel halfway through. Prieto notes that 2-2-3, 2-3-2, 3-2-2 played in a row reads the same forwards and backwards, a rhythmic palindrome.
- **A cycle can combine lengths.** 5 + 7 = 12, so 2-3 2-2-3 fits a 12/8 bar. In triplets it sounds like clave; in sixteenths it's a different riff with the same numbers.

## The same cell at four speeds

Prieto's next step is to put one number string on different subdivisions against a steady quarter-note pulse <Source book="prieto" where="Level 3, Odd Meters" />. A 5-cycle in a 5/4 bar:

| Subdivision | Times per 5/4 bar |
|---|---|
| Quarter notes | once |
| Eighth notes | twice |
| Eighth-note triplets | three times |
| Sixteenth notes | four times |

The cell is the same; its relation to the pulse is not. In quarters it *is* the meter. In sixteenths it's a fast figure crossing a slow bar. For a song this is a ready-made way to grow a section: introduce a cell slowly, then double its speed against the same pulse, so the listener hears the idea first and the density later. It also connects to London's timing window: at quarter = 100, a 5-cycle in sixteenths lasts 750 ms; in quarters it lasts 3 seconds.

## Pattern fixed, or pulse fixed

Prieto's central claim is that **a pattern only exists against a pulse**: the same structure means different things depending on what it's heard against <Source book="prieto" where="Level 3" />. He calls moving one structure through different settings "meta transmutation", and there are two directions:

- **The pulse stays, the pattern changes speed.** One string (2-3 2-2-3) played in eighths, then sixteenths, then triplets, over the same quarter-note pulse.
- **The pattern stays, the pulse changes.** The string stays in sixteenths, and the pulse under it changes: three dotted-quarter beats in one bar, four dotted-eighth beats in the next.

This is the same idea as Krebs's metrical processes from the other side, and it's the main tool Tool and Meshuggah use: the guitar loop keeps its shape while the drummer moves the pulse under it. Prieto's version makes it something a band can rehearse.

## Cycles of two lengths

When two parts loop patterns of different lengths, they line up again after the lowest common multiple of the lengths <Source book="prieto" where="Level 3, Multi-Meters" />. When the lengths share no factor, that's simply the product:

| Lengths | Realign after | |
|---|---|---|
| 2 against 3 | 6 | |
| 3 against 4 | 12 | |
| 4 against 5 | 20 | |
| 5 against 7 | 35 | |
| 7 against 9 | 63 | |
| 3 against 9 | 9 | (3 already fits inside 9) |

He also pairs **same-length patterns with different groupings**: 7 as 2-2-3 against 3-2-2, or 9 as 2-3-2-2 against 3-3-3. They realign every bar but disagree inside it, which is a grouping dissonance in Krebs's terms.

Two uses for form:

- **The realignment point is a landmark.** If the guitar loops 7 and the drums loop 9, they meet every 63 units. Put the vocal entry, the chord change or the section boundary there, and the listener hears a release; put it anywhere else, and the layers keep their tension across the seam.
- **The cycle length tells you the section length.** A section that wants to resolve its layers needs to last a whole number of realignments, or deliberately stop short of one (Tool's sections often end before the layers meet).

## Dots and lines: a chart for the drummer's plan

To show how a pattern sits against a reference rhythm, Prieto draws **2 as a dot and 3 as a line**, set in four columns, one column per clave cycle <Source book="prieto" where="Level 3, cycles 1–11" />. A 5-cycle (2-3) against a 12-unit clave fills four columns, three rows each; a 7-cycle (2-2-3) has two dots and a line per row. Then he varies the chart:

- **Switch at the midpoint.** Halfway through the sequence (column 3) the clave turns to its other side, so he changes the pattern there: two columns of a 5-pattern, then two of a 7-pattern.
- **Every column different.** 2-3, then 3-2, then 2-2-3, then 3-2-2.
- **Change the subdivision at the midpoint.** Two columns in triplets, two in sixteenths, with the reference pattern changing feel with it.

For the band, the reference doesn't have to be clave. It can be the bass riff, the guitar loop or a hi-hat ostinato. The chart is a quick way for the drummer to show the others where a grouping change falls against the loop they're playing.

## The Karnatic version: cycles of unequal limbs

South Indian (Karnatic) music organises rhythm the same way, as cycles, and its vocabulary is worth having <Source book="sriram" where="Taalam; Elements of a Recital" />:

- **A taala is a cycle built from unequal limbs**, counted with claps, waves and finger counts. The common Aadi taala is 4 + 2 + 2 = 8 beats. There are seven families of limb patterns, five possible lengths for the variable limb (3, 4, 5, 7 or 9) and so 35 taalas, each playable in five subdivisions: 175 patterns. This is the additive meter of 2s and 3s, built into a system.
- **Nadai** is the subdivision of each beat: 4, 3, 5, 7 or 9 notes. Changing nadai over the same taala is Prieto's "same cell at a new speed" from the other side.
- **Speeds.** The same line can be performed at half, normal or double speed against the cycle. In the *pallavi* of a Ragam–Tanam–Pallavi, the singer renders one lyric line once per cycle, twice per cycle, or once every two cycles, either by keeping the rhythm and changing the melody's pace or the reverse. That's augmentation and diminution as performance.
- **Eduppu** is where a phrase enters relative to the cycle: on beat 1 (*sama*), after it (*anaagatha*), or before it as a pickup (*atheetha*). An entry after beat 1 leaves a gap that lets each improvised passage return to the fixed line "with great momentum".

The primer also describes the shape of a concert piece that maps onto long-form writing: an unmetered exploration (aalapana) that moves middle register → high → low → middle; a pulsed but unmetered section (tanam); then the metered song. Melody lines are varied step by step, simple first and more complex later (*sangathi*).

<Aside>

What the primer doesn't cover is the cadence technique your drummer needs most: the *tihai* and *mukthay* (a phrase played three times so the last note lands on beat 1) and the systematic grouping changes (*gati bhedam*, *jathi bhedam*). Those are in Rafael Reina's *Applying Karnatic Rhythmical Techniques to Western Music*, which is still on the reading list.

</Aside>

1. Pick a 7/8 riff the band already plays. Write its number string (2-2-3, 2-3-2 or 3-2-2).
2. Play the other two orders over the same chord changes. Which one makes the bar's 1 feel strongest, and which one leans into the next bar?
3. Combine two orders into a two-bar pattern (2-2-3 3-2-2). Use it for a verse and keep the plain riff for the chorus.

</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Clap and sing (band rehearsal)">

Prieto's method works for a whole band. One person claps a number string while another sings or plays the riff against it, then one person does both <Source book="prieto" where="Levels 1–2" />.

1. Start slow (quarter = 65–80) and learn each string by memory before adding the riff.
2. Use the audio examples: each part is on its own side, so one player can mute a side and play that part.
3. Then do it with your own material: the drummer claps the grouping of a new part while the guitarist plays the riff, before anyone picks up sticks.

</Exercise>

<Exercise title="Find the landmark">

1. Choose two loops in one of your songs that have different lengths (say the guitar repeats every 7 eighths and the drums every 4 quarters = 8 eighths).
2. Work out where they realign (here, every 56 eighths).
3. Put a section boundary or vocal entry exactly there in one version, and two eighths early in another. Play both to the band. Which one sounds like an arrival?

</Exercise>

<ListenerSide grade="moderate">

Prieto's book is a teaching method from long classroom experience, not a study of listeners. The ideas it rests on are well supported: grouping by 2s and 3s matches London's research on non-isochronous meters, and listeners do track one meter at a time and feel other layers against it. Whether an audience hears the realignment point of a 63-beat cycle is less clear; long cycles are probably felt as a release when the layers meet, rather than counted. Whether listeners hear it is something to test.

</ListenerSide>

## A shared alphabet with the drummer

Benny Greb's method gives drummer and band a vocabulary for rhythms at the level of one beat <Source book="greb" where="Ch. 1" />. Every possible one-beat rhythm is a **letter**. The 16 on/off combinations of four sixteenths are letters A–P ("binary"), and the 8 combinations of three triplet eighths are Q–X ("ternary"). He drills them as hand exercises, rudiments, and then on the kit as bass-drum, hi-hat and ghost-note letters over an ostinato. A riff can then be named as a string of letters and passed on without notation, much like Prieto's numbers. His next chapter, "Words", builds groupings of 3, 5 and 7 from them (see the exercise in *Odd meters*).
