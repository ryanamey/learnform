## What closure is for

Every writer in this course treats endings as graded: some close a little, some close a lot. Snyder explains why music has to do this at all. Short-term memory holds only a few seconds, so music arrives in chunks, and the chunks have to be connected; closure is how a boundary is made stronger or weaker <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 5" />. He calls the control of boundary strength a creative use of the limits of short-term memory. Without those limits, he argues, there would be no need for units at all.

He separates two kinds of closure:

- **Syntactic closure** comes from learned, style-specific patterns. A cadence formula closes because the style has used it to close, many times; Snyder calls such patterns relatively arbitrary. He deliberately leaves syntax out of his book.
- **Natural closure** (his book's subject) comes from features that work with little or no learning: a drop in intensity, longer durations, a pause, repetition, a return to something central.

He adds two observations that the other writers share in their own words. Closure is **partial**: some parameters close while others stay open, and this is one of the main ways music keeps going. And the **more parameters close at once, the higher the level** of the boundary. Patterns with no organised motion and no expectation don't close at all; they **simply stop**.

<KeyIdea>Snyder: closure comes either from learned formulas (syntactic) or from falling intensity, length, pause, repetition and return (natural). The more parameters close together, the bigger the boundary.</KeyIdea>

## Falling intensity

Snyder borrows James Tenney's sense of **intensity**: any change that raises neural activity. Higher pitch, more dissonance, brighter sound, faster events, denser texture and more loudness all count as more intense <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 5" />. A group feels more closed the more its parameters fall in intensity at the end. He points to speech: falling intonation at the end of a statement is close to universal across languages, questions stay high, and the word cadence itself comes from the Latin for falling. Longer durations close as well, and so does **repetition**: a repeated pattern sounds more closural than a new one. His example is North Indian raga, where a short melodic pattern repeated closes each rhythmic cycle. A **return to the central pitch** closes too, because memory already knows it as home.

## Belkin's dimensions

Belkin's chapter on punctuation covers the same ground from the composer's desk, dimension by dimension <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 5" />:

- **Melody.** Falling is conclusive and rising sounds like a question, but always relative to context: after an angular line, smooth motion suggests repose even if it rises a little. Small intervals into stable notes (his leading-tone principle) close more than leaps. Ending in a new, unprepared register sounds like a mistake.
- **Harmony.** The tonic closes most. Outside tonality, closing means reducing tension: less dissonant intervals, spacing nearer the overtone series, outer voices converging. Harmonic rhythm often speeds up and then settles.
- **Rhythm.** No punctuation without it: a loss of momentum, a landing on a strong beat, a pause. He describes endings in Shostakovich and Mahler where the note values simply get longer until the motion stops.
- **Dynamics.** A falling figure with a diminuendo closes; a crescendo into the cadence is propulsive and climactic; a sudden change of level is an interruption that creates suspense.
- **Texture and timbre.** Thinning works like a diminuendo; he names the fade-out ending of pop songs as an example. But a change of instrument alone, not coordinated with rhythm, line or harmony, sounds arbitrary.

His rule is coordination: punctuation needs at least **two dimensions** working together, and he holds that harmony alone never makes a cadence, otherwise every V–I would be one.

## Schoenberg's cadence

Schoenberg's account belongs to the syntactic side but adds a melodic one <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. VII–VIII" />. A cadence combines harmonic delimitation with **liquidation of motival obligations**: the features that demand continuation are reduced to ones that don't. The melody before a cadence takes on a contour of its own. Either it follows the tendency of the smallest notes and speeds up, like an accelerando, or it moves against it with longer notes, a written-out ritardando. A climax recedes to the middle register.

  <Position book="belkin" where="Ch. 5">Punctuation needs at least two coordinated dimensions; almost always rhythmic relaxation. Harmony alone never makes one.</Position>
  <Position book="caplin" where="Ch. 2–3">A cadence is harmonic and syntactic: a cadential progression with a falling melody. Only three kinds in Classical style. A stop is not an end.</Position>

<ListenerSide grade="moderate">

Snyder reports that music with clear, hierarchical closure is much easier to hold in memory than music without it (he cites Bigand), and that falling intonation at the end of statements is found across a very large sample of languages. Whether falling pitch closes because of speech or because of something deeper he leaves open. Belkin's dimension-by-dimension account is a composer's experience, consistent with Snyder's but not tested by him.
