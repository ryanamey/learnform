## Reward arrives before the peak

Salimpoor and colleagues measured what happens in the brain around musical chills <Source book="salimpoor" />. Out of 217 people who answered an advert, five rounds of screening left **eight** who got chills reliably: at the same point in the music every time, undiminished by repeated listening, and not tied to a personal memory. Each brought instrumental pieces that gave them chills (classical, folk, jazz, electronica, rock, punk, techno, tango). Someone else's favourites, which they found neutral, served as the comparison. Listeners pressed a button at each chill, about three per excerpt.

What they found:

- **Pleasure released dopamine** in the brain's reward system, measured directly with a PET tracer. It was the first direct evidence for this with music.
- **Two regions, two moments.** In the seconds *before* a chill, one region (the caudate) became more active, then dropped at the chill itself. Another (the nucleus accumbens) kept rising and peaked **during** the chill. Listeners also rated the run-up as more pleasurable than the piece on average. Anticipation was rewarding in its own right.
- **More chills, more anticipation activity; stronger chills, more peak activity.** The number of chills a person had tracked the anticipation region; how intense they were tracked the peak region.
- **Chills aren't required.** With every chill moment removed, the peak region still rose step by step from neutral to "low pleasure" to "high pleasure." Chills are a convenient marker of the top of that scale, not the thing itself.

One correction to how this study is often quoted: the **15 seconds** before each chill is the window the researchers *chose* to analyse, following an earlier study. They didn't find that anticipation lasts 15 seconds. The authors also leave open whether anticipation comes from knowing the style's rules or from knowing that a favourite moment is coming in this particular piece; they suspect both. They point out that composers heighten it by delaying the expected outcome, with an unexpected note or a slowing tempo before the resolution.

Caveats: eight participants, all selected because they get chills easily, listening to music they chose and knew well.

## Why a surprise can feel good

David Huron's theory of expectation explains the mechanism behind those two peaks <Source book="huron" where="Ch. 1–2 (your copy ends early in ch. 3)" />. He splits the feelings an expected event produces into five responses, two before it and three after:

| | Response | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Before | **Imagination** | You feel a muted version of a possible outcome in advance, which motivates you to wait for it |
| Before | **Tension** | Body and attention gear up for the moment. If the timing is uncertain, you have to stay ready longer, which is tiring |
| After | **Prediction** | You're rewarded for having guessed right, whether the outcome was good or bad |
| After | **Reaction** | A fast, unthinking response that always assumes the worst about anything surprising |
| After | **Appraisal** | A slower, conscious judgment of what actually happened |

The key is **contrastive valence**. The fast reaction treats every surprise as a danger; a moment later the appraisal finds it harmless (it's only music), and the gap between the two feels good. That's why an unexpected moment can be more pleasurable than an expected one, and why a loud, sudden event in a safe setting is enjoyable rather than frightening. He also notes that the fast reaction never learns that music is safe: it works from general style knowledge, not memory of this song, so a well-made surprise keeps working on the tenth listen.

Strong surprises come out as one of three responses, which Huron links to the three animal defences:

- **Frisson** (chills, goose flesh), linked to the *fight* display of raised hair. It's most likely when a passage is both **loud** and **violates an expectation**, such as an abrupt modulation or an entry that comes early. Huron found he could make chills stronger or weaker just by changing the playback volume.
- **Laughter**, linked to the panting of *flight*: a danger that appears and dissolves at once.
- **Awe**, linked to *freezing*: a gasp and held breath before something that stays big.

He also separates uncertainty about **what** will happen from uncertainty about **when**. Each creates a different kind of tension, and a composer can hold one fixed while varying the other.

<KeyIdea>For a chill, combine loudness with a broken expectation, set up by a style the listener already knows. Surprise only works against a norm.</KeyIdea>

## Groove: the inverted U

Vuust & Witek's review treats **meter as the brain's prediction model** and rhythm as the input it checks against that model <Source book="vuust-witek" />. The gap between them is the prediction error, and the article argues that syncopation, polyrhythm and groove are all versions of that gap. It's a theory paper built on other studies; these are the findings it reports:

- **The default model is duple.** Played a perfectly even click, people's brains still mark every other beat as stronger. Listeners also bounced in duple or triple time (babies on a parent's knee, adults on their own) went on to hear an ambiguous rhythm in the meter they had moved to. Movement shapes which meter you hear.
- **Syncopation works only against a meter it doesn't destroy.** For a syncopation to have its effect, it has to contradict the meter without undermining it. The deeper levels are less certain than the theory books suggest: studies struggle to show that listeners weight anything below the downbeat in a strict hierarchy.
- **Training makes the model stronger, so violations hit harder.** Expert jazz musicians' brains responded to kick-drum syncopations that most non-musicians' brains didn't flag at all. For two of the jazz players, the tapping showed them shifting the downbeat: the model itself was updated.
- **Holding a counter-meter takes effort.** In the sax solo of Sting's "Lazarus Heart" the rhythm moves to a different meter for six bars with no trace of the old one, then the two line up again. Rhythm-section players (drummers, bassists, guitarists, pianists) tapped the main meter while focusing on either one. Holding the counter-meter recruited extra brain areas, and the better the player's timing, the less extra activity it needed. A single syncopation is handled early in the hearing system; a polyrhythm that lasts for bars keeps higher-level areas working.
- **3 against 4 flips like Rubin's vase.** The same pattern can be heard as 3/4 with a four-beat counter-rhythm or 4/4 with a three-beat one, and attention tends to switch between them. Unlike the vase, a trained musician can choose to hold one reading against the evidence.
- **Groove peaks at medium syncopation.** Witek's survey used 50 funk drum breaks, each a two-bar phrase repeated four times. Pleasure and the wish to move peaked in the middle of the syncopation range. The authors' explanation: with too little syncopation there's no error to process; with too much, the brain can't find a meter, so there's no model to be wrong against. More objective complexity can mean *less* effect. People who danced a lot rated the breaks higher, whatever their formal training.
- **Repeated grooves stay engaging** because the higher levels learn to expect the deviations; the tension between the levels doesn't go away.

Why prediction error should feel good is, the authors admit, unsolved. Their best guess is dopamine rewarding the learning (the Salimpoor study above shows dopamine in music listening, but not this link), and it hasn't been tested.

## Odd meters are learned

Hannon & Trehub (2005) found that North American adults missed rhythm changes in complex Balkan-style meters, while Bulgarian and Macedonian adults, and 6-month-old infants, did not. Preference for simple meters is **learned through exposure**, not built in. (Summarised from the psychology report; not page-verified here.) For a single song, the implication is that repetition is how the audience learns your meter.

## How to use it

<Steps>

1. **Anchor layer.** In every complex section keep one layer clear: kick on the cycle downbeat, steady hats, a pedal note or drone. Matthews et al. (2019) found simpler harmony strengthens the groove response to syncopation.
2. **State, repeat, then displace.** Introduce an odd-meter riff plainly, repeat it enough to be learned, and only then add displacement or polyrhythm. A counter-meter needs a model to push against.
3. **Plan the realignment.** A long counter-meter costs the listener effort. Like the "Lazarus Heart" solo, let it run for a set span and then line up with the main meter again, so the return is felt as an arrival.
4. **Calibrate to the audience, not the drummer.** What the band hears as "simple" sits far to the right of the inverted U for most listeners. Use the listening test to find where they actually are.
5. **Body sections vs. puzzle sections.** Use simpler or longer cycles where you want people to move. Save the puzzles for listening sections.
6. **Approach every climax audibly.** Combine Module 3's levers and cues over a span long enough to recognise as a run-up (the studies looked at the 15 seconds before a peak), and consider delaying the arrival slightly.

</Steps>

Groove's inverted U comes from funk loops in 4/4. A 2024 study reportedly found no effect of drum complexity, and no study has tested groove in odd meters. Salimpoor's anticipation finding comes from a tiny, selected sample. Learned meter (Hannon & Trehub) is strong evidence for enculturation; that the same learning happens *within* a single song is an inference.
