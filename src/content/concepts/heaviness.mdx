## Heaviness has more than one knob

Metal players use "heavy" for many things, and the research agrees that it's a bundle, not one property. Jan-Peter Herbst summarises the earlier work <Source book="herbst18" where="Introduction" />: Berger and Fales tied heaviness to piercing treble, loudness and harmonic dissonance, and found distortion behind all three, because distortion turns the guitar from a plucked, decaying instrument into a sustained, driven one. Over metal's history the guitar got more distorted and its frequency range wider, and layering several guitar tracks added density at the cost of clarity. Very slow or very fast tempos, an obscured tonality and dark modes (Phrygian, Locrian) add heaviness too. Black metal gets it from full chords and fast tremolo picking; death metal from going lower.

For a writer that means heaviness can be built from at least four places:

| Knob | What changes | Where it's planned |
|---|---|---|
| **Gain** | Clean, crunch, drive, high gain | The guitar and bass sound per section |
| **Voicing** | Single notes, power chords, triads, extended chords | The riff |
| **Density** | How many guitar layers, how low, how much space | The arrangement and mix |
| **Structure** | Tempo extremes, dark modes, dissonant intervals | The riff and the section |

<KeyIdea>Heaviness is something you plan section by section, like loudness or register. Decide which knob each heavy section uses, and save at least one for the climax.</KeyIdea>

## What distortion does to a chord

Esa Lilja measured distorted chords with acousticians at the Helsinki University of Technology <Source book="lilja" where="Ch. 4.1–4.3" />. Distortion does two things. It boosts the upper overtones of every note (harmonic distortion), and it creates new tones from the combinations of the notes played (intermodulation). These combination tones are real, measurable sound, not an illusion, and they appear without high volume. Distortion even makes the guitar *more* in tune with itself, because the exactly harmonic distortion partials drown out the slightly out-of-tune overtones of a real string.

What that means for each chord shape:

- **The power chord** is the clearest structure under gain. A fifth produces a new low tone an octave below the root (a "distortion fundamental"), and every other tone it generates belongs to that one harmonic series, including a **major third and a minor seventh nobody played**. Pete Townshend said he leaves thirds out because he can already hear them in the distortion.
- **The major triad** still works, but the third you play (equal-tempered) and the third the distortion generates (pure) don't match, so they beat against each other. At the milder gain of late-1960s rock it's fine; at 1980s gain it's rough.
- **The minor triad** turns to mud. Its combination tones belong to three different harmonic series, so the low end is crowded and the root is hard to hear. That's why minor triads are rare in heavy guitar parts, and why players swap them for power chords or major triads even where the mode says minor (Sabbath's "Die Young" plays the VI and VII as major triads but the tonic as a power chord).

Lilja turns this into a chord classification. **Vertical consonances** are the shapes that fit the lowest harmonics of one fundamental: the power chord, the major triad and the dominant seventh, in rising order of roughness. Everything else (the minor triad, the minor-sixth dyad, Iommi's stacked-fourth chords) is a **vertical dissonance** under gain <Source book="lilja" where="Ch. 4.5–4.6" />. Two consequences:

- **A chord can be out of the mode and still sound "right".** A major chord on the tonic of a Dorian song ("White Room") or a 7♯9 tonic ("Purple Haze") fuses into its root and works as that root, not as a modulation.
- **A chord can be in the mode and still sound rough.** The G minor triad in Uriah Heep's "July Morning" chorus stands out against the power chords around it. Rough chords can be used on purpose: Lilja points to the minor-seventh chords in the bridge of "Heaven and Hell" and to "Supernaut", which puts its roughest chord on the tonic downbeat, like a jazz arranger putting the most dissonant voicing on the most important beat.

As gain went up through the 1970s and 1980s, chords got simpler: dominant sevenths and triads gave way to power chords. Extended chords stayed in clean passages (Zeppelin's "No Quarter").

## What listeners hear: Herbst's experiment

Herbst recorded five structures on the same root (single note, power chord, major triad, minor triad, an altered 7♯9 chord) through five valve amps at three gain settings, measured them, and had 171 listeners rate how pleasant they were <Source book="herbst18" where="Method, Results" />. The main results:

- **Both gain and harmonic complexity make a sound harsher, and most of all together.** Acoustically, gain had a slightly bigger effect than the chord.
- **The first step matters most.** Going from clean to overdrive changed the ratings about twice as much as going from overdrive to full distortion.
- **The order holds at every gain level**: power chord, then major, then minor, then the altered chord. Power chords and major chords barely differ; the gap between major and minor grows large once gain is added.
- **The best predictor of harshness was fluctuation**, how much the spectrum changes from moment to moment (the "buzzing" quality), not roughness in the classical sense. Brightness and loudness came next.
- **Listeners differ more than chords do.** For people who like rock and metal, gain alone hardly lowered the ratings; only fluctuation did. Guitarists, older listeners and men were less bothered. Herbst's conclusion is that for metal fans, heaviness takes **structure plus the right amount of gain**: distortion alone isn't heavy to them.

His discussion adds the production side (after Mark Mynett): heaviness and clarity trade against each other. Layering guitars widens the sound but blurs the attack; complex chords lower clarity further; and a distorted guitar covers roughly 50 Hz to 12 kHz, so it competes with every other instrument. A guitar that takes all the space leaves none for the bass, drums and voice that also make a mix heavy <Source book="herbst18" where="Discussion" />.

<ListenerSide grade="moderate">

Herbst's results come from 171 German university students, mostly music students, rating isolated chords rather than songs, and he notes that repetition within a song changes how a sound is heard. Lilja's acoustic measurements are solid, but his consonance categories also rest on how often structures are used. Both agree on the direction (simple voicings stay clear under gain; minor and complex ones don't), which is enough to plan with.

</ListenerSide>

## Planning heaviness across a song

Putting the two sources together with the planner's levers:

- **Don't spend the gain step early.** The jump from clean to driven is the biggest change in harshness listeners hear. A song that starts at full gain has used its biggest heaviness step before the first verse.
- **After the gain step, build with structure and density.** More gain on top of high gain adds little. A later section gets heavier through the riff (darker mode, a tritone, a rough chord on the strong beat), the register (lower), or the arrangement (a second guitar, the bass doubling), not through another notch on the amp.
- **Keep the root clear where the vocal has to sit.** Power chords and single notes leave space; minor triads and extended chords fill it with combination tones.
- **Use roughness as an event.** A deliberately rough chord (a minor seventh, a cluster, Iommi's fourths) on a structural downbeat works because the chords around it are clean-sounding power chords.
- **Clean is the marked sound in metal.** Lavengood's point on *Layers and the sound-box*: in metal a clean guitar is what draws attention, so a clean passage is a contrast tool in its own right.

<Exercise title="Gain × voicing test">

1. Take one riff and record it six ways: power chords and triads, each at clean, crunch and high gain. Keep the tempo, mic and level the same.
2. Play the six in random order to the band (or anyone) and have each person rate *heavy* and *clear* from 1 to 7.
3. Compare the steps: how much did the first gain step change the ratings, and how much the second? Did triads lose clarity faster than power chords?
4. Write the result into the form plan: for each section, the gain level and voicing you'll use, and which knob the climax saves.

</Exercise>

<Exercise title="One heavy event">

Write a sixteen-bar section in power chords at full gain. Then replace one chord, on the most important downbeat, with a rough voicing (a minor seventh, a minor-sixth dyad, a stack of fourths). Play both versions. Does the rough chord read as an arrival, or as a mistake? Try moving it to a weak beat and compare.

</Exercise>
