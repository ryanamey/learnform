## Every seam has a strength

Every boundary in a song tells the listener two things: *where we are* and *how much is over*. Belkin asks you to rate that second thing on a scale from 1 to 10 <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 5 Punctuating" />. A 1 is a barely noticeable breath inside a riff. A 10 is the end of the piece. Everything in between is how a large form gets its hierarchy: small stops inside phrases, bigger ones between sections, the biggest only at the end.

The rule that matters most for long songs: **the last punctuation must be the strongest, and most internal ones should be weak.** Belkin calls too much strong punctuation in mid-piece the most common fault in student work <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 18 Ending" />. Each full stop spends momentum, and you have to rebuild it from zero.

<KeyIdea>Rate every seam in your song 1–10. If any internal seam is as strong as the ending, either the song is two songs or that seam needs a “yes, but…”.</KeyIdea>

## What makes a stop strong

A cadence is never made by one dimension. Belkin's point is that **at least two dimensions** have to close together, and that it almost always involves rhythmic relaxation: longer notes, a landing on a strong beat, or a pause <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 5" />. The more dimensions close at once, the stronger the punctuation. Snyder reaches the same rule from memory research: closure can happen in any parameter, partial closure (some parameters close, others stay open) keeps music moving, and the more parameters close at once, the higher the level of the boundary <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 5 Closure" />.

What closes, dimension by dimension:

| Dimension | Closes when… | Stays open when… |
|---|---|---|
| **Melody / riff** | it falls; it lands by step after leaps | it rises (a question); it lands in a new register |
| **Harmony** | it reaches the tonal centre; it gets less dissonant | it lands on V, ♭VI or any “wrong” chord |
| **Rhythm** | notes lengthen; it lands on a downbeat | it keeps its subdivision; it stops on an offbeat |
| **Drums / texture** | layers drop out; a final hit | the groove keeps going; a layer enters |
| **Dynamics** | it gets softer (or one final loud hit after a build) | a sudden change: that's an interruption, not a close |

Snyder adds a general version: closure comes with a **drop in intensity** in any parameter (loudness, pitch, dissonance, brightness, speed, density), with longer durations, with silence, and with **return to something familiar** <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 5" />.

## “Yes, but…”: the weak cadence that drives form

The most useful seam in a long form is the one that closes *something* and leaves *something else* open. Belkin calls it “yes, but…” punctuation <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 5, Ch. 8" />. The riff lands, but the drums keep going. The chord resolves, but the bass walks into the next bar. The band stops, but on the wrong chord. One dimension contradicts the others, so the listener breathes without losing the thread.

Two more techniques from the same chapter:

- **Elision:** the last note of one phrase is the first note of the next. Used a lot in riff music without anyone naming it.
- **Silence:** works only after something incomplete. After an interrupted pattern, silence is suspense; the listener fills it with expectation. After a full cadence, silence just asks “is it over?”.

## Ending is not stopping

Caplin separates two things musicians constantly confuse <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 3, End vs. Stop; Ch. 5" />:

- **End vs. stop.** A cadence is the formal end of a harmonic and melodic process. A stop is the music ceasing to move. They often coincide, but a cadence can happen while the drums play straight through it, and the band can stop dead where nothing has ended. A band stop is not an ending.
- **Syntactical vs. rhetorical strength.** The type of landing decides how much is closed; loudness, the full band and the downbeat decide only how strongly it hits. A quiet landing on the home chord closes more than a huge crash on the dominant.
- **Holding the dominant doesn't end anything.** A passage that sits on the tense chord, however long and however it stops, has no cadence in it, because nothing moved to arrive.
- **A sequence is not a cadence.** The last link of a sequence repeats an earlier unit, so it can't work as a closing unit. Composers add a separate closing gesture after the sequence; without one, the passage simply stops in the middle.
- **Resolution is not arrival.** The big chord that follows a long held dominant usually *begins* the next section. The previous section ended earlier, when the dominant arrived.
- **Cadence vs. codetta.** The final bars of most pieces aren't cadences: they're short codettas after a landing that already happened.

Elision (the end of one unit is the start of the next) is one boundary process; Caplin adds the **lead-in**, a short link that belongs to neither phrase and could be removed, and **melodic overlap**, where a melody's last note falls on the next group's downbeat without that whole bar belonging to the first group.

## Loops and periods

Moore adds a distinction that riff bands need more than tonal theory does <Source book="moore12" where="Ch. 3, pp. 85–89" />. Songs sit on a continuum between two ways of organising time:

- **Open-ended repetition.** A pattern loops without closing. At the far end there are no sections at all (Lil' Louis's "French Kiss", John Lee Hooker's "Boogie Chillen'"); a little further in, a groove changes section at a point you can't predict (James Brown's "Sex Machine"). Riff cycles live near this end.
- **Period structure.** Phrases come in pairs that behave like question and answer, and **what matters is how each one ends**. The first ends open (on a chord other than home), the second closed. ABBA's "Take a Chance on Me" is the textbook case. Moore notes that open–closed is the norm, closed–closed happens (the second phrase of the Beatles' "Hey Jude" verse is richer and sounds *more* closed than the first), open–open loses some pull, and **closed–open is almost unknown**.

A **ballad structure** stretches the question and answer over four phrases: the first two set it up, the third wanders (Sinatra's "Strangers in the Night" starts it on a new chord and cycles round), and the fourth has to close all the harder.

The two methods mix, and the mixes are where the craft is:

- **Short-circuit a loop to close it.** Slade's "Thanks for the Memory" has a verse with no closed phrase at all; the chorus loops an open progression three times, and on the fourth pass cuts straight to the home chord. An open loop gets the closing power of a period from one change on its last repeat.
- **Give each section its own method.** Inspiral Carpets' "This Is How It Feels" loops an open pattern in the verse and closes in the chorus. Michelle Branch's "Here with Me" does the reverse: the chorus loops, while the verse repeats a pattern three times and then moves somewhere else, nodding towards a period.
- **Check the joins.** Moore's question for any loop: is the seam between one repeat and the next smooth or not, and why? <Source book="moore12" where="Ch. 11, Q 3.16" />

## Rock cadences: open, misplaced, rare

Stephenson's chapter on cadences describes what rock does with all this <Source book="stephenson" where="Ch. 3; Ch. 1 (V–I between units); Ch. 5 (antiperiodic structure)" />:

- **Open cadences are the norm, and they promise nothing.** A rock phrase can end on almost any chord (ii, iii, IV, ♭VI, ♭VII…), and many songs end every phrase on V and never close. Don't call these half cadences: a half cadence implies a full one is coming, and in rock it usually isn't. Degrees of finality colour a line or lead into the next one; they don't tell the listener where the song is going.
- **Resolution moves to the start.** Units typically begin on I and end on V, so the V–I happens *across* the seam, and the I that sounds like a resolution is also the launch of the next unit. Stephenson calls this a chain of overlapping propelling gestures: the style has no built-in way to end.
- **Melody and harmony keep separate appointments.** The melody may reach the home note over another chord, or arrive a little early or late; the full traditional V–I cadence with everything landing together is rare.
- **So a real cadence is an event.** Because closure is rare, the songs that use it get a strong effect from it. In Fleetwood Mac's "I'm So Afraid" the cadences come earlier and earlier through the song, and the first fully coordinated cadence arrives on the line about dying. A closing line placed at the *start* of a unit, where the music has to keep going, can sound unconvinced: Stephenson hears that in John Waite's "Missing You", where the singer insists he doesn't miss her.

For a riff band this is the default state: loops are open, and the downbeat that restarts the riff is both arrival and departure. The design question is where, if anywhere, the song is allowed to close.

<KeyIdea>A riff that loops is an open phrase. The listener is waiting for its answer. Decide which repeat gets the answer, and change only what's needed (often one chord, one note, or one bar shortened) to give it.</KeyIdea>

## Rating the seams of a song

Belkin rates real examples on the scale and uses the ratings as a design tool. Do the same with your songs:

<Steps>

1. List every boundary in the song: phrase ends, section ends, the end.
2. Rate each 1–10. Be honest: a full band stop with a ring-out is a 7 or 8, wherever it is.
3. Check the hierarchy. Phrase ends should be lower than section ends, section ends lower than the ending.
4. For any internal seam rated 7 or more, decide whether you want the song to restart there. If not, keep one dimension going (drums, a bass line, a held note, feedback) to bring it down to 4–5.

</Steps>

<ListenerSide grade="moderate">

Snyder's account of closure rests on memory research: boundaries create the chunks memory stores, and music with clear, hierarchical closure is retained far more easily than music without it (he cites Bigand). Belkin's ratings are a composer's judgement, not measured values. The two agree on the mechanism: more dimensions closing together means a bigger boundary.

</ListenerSide>
