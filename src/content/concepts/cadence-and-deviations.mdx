## Caplin's cadence

Caplin's account of the cadence is the most exact in this course, and the most limited in scope: it describes Classical style (Haydn, Mozart, Beethoven) <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 2–3" />. In it, a cadence is a **harmonic and syntactic** event: a **cadential progression** (pre-dominant, dominant in root position, tonic) under a melody that typically falls, hence the word, from the Latin *cadere*. There are only three basic kinds:

| Cadence | Harmony | Melody ends on | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Perfect authentic (PAC)** | V–I, both in root position | the tonic note | strongest |
| **Imperfect authentic (IAC)** | V–I | the third (or fifth) | weaker: the melody is open |
| **Half cadence (HC)** | ends on V | — | weakest: harmony and melody open |

He separates three things the word cadence can mean: the **cadential arrival** (the moment the final chord arrives), **cadential function** (the span that sets up the arrival) and the **cadential progression** (the chords, which can also appear where nothing ends). And he states that IV–I is not a genuine cadence in Classical style: the plagal cadence is, in his words, a myth, and IV–I after a real cadence is usually a codetta (see *Riff harmony*, where Lilja finds the opposite in metal).

<KeyIdea>Caplin: in Classical style a cadence is a harmonic formula of three kinds (PAC, IAC, HC). How loud or full it is changes its impact, not how much it closes.</KeyIdea>

## Syntactical and rhetorical strength

Two cadences of the same type close the same amount, says Caplin, whatever happens around them <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 3" />. Metrical position, dynamics, texture and register change only their **rhetorical** strength: how emphatic they sound. Their **syntactical** strength comes from the type alone. A quiet perfect authentic cadence closes more than a loud half cadence.

## End is not stop

Caplin's other key distinction <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 3, 5" />:

- **End versus stop.** A cadence is a formal end. A stop is the music ceasing to move. A cadence can happen while the accompaniment plays straight through it, and the music can stop where nothing has ended.
- **Holding the dominant ends nothing.** A passage standing on the dominant after a half cadence has no cadence in it, however long it lasts.
- **A sequence is not a cadence.** Its last link repeats an earlier unit and can't work as a closing unit; composers add a separate closing gesture after it.
- **Resolution is not arrival.** The tonic that follows a long dominant usually *begins* the next section; the previous one ended when the dominant arrived.

- **Abandoned cadence:** the cadential dominant itself fails, inverted or left out, so the progression never reaches its goal.

After a deviation the music usually backs up and tries again, which is one of the main ways phrases get longer in Classical style.

## Before the beginning, after the end

Caplin adds **framing functions** around themes <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 5" />:

- A **thematic introduction**: a bar or two, often just the accompaniment pattern starting before the melody (he calls one example a vamp).
- A **closing section** after an authentic cadence, made of **codettas**: short units circling the tonic note, often fragmenting, often leaning on the subdominant as a counterweight to the cadential dominant. He notes that the very last bars of a piece rarely contain cadences; they are codettas after a cadence that already happened.
- **Standing on the dominant** after a half cadence: holding the open state, and its energy, before what comes next.

He describes their energy with Wallace Berry's terms: introductions often have a **progressive** dynamic (building up), closing sections a **recessive** one (dissolving), though not always. *Cues and arrivals* takes up the build-up side.

Boundary processes between phrases: **elision** (the end of one unit is also the start of the next), the **lead-in** (a short link that belongs to neither phrase and could be removed) and **melodic overlap** (a melody's last note falls on the next downbeat without that bar belonging to the first group).

## Schoenberg's list is longer

Schoenberg names more cadences than Caplin admits: endings on I, V or III, with full, half, Phrygian or plagal cadences, perfect or imperfect, "according to its function" <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. VIII" />. He also describes the cadence as harmonic delimitation together with liquidation (see *Natural and learned closure*).

<Positions title="Cadence in the four books">
  <Position book="caplin" where="Ch. 2–5">Three kinds only (PAC, IAC, HC); harmonic and syntactic; end versus stop; syntactical versus rhetorical strength; deviations: deceptive, evaded, abandoned.</Position>
  <Position book="schoenberg" where="Ch. VII–VIII">Full, half, Phrygian, plagal; perfect or imperfect. Cadence equals harmonic delimitation plus liquidation of what demands continuation.</Position>
  <Position book="belkin" where="Ch. 5">Prefers "punctuation" because the definition of cadence isn't agreed. Harmony alone never makes one; two or more dimensions must coordinate.</Position>
  <Position book="snyder" where="Ch. 5">Cadences are syntactic closure: learned, style-specific, relatively arbitrary. His book deals with the natural kind instead.</Position>
</Positions>

## Practice

<Exercise title="Recompose a cadence">

1. Take a section ending from one of your songs that lands on its home chord.
2. Write three alternatives in Caplin's terms: a deceptive version (land on another chord, then repeat and land properly), an evaded version (the landing becomes the start of the phrase again), and a version with a codetta added after the landing.
3. Which one makes the section longer without making it feel padded?

</Exercise>
