## Bob Snyder, *Music and Memory: An Introduction*

Snyder taught at the School of the Art Institute of Chicago; *Music and Memory* was published by MIT Press in 2000. He wrote it for composition students who were mostly not trained in Western theory, and he wanted a less Eurocentric account, so he went to cognitive psychology for answers to "why" questions: why phrases, why falling motion sounds closural.

**How it was read for the app:** in full, preface through chapter 13 with all chapter notes, the postscript and the listening appendix; glossary and references skimmed, index not read. Figures are known only through the text.

## His question

**How do the structure and limits of human memory shape what we can hear as units, boundaries and form?** His answer is a three-level model: event fusion (echoic memory), melodic and rhythmic grouping (short-term memory), form (long-term memory). He doesn't offer a theory of named forms; he describes the conditions under which boundaries, closure and large-scale shape become perceptible and memorable.

He is explicit about limits: the field is evolving, many of its constructs are inferred, and he leaves out harmony, detailed timbre and emotion. He takes no side on aesthetics: music that works with memory and music that deliberately works against it are both, for him, legitimate.

## His toolkit

- **Time thresholds**: order clear up to about 5 events per second; patterns heard as wholes around 10; pitch fusion around 20 (Ch. 2, 10).
- **Short-term memory**: about 3–5 seconds, a handful of chunks; the phrase as the largest unit heard whole (Ch. 4).
- **Grouping**: proximity, similarity, continuity, intensification, parallelism, the tonal centre (Ch. 3).
- **Chunking and hierarchy**: chunks of chunks; recall enters at chunk boundaries (Ch. 3–4).
- **Closure**: graded; natural (falling, lengthening, pause) versus syntactic (learned formulas) (Ch. 5).
- **Habituation**: what is constant leaves attention; accompaniments are often more repetitive than foregrounds (Ch. 2).
- **Categories and nuance**: we perceive more than we remember; feel lives in nuance (Ch. 7).
- **Schemas**: slots and defaults; forms and genres as cultural schemas (Ch. 8).
- **Metaphor**: up and down, centre, path, container as ways we describe and invent music (Ch. 9).
- **Meter as schema**: graded downbeats, tension zones before them, long cycles needing landmarks (Ch. 12).
- **Primary versus secondary parameters** (Ch. 13).
- **Sections**: held together by constancy, separated by change in several parameters (Ch. 13).
- **Remembered versus clock time** (Ch. 13).
- **Linearity**: directed, nondirected, multiply directed, nonlinear; strategies that exploit or sabotage memory (Ch. 13).

## Where he agrees with the others

- Phrase size: Schoenberg's breath, Belkin's attention span, Caplin's four bars at ordinary tempos all fall in his short-term memory range.
- Rhythm as a carrier of identity, with Schoenberg.
- Closure graded and hierarchical, with Caplin and Belkin; boundaries stronger when several dimensions coincide, with Belkin.
- Return as homecoming, with all three; he adds the memory explanation.

## Where he differs

- **Large-scale tonal form.** He cites studies that challenge whether listeners hear long-range key plans, while allowing that experienced listeners may differ; Schoenberg and Caplin treat them as structural (Course IX).
- **Hierarchy.** For him an advantage for memory but not a requirement; nonhierarchical and memory-defeating music are legitimate aims (Course VII).
- **"Primary."** Pitch, harmony, rhythm, against Belkin's list. → *Seminar: Which dimensions carry a motive's identity?*
- **Clock proportions.** Proportions in seconds are not what listeners perceive, which bears on time-based rules such as climax at two thirds (Course VII).
- **Who is the experienced listener?** He thinks truly experienced listeners are less common than analysts assume.

## Where the app uses him

Course I (time levels, motive, phrase, memory); Course II (closure, boundaries); Course III (meter as schema); Course VII (time, linearity, proportion); Course XII (habituation, schemas, expectation).

## How to read him

Chapter 1 first; it holds the whole model. Then 3, 4 and 5 (grouping, short-term memory, closure), then 12 and 13 (rhythm, form). The listening appendix is worth working through with headphones; many examples are outside Western concert music.
