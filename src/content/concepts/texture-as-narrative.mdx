## When texture tells the story

The lessons so far treated texture as a tool for marking joints and building energy. Three studies of long rock pieces go further: they find texture carrying the *meaning* of a form, its large contrasts and its story.

## Chaos and order: "Close to the Edge"

John Covach's analysis of Yes's "Close to the Edge" reads its textures as a narrative <Source book="covach" where="Ch. 1 (Covach)" />. The introduction is deliberately overcomplicated and frantic: lead guitar lines scurry over a bass ostinato, interrupted by bursts of vocal "ah"s. Covach hears it as the chaos of the material world. Later comes a section with a completely different texture: sustained, floating sounds (Mellotron, fragments of guitar, piano, bass pedals) in a sea of reverb, which he hears as spiritual order. The contrast between verse and chorus inside the songs is reproduced at the largest level, so the whole piece becomes a two-part form built on two textures.

The point for writing is that a texture can stand for an idea through a whole piece. Once the listener has learned what the frantic texture and the floating texture "mean", their alternation and final balance carry the form.

## Layering motives at the climax

Smolko's analysis of *Thick as a Brick* follows its motives the way one follows leitmotifs in opera <Source book="smolko" where="Ch. 5" />. Motives come back on different instruments, in different registers and with different organ settings. At the climax near the end, two motives that had been heard separately are **layered**: one in contrary motion against the other, locking in sync. Then the band cuts out, leaving one motive alone.

This is a textural use of form: the climax is the moment when separate material is heard together. Hear the principle with two motives:

Smolko also describes how the album's style mixes are joined. He borrows two images: a **tapestry**, where different styles are blended with transitions, and a **quilt**, where the seams show (as on Zappa's *Freak Out!* or the Beatles' *White Album*). *Thick as a Brick*, for him, is closer to the tapestry. He finds four functions for its instrumental passages: **transition** (destabilising, fragmenting, changing tempo or meter, with instruments trading motives), **soloing**, **contrast** (abrupt changes of style, tempo, dynamics, key, meter or instrumentation) and **repetition** (keeping material going with new instrumentation or accents, like a Baroque ritornello).

## A mosaic of "feels": Brian Wilson

Daniel Harrison's essay on the Beach Boys' experimental music describes a different way of building a long form from textures <Source book="covach" where="Ch. 2 (Harrison)" />. Brian Wilson assembled songs from fragments he called **feels**, each with its own mood, fitted together like a **mosaic**. "Good Vibrations" joins six very different feels with radical breaks in key, texture, instrumentation and mood, and develops without a predetermined formal pattern.

Harrison adds an observation about attention. On *Smiley Smile*, Wilson used the same compositional devices without the dense production, and with little formal or harmonic development the listener's focus moves to **instrumentation, timbre and reverb**. When texture is absorbing enough, little attention is left for following the form. That cuts both ways for a band: a rich texture can hold a passage that has no development, but it can also hide a form the band wants the listener to follow.

## A timbre can be a theme

LaRue warns against judging themes only by melody <Source book="larue" where="Ch. 6, Thematic Relationship" />. All melodies can act as themes, he writes, but not all themes are melodies. In the nineteenth century a single striking rhythm, a "perfumed" chord or a shimmering timbre could be the central idea of a piece. Belkin makes the same point at the level of the motive <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 1" />: one note can be a motive if its timbre and articulation are distinctive, and the major-to-minor chord in Mahler's Sixth, with oboes and trumpets cross-fading, works as a **timbral motive**.

LaRue also notes why colour matters for form: it is easier to notice a theme's return if its tone colour also recalls the earlier appearance <Source book="larue" where="Ch. 2, Contributions of Sound to Shape" />.

<KeyIdea>Texture can carry a piece's large contrasts and its story (Covach), bring separate material together at a climax (Smolko), and even be the theme itself (LaRue, Belkin). A form can be built from textures as much as from tunes.</KeyIdea>

## Where the writers stand

<Positions title="Texture and the large form">
  <Position book="covach" where="Ch. 1 (Covach)">Contrasting textures can represent opposed ideas and organise a whole long piece.</Position>
  <Position book="smolko" where="Ch. 5">Motives return in new colours like leitmotifs; the climax layers them; instrumental passages have four functions.</Position>
  <Position book="covach" where="Ch. 2 (Harrison)">A form can be a mosaic of textural "feels"; an absorbing texture can take attention away from form.</Position>
  <Position book="larue" where="Ch. 2, 6">A timbre can be a theme; matching colour helps the listener recognise a return.</Position>
</Positions>

Covach and Smolko read the textures as meaning something (chaos and order, a journey). Harrison and LaRue stay with what the listener can attend to. That difference is real: how far a texture "means" anything beyond itself is a question of interpretation, which Course XIII takes up.

<ListenerSide grade="moderate">

These are analyses of particular pieces, not perceptual studies. The general claim that a return is easier to recognise when its timbre returns too is consistent with research Snyder reports on similarity and memory.

</ListenerSide>

## Practice

<Exercise title="Two textures, one story">

1. Choose two textures for a long piece: for example frantic and floating, or dense and bare.
2. Write down what each one stands for in this piece, in a word.
3. Plan where each appears and the one place where they meet or merge.

</Exercise>

<GuitarPro title="Layer two motives">

1. Write two short motives in different registers, one rising and one falling.
2. Use each one alone in a different section.
3. Write a climax section where both are heard together, and a final bar where one drops out.

</GuitarPro>

<ApplyIt>

Pick one sound in your band (a guitar-synth patch, a folk drum, a vocal timbre) and treat it as a theme: decide where it first appears, where it comes back, and what is different around it each time.

</ApplyIt>
