## Fred Lerdahl & Ray Jackendoff, *A Generative Theory of Tonal Music*

A composer and music theorist (Lerdahl) and a linguist (Jackendoff) wrote this book together. It was published by MIT Press in 1983 and is usually called GTTM. Modelled on linguistics, it tries to state as rules what an experienced listener unconsciously infers when hearing tonal music. There are three kinds of rules: well-formedness rules say what structures are possible, preference rules say which one is heard, and transformational rules handle exceptions.

**How it was read for the app:** our copy is a scan, so it was converted to text first. Ch. 2 (rhythmic structure) was read in full. Ch. 4 (meter) was read in large part: the rules, the tactus discussion, other metrical idioms and hypermetrical irregularities. Its worked examples were skimmed. Ch. 3 (grouping) was read in part: the rules, the perceptual motivation, parallelism, overlaps and the performer. Ch. 5–12 (the reductions) were not read for Course III.

## Their question

**What does a listener infer, and can it be written as rules?** For rhythm, the first step is to separate **grouping** (motives, phrases, sections) from **meter** (a regular pattern of strong and weak beats). Much of rhythm's richness comes from how the two interact.

## Their toolkit

- **Three kinds of accent**: phenomenal (on the surface), structural (beginnings and cadences), metrical (strong beats) (Ch. 2).
- **Beats are points; time-spans lie between them** (Ch. 2).
- **Tactus**: the level where you tap your foot, the most regular. Perception fades the further you go up or down from it (Ch. 2, 4).
- **Grouping preference rules**: proximity (rests, long notes), change (register, dynamics, articulation), intensification, symmetry, parallelism (Ch. 3).
- **Metrical preference rules**: parallelism, strong beat early, onsets, stress, length (harmonic rhythm the strongest), stable bass, cadences, suspensions, binary regularity (Ch. 4).
- **In phase / out of phase; anacrusis** (Ch. 2).
- **Structural anacrusis → structural downbeat** (Ch. 2).
- **Metrical deletion**: "the strong beat comes too soon" at an elision (Ch. 4).
- **Idiom-specific rules**: Balkan meters built from quick (2) and slow (3) beats; Stravinsky's changing meters (Ch. 4).

## Where they agree with the others

- With **Hasty, London and Snyder**: meter is "relatively local", and the 4-bar level is often open to interpretation.
- With **Yust**: hypermetrical irregularities come with elisions (Yust adopts their metrical deletion).
- With **London**: Balkan-type uneven meters are real meters, with their own rules.

## Where they differ

- **Beats as points** (against Hasty).
- **Grouping vs meter.** Yust renames their grouping "formal structure" and insists rhythm is grouping too.
- **Cadence on a strong beat.** For them it is a possible, dynamically charged "structural downbeat". For Yust it prevents closure unless the tonic fills the unit. See *Seminar: Does a cadence on the downbeat close or open?*
- **Odd meters.** Real meters for them, "rhythmic topoi" for Yust.
- **Accent.** Yust and Hasty criticise reasoning from accents.

## Where the app uses them

## How to read them

Read Ch. 2 first (rhythm without pitch), then Ch. 3–4 for the rules. Skip the reductions unless you want their tonal theory.
