## Jan LaRue, *Guidelines for Style Analysis*

LaRue (1918–2003) was a musicologist at New York University who specialised in the eighteenth-century symphony. *Guidelines for Style Analysis* (Norton, 1970) is a short handbook for describing any musical style systematically. It is written for analysts, but its vocabulary works just as well for describing what a band's sections sound like.

**How it was read for the app:** the copy is a scan, converted to text. Ch. 1 (fundamentals), 2 (Sound) and 6 (Growth) were read in full, except ch. 6's closing section on texts, which was skimmed. Ch. 3 (Harmony) was read to about p. 54; ch. 5 (Rhythm) in its parts on the layers of rhythm, textural rhythm and small dimensions; ch. 4 (Melody) was only searched for passages on texture; ch. 7 was not read. The music examples are images, so they are known only from their captions.

## His question

**How can an analyst describe what is characteristic of a style, in every element and at every scale, without drowning in detail?**

## His toolkit

- **SHMRG**: Sound, Harmony, Melody, Rhythm, and Growth (form), which is the result of the others. Growth has two sides, **Movement** and **Shape**; "Shape is the memory of Movement" (Ch. 1, 6).
- **Large, middle and small dimensions** (whole works, sections and phrases, motives and details) (Ch. 1).
- **Typology, Movement, Shape**: the routine applied to every element (Ch. 1).
- **Three states of motion**: stability, local activity, directional motion (Ch. 1).
- **Sound**: timbre, dynamics, and **texture vs fabric**; **linear vs massive**; **degree and frequency of contrast**; **terrace vs tapered dynamics**; **reverse idiom** (Ch. 2).
- **The Rule of Three**: every pair of opposites gets a middle grade (Ch. 1, 2).
- **Three layers of rhythm**: continuum, surface rhythm, interactions; **textural, timbral and dynamic rhythm**; contour rhythm (Ch. 2, 5).
- **Articulation and its weight** (duration, number of elements, concinnity); **stratification, elision, truncation, lamination**; **dimensional stratification**; **reversibility** (Ch. 6).
- **Concinnity**: the coordination of elements (Ch. 1, 6).

## Where he agrees with the others

- With **Snyder** and **Belkin**: a joint weighs more when more elements change together. Snyder gets there from memory research, Belkin from composing, LaRue from analysis.
- With **Schoenberg** and **Belkin**: form grows out of the material rather than being a container. LaRue's word "Growth" was chosen to avoid the static word "form".
- With **Moore** and **Lavengood**: timbre and texture need their own vocabulary.

## Where he differs

- **Sound's role.** For him Sound is mostly secondary for motion but primary for marking joints. Meyer (as Snyder reports him) calls these "secondary parameters"; Moore makes texture the starting point for rock. See *Seminar: Is texture primary or secondary?*
- **Counterpoint.** His test for real counterpoint is roughly equal activity plus overlapping articulation (one line keeps going over another's cadence). Yust defines counterpoint as independence plus synthesis into one structure. This will be a seminar in the planned *Counterpoint* lesson.
- **Hypermeter.** He doesn't claim large units are metrical, but argues that changes of scoring can make them audible. Compare *Seminar: Is hypermeter real above two bars?*

## How to read him

Read ch. 1, then ch. 2 (Sound) and ch. 6 (Growth). The other element chapters can be read when they're needed. He wants the framework used as a background for noticing significant things, not as a checklist to fill in.
