## The question

A riff repeats sixteen times with nothing changing. Is the music standing still or moving? Groove-based music depends on the answer, and the writers disagree.

## Snyder: identical repetition is static, and that can be the point

Snyder describes progressive change as the strongest kind of musical motion, and identical repetition as not progressive: a nonlinear pattern sets up fixed assumptions rather than a developing set of expectations <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 5, 9" />. Repeated, constant material habituates and moves into the background <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 2" />. But he doesn't rank the options: low-information strategies (much repetition, very similar gestures) are a legitimate aim, and music can repeat in some parameters while changing in others <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 13" />. He also places feel, the systematic nuance of timing, below the level that memory keeps, which is why recordings stay fresh <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 7, 12" />, and he cites Charles Keil's idea of participatory discrepancies.

## Belkin: repetition needs variation and progression

Literal repetition ten times is boring; too much variation is incoherent <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 1" />. His chapter on progressing asks for incremental change toward goals, and Western music, he says, is strongly directional, with a qualification in his notes <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 13" />.

## Schoenberg: repetition alone is monotony

Intelligibility needs repetition; repetition without variation becomes monotony <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. III, V" />.

## Kramer: different kinds of time

Kramer describes **vertical time**, one extended present with no phrase hierarchy and no climax, as a way of listening in its own right, alongside linear time <Source book="kramer" where="Ch. 2" />. Which mode you hear depends partly on the listener.

## Garza and London: feel is structure

Garza treats time feel (half, normal, double, quarter time) as something that changes while a riff stays <Source book="garza" />. London argues that systematic timing patterns are part of the meter itself, so two performances of one rhythm can be different meters <Source book="london" where="Ch. 9" />.

## Hasty: the diffuse present

Hasty adds a view from the other end <Source book="hasty" where="Ch. 14–16" />. In a long repetitive stretch, such as the end of Schütz's motet or much post-war music, projection weakens. Phrases lose their boundaries, and we find ourselves in a "diffuse present" of uncertain extent. He doesn't call this motion or stasis. He argues that whatever vividness such music has comes from the variety we manage to attend to, and that large-scale homogeneity risks dullness through too little novelty, not too much. He also rejects the claim that such moments are "timeless": a vivid now needs a past and a future.

## Hear the question

Loop any riff example in Course III for a full minute without changing anything. Then loop it again and change only the cymbal pattern after 30 seconds (*Time feel*). Did the first loop move?

## Where they agree

Something has to change somewhere for attention to stay. Repetition makes material learnable.

## Where they differ

- **Is a repeated groove motion?** Snyder: not progressive motion, though it may carry life in nuance. Belkin and Schoenberg: it needs variation. Kramer: it can be a different kind of time. London and Garza: feel and timing are part of the structure.
- **Is stasis a fault?** Belkin and Schoenberg lean toward yes; Snyder and Kramer treat it as one legitimate option.
