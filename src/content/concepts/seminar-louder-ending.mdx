## The question

Does a louder, fuller, heavier landing close *more*? Bands often end a section with everything on the downbeat at full volume. The writers disagree about what that does.

## Caplin: louder is more emphatic, not more closed

Caplin separates **syntactical strength** from **rhetorical strength** <Source book="caplin" where="Ch. 3" />. How much a cadence closes depends only on its type: perfect authentic closes more than imperfect authentic, which closes more than a half cadence. Metrical position, dynamics, texture and register change how emphatic the cadence sounds, not how much it closes. A soft perfect authentic cadence closes more than a fortissimo half cadence.

## Belkin: every dimension counts, and loudness cuts both ways

Belkin rates punctuation by weighing all the dimensions together, dynamics and texture included <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 5" />. In his rating of the opening of Beethoven's *Appassionata*, a stop that is low, thick and held under a fermata rates 6–7, but its sudden quiet and its inverted dominant keep it from rating higher. He describes how dynamics work at a cadence: a falling figure with a diminuendo closes; a crescendo into the cadence is **propulsive and climactic**; a sudden change of level is an interruption, which creates suspense. At the end of a piece, he says, the final moments need all dimensions to agree, and both a climactic ending and a fade-out reach an extreme (loudest or softest, highest or lowest) so that nothing further seems possible <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 18" />.

## Snyder: closure comes with falling intensity

Snyder counts loudness among the parameters of intensity, along with pitch height, dissonance, brightness, speed and density <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 5" />. A group feels more closed the more its intensity falls at the end. On that account, a louder landing raises intensity and works against closure, unless other parameters fall more. He describes directed motion as heading toward a maximum or a minimum: a climax or a resolution.

## Schoenberg: a climax recedes

Schoenberg's cadence contour has the melody recede from its climax to the middle register before the cadence <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. VII" />.

## Hear the question

Compare example 2 (*Falling and lengthening*) and example 6 (*The Aeolian cadence*) in *Natural and learned closure*. Both end on a long E with a crash. In 2 the pitch falls before the landing; in 6 the power chords rise to it. Then play any section ending of your own twice, once with the full band landing loud and once with the band dropping to the bass alone. Which closes more?

## Where they agree

Endings are graded. Loudness changes how an ending is heard. The very last moment of a piece needs more than one dimension to close.

## Where they differ

- **Does loudness affect how much is closed?** Caplin: no, only how emphatically. Belkin: yes, as one dimension among several, in either direction depending on how it's used. Snyder: rising loudness is rising intensity, which works against closure.
- **Climactic endings.** Belkin treats a climactic final ending as a legitimate extreme. Snyder's intensity account explains the fade-out more directly than the climactic ending.
