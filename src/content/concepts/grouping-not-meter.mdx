## Two things we hear at once

Lerdahl & Jackendoff open their account of rhythm with a distinction they think theory has often blurred <Source book="gttm" where="Ch. 2" />. While listening, we do two things at once:

- We cut the music into **groups**: motives, phrases, sections. This is where a singer breathes and where a band naturally stops.
- We infer a **meter**: a regular pattern of strong and weak beats. This is where the foot taps.

The two are independent. A group can start anywhere in the bar and last any length; a meter goes on regardless of where the groups start. Two of their claims follow:

- **Groups don't receive metrical accent.** A phrase is not "strong" or "weak"; only its beats are. They criticise Cooper and Meyer, whose prosodic analysis gave whole groups an accent and so ended up giving beats a duration.
- **Beats don't belong to groups.** In meter alone, the fourth beat of a 4/4 bar belongs no more to the third beat than to the next downbeat; a meter is "like wallpaper". Whether a weak beat is an **upbeat** (grouped with what follows) or an **afterbeat** (grouped with what precedes) depends on where the grouping boundary falls.

## How we hear group boundaries

GTTM's **grouping preference rules** describe what makes a boundary <Source book="gttm" where="Ch. 3" />. They were modelled on the Gestalt laws of visual grouping:

1. Avoid very small groups, single notes especially.
2. **Proximity**: a boundary falls after a rest or a long note, or where the gap between attacks is larger.
3. **Change**: a boundary falls where register, dynamics, articulation or note length changes.
4. **Intensification**: where these effects are larger, a larger-level boundary falls.
5. **Symmetry**: prefer groups that divide into two equal halves.
6. **Parallelism**: parallel passages form parallel parts of groups. They often start alike and diverge toward a cadence.
7. **Stability**: prefer groupings that make the harmony and voice leading more stable.

When the rules agree, we hear clear groups. When they conflict, the grouping is ambiguous. They give rough relative strengths (a rest or slur break is stronger than a gap between attacks; a change of dynamics is stronger than a gap) and refuse to put numbers on them. Parallelism, they write, is the most important and the least formalised.

## In phase and out of phase

Once both structures are present, they can line up or not <Source book="gttm" where="Ch. 2" />. If a group starts on its strongest beat, grouping and meter are **in phase**. If it starts before its strongest beat, they are **out of phase**. The stretch from the group's start to its strongest beat is its **anacrusis**. A short anacrusis (one pickup note) puts them only slightly out of phase. A long one, as in the opening of Mozart's Symphony 40 where the theme drives toward a strong beat two bars in, puts them acutely out of phase, which is harder for the listener to process and more energetic.

## Two other ways of putting it

**Yust** accepts the distinction but renames it <Source book="yust" where="Ch. 6" />. What GTTM calls grouping, he calls **formal structure**: grouping by repetition and contrast. Meter, he says, is also a kind of grouping, by **regularity**. So the real distinction is between grouping by regularity (rhythmic structure) and grouping by repetition and contrast (form). He adds that repetition tends to keep the two roughly in step at the phrase level, which is why they are so easily confused. Moving a phrase into and out of phase with the hypermeter is an expressive tool: Haydn's "Mercury" Symphony changes from in-phase to upbeat phrasing as the theme turns from assertive to lyrical.

**Hasty** starts from somewhere else <Source book="hasty" where="Ch. 9" />. Instead of strong and weak beats plus separate groups, he speaks of **beginning**, **continuation** and **anacrusis** inside one process. An anacrusis is a continuation that comes to be directed toward a new beginning; it exists only once that beginning arrives. For him, metrical accent and grouping can't be peeled apart as two structures, because both come from the same act of attending to durations.

## Where the writers stand

<Positions title="Grouping and meter">
  <Position book="gttm" where="Ch. 2–3">Two independent structures; groups have no accent and beats no grouping; their interaction makes upbeats, afterbeats and cadences.</Position>
  <Position book="yust" where="Ch. 1, 6">Meter is grouping by regularity, form is grouping by repetition and contrast; phase between them is expressive.</Position>
  <Position book="hasty" where="Ch. 7, 9">Beginning, continuation and anacrusis belong to one process; the two-structure model splits what we hear as one.</Position>
  <Position book="snyder" where="Ch. 3, 12">Grouping by proximity and similarity happens before meter; a group that starts or ends off the downbeat adds metrical tension.</Position>
  <Position book="caplin" where="Ch. 2">Grouping and meter are distinct; formal functions are defined by grouping and harmony, not by meter.</Position>
</Positions>

<ListenerSide grade="strong">

Grouping by proximity and change is among the best-supported findings in music perception, and it parallels visual grouping. The preference rules are a theory, and GTTM itself says they can't be given exact weights.

</ListenerSide>

## Practice

<Exercise title="Move the boundary, keep the meter">

1. Play a riff of yours with the drums unchanged.
2. Make three versions that move its group boundary using only rests, long notes or a change of register.
3. For each, decide whether the weak beat before the boundary is now an upbeat or an afterbeat.

</Exercise>

</GuitarPro>
