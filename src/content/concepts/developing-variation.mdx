## Variants that have children

Schoenberg separates two kinds of change <Source book="schoenberg" where="Ch. III" />. A **variant** is a local decoration: it changes a detail and has no consequences. **Developing variation** is different. Each new form of the motive grows out of the *previous* form, not just the original, and the changes have consequences for what comes next. The result feels like growth rather than repetition with decoration.

He compares a piece to a photograph album showing one subject's life under changing circumstances. The order of the photos isn't chronological, though. It follows what the listener can understand.

<KeyIdea>In developing variation, each idea is the child of the previous one. Coherence comes from the chain of descent, not from resemblance to the original.</KeyIdea>

## Hear a chain

Each step below is derived from the step before it. Compare D3 directly with M: there is almost no surface resemblance left. Yet played in order, nothing sounds arbitrary.

<ExampleList set="developing-chain" />

This is the key mechanism behind long progressive pieces. Ten minutes of music can come from one cell without repeating it literally, because every new idea is reached through a series of medium (2–3) steps instead of one jump to a 5.

## Two tools for keeping the chain audible

**1. Double motives.** Belkin shows that a motif made of two clearly different parts (call them *a* and *b*) can carry a long passage just by reordering them: *a b*, *a a b*, *b a*, *a b b a* <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 1, Double motives" />. That gives much more variety than a single cell while staying close to the source.

<ExampleList set="double-motive" />

Motif M is already a double motive. *a* is the stutter-and-rise, and *b* is the fall from the tritone. A riff section can run for a long time on combinations of these two.

**2. Wandering, then a clear return.** Belkin analyses a Bach allemande where the motif drifts through increasingly remote forms, even introducing a contrasting syncopated figure. It works for three reasons:
- the new figure is prepared by details just before it
- it arrives at a point where new material is natural (after a cadence, or at a peak)
- the music then returns to a **literal** statement of the original

The rule he draws: the listener needs regular doses of easy familiarity to follow the argument without strain. After a wander, *pay the listener back* with a clear return.

## Ornamentation: one variant, a whole section

The opposite strategy is to make one decorated version of the motif and keep it as the basis of an entire section <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 1, Motivic ornamentation" />. The connection is heard once, at the switch. After that the new version sets its own rules. Variation forms work this way, and so do many songs where a later section is "the same riff, but…" played its new way for a long stretch.

<ListenerSide grade="moderate">

Variation is only heard as development if the source is still remembered (Snyder: long-term memory keeps a sketch, not detail). That is why Belkin's wandering example ends with a clear recall of the original, and why a chain of variants needs a clear return now and then. Without one, listeners don't follow a family tree, they hear a string of new ideas.

</ListenerSide>

## Practice

<Exercise title="A five-generation family tree">

1. Start with a source motif in the Motif bank.
2. Create a child at rating 2. Then a child of the *child* at 2–3. Keep going for five generations, each one derived only from its parent.
3. Play generation 5 against the source. If it's rated 4–5 against the source but each step was 2–3, you've built a developing-variation chain.
4. Try the reverse: compose a passage that wanders out through the chain and then returns to the source literally. Does the return feel earned?

</Exercise>

<ApplyIt>

Pick one cell from your motif bank and derive three generations from it, each with a different transformation. Could the third generation sit in a different song from the first? Draw the family tree.

</ApplyIt>
