## Small events that make a boundary land

Emily Schwitzgebel studied 200 Hot 100 songs and identified **textural cues**: brief, novel, local events, just before a section boundary, that signal a coming change in texture <Source book="schwitzgebel" where="§1" />. Their suddenness creates tension. When the predicted change arrives, it releases. Her texture model uses Allan Moore's four layers (explicit beat, functional bass, melody, harmonic filler), adds Lavengood's novelty layer as a fifth, and treats **production** as something that changes any layer: a low-pass filter makes a section sound thin, sidechain compression makes the beat pump.

Why the cue works so close to the boundary: listeners hold only the last two or three seconds clearly in mind (seven or eight at most), so a local signal is what reminds them, just in time, of a form they already know. In Huron's terms the cue triggers tension and prediction; the change itself is the reaction.

<Steps title="The four cues">

1. **Acceleration:** rhythmic activity increases, usually in the beat layer (kick or snare), into the new section.
2. **Withholding:** one or more layers drop out suddenly. Pulling the beat just before a drop is the classic case.
3. **Soar / swoop:** a fast rise (a "riser") or fall in pitch, often in the harmonic filler or as a production effect.
4. **Upbeat:** a new event on the upbeat just before the downbeat: a single hit, a short fill, a melodic pickup.

</Steps>

Cues can be **compounded** (a soar, then withholding, then an upbeat) for more tension.

**How common they are.** She coded every section change in 100 hits from 1958–1991 and 100 from 1992–2021. In the older songs about 28% of section changes were cued; in the newer ones about 61%, a significant difference, rising steadily year by year <Source book="schwitzgebel" where="§2, Ex. 3–5" />. She links this to the shift in pop from harmony to texture and production as the main way of shaping form. Caveat: she coded the songs herself, knowing her hypothesis, and the samples are 100 songs each.

### How the four example songs ration their cues

- **Not every boundary.** In Glass Animals' "Heat Waves" the move from the first chorus back to the verse, and the start of the second verse, are uncued: the listener already knows that part of the form. The bridge entry is usually uncued in all her examples, because a bridge should arrive as a surprise.
- **Change the cue type to keep it new.** "Heat Waves" uses withholding and an upbeat tom hit, then later a soar instead; the biggest moment stacks all three (a soar, a full bar of silence, then the tom).
- **Cue the drop as well as the rise.** Ed Sheeran's "Bad Habits" signals the return to the verse too: a full bar with everything withheld and a quiet swoosh.
- **A cue can lie.** A cue promises a full section; delivering a thin one instead (see *Where the climax goes*) turns the promise into suspense.

The songwriters she quotes describe the same thing in plain words: Sheeran's co-writers on "Shape of You" talk about something coming in or dropping out whenever it starts to get boring, building and dropping, tension and release; Charlie Puth explains a song where the music goes quiet and then panned snares tell you it's about to get loud again <Source book="schwitzgebel" where="[4.3]–[4.4]" />.

<KeyIdea>A cue is a promise about the next section. Keep the promise for release; break it for surprise. Don't cue every boundary.</KeyIdea>

## How to use it

<Steps>

- **Restraint.** In Schwitzgebel's examples many boundaries go uncued. A cue at every join makes the song predictable.
- **Contrast.** A cue hits hardest when the texture before it differs clearly from the texture after it.
- **Match the cue to the instrument.** Your drummer owns acceleration and upbeat. The guitar synth owns soar/swoop (and modern effects can do it without any notes). Withholding is a band decision: who drops out?
- **Cue with meter.** Module 2 gives you cues the pop corpus doesn't have: a partial bar, a hemiola, a riff cut off mid-cycle. A metric cue at the boundary does the same job as acceleration.

</Steps>

<ListenerSide grade="moderate">

Schwitzgebel argues that cues work largely below conscious awareness and carry little cognitive load, because they operate within a short listening window. This fits Huron's expectation theory and Deliège's finding that listeners abstract short, distinctive cues to build a sense of a piece's structure, familiar or not. Her own evidence is a corpus count plus analyses of her own hearing, not a listening experiment, so treat the mechanism as well-motivated rather than proven.

</ListenerSide>
