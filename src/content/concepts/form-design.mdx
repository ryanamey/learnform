## Principles, not moulds

Belkin's conclusion is the reason this module exists. Mature composers don't pour music into standard forms; they think in **formal principles**: presenting, contrasting, connecting, progressing, returning, ending. His chapter titles are verbs because these are things the music has to *do* <Source book="belkin" where="Conclusion" />. The textbook forms are practice examples of those principles. That's what makes the approach usable for a band like yours: the questions (how to begin, how much contrast, how to return, how to end) are the same in any style.

Two consequences:

- **Parts aren't interchangeable.** Even an identical passage changes meaning depending on where it falls, because memory and expectation have changed.
- **Every piece has a frame**: beginning, continuing, ending. Each needs its own design.

<KeyIdea>Design the whole from a few decisions: does material return, where is the climax, how does it end, and what holds it together.</KeyIdea>

## The coordination principle

Belkin's central craft rule: **no single dimension convinces on its own** <Source book="belkin" where="Conclusion" />. A high note with no support from harmony, texture or dynamics sounds like a mistake; a timbre change mid-phrase with nothing else changing sounds like a wrong entry. But coordination is a dial, not a switch:

- Only at a **major climax** or the **ending** should all dimensions point the same way.
- Everywhere else, controlling *how many* dimensions move together is how you fine-tune intensity and mood.
- With no coordination at all, nothing lands.

This is the thread running through the whole module: punctuation strength, contrast size and climax strength are all, at bottom, *how many dimensions agree at once*.

## Two questions that define the shape

Module 3 asked where the climax goes. Add a second question: **does material come back after contrast?** Together they give you a design space. The table is my synthesis of Module 3's climax shapes with Osborn's work, not a table from any one source.

| | Material returns | No return (through-composed) |
|---|---|---|
| **Climax inside** (on a returning section) | Verse–chorus with a telos chorus; rondo-like riff returns | Rare: a peak with no later return leaves the end to fade or stop |
| **Climax at the end** | Final chorus as peak; a stairstepper | **Terminally climactic** form: a new section, once, at the end (Osborn's “Pushit”) |
| **No single climax** | Process pieces, trance-like cycles | Concatenated riffs (much math-metal); post-rock that thins out |

Osborn's 2011 study gives the through-composed column its own taxonomy <Source book="osborn11" />. He calls a piece through-composed when no clearly identifiable section returns after contrasting material (immediate repetition is allowed). Two questions sort it:

- **One part or grouped?** Is the piece one continuous span, or divided into **section groups** that share a character (texture, rhythm, tonal centre)?
- **One theme or many?** Is the material unified (sections share melodic, harmonic or rhythmic material) or diverse?

| Type | Shape | What holds it together | Osborn's genre tendency |
|---|---|---|---|
| I · one part, one theme | One idea developed through timbre, loudness, rhythm | The single idea; often a cumulative build | Post-rock |
| II · grouped, one theme | Several groups, each on related material | Shared material; groups distinguished by groove and texture | |
| III · one part, many themes | Riffs and grooves strung one after another | Genre logic: groove, intensity, density; abrupt endings | Math-metal and metalcore |
| IV · grouped, many themes | Groups of diverse sections | Timbre, rhythm, texture, key — not theme. Sections can work *as* verses or choruses without returning | |

Two of his points matter most for you. First, many through-composed pieces **save their most memorable material for the end**, because without a return something else has to provide closure; that's the link to the terminal climax. Second, type IV shows that sections can keep their **function** (verse-like, chorus-like) without repeating their material. His caveat: these are categories for analysis, not recipes, and real pieces blend them. Tool appears only in a footnote, where several tracks on *Ænima* are called mostly through-composed.

## Memory sets the limits

Snyder's reminder for any large plan: short-term memory holds a few seconds and a few chunks; beyond that, listeners keep a **sketch** — the boundaries, the landmarks, the salient moments — not the details <Source book="snyder" where="Ch. 1, Ch. 5" />. Belkin says the same thing from the composer's side: listeners remember the beginning, the biggest climaxes and contrasts, and the ending, so the composer must make the important moments salient enough to be remembered <Source book="belkin" where="Ch. 18" />. Module 5's *What listeners actually hear* covers the evidence that proportions and long-range key plans are barely perceived. Design for the ear, scaffold for yourself.

Kramer's ideas about **moment form** (independent sections with no overall direction) and proportion belong here too, but *The Time of Music* hasn't been read yet, so they're left out until it has.

## A sketching method

Belkin's appendix on sketching describes how to grow a form from ideas instead of fitting ideas into a mould <Source book="belkin" where="Appendix A" />. Adapted for a band working remotely:

<Steps>

1. **One idea per card** (one file, one riff-board entry). Don't combine yet.
2. **Explore each idea alone**: close and remote variants. Ask how it could appear later in the opposite character — the lyrical idea as a full-band explosion, the heavy riff as a clean whisper.
3. **Plan each idea's career**: for example, heard twice in its first character, then once transformed as a surprise. Some ideas are seeds for a later turning point.
4. **Extend** each idea into a few phrases.
5. **Shuffle the cards.** Lay them out and try different orders; Belkin mentions Stravinsky's table of paper scraps. a → b and b → a can differ enormously in impact.
6. **Decide each join**: sudden contrast or transition, and how big (contrast scale).
7. **Make a rough draft of the whole** and listen straight through. Check: an idea too short or too long, a transition too slow or too sudden, not enough return to balance, a missing intro or coda.
8. **Take a day or two off**, then listen as if it were someone else's.
9. Only then fill in details.

</Steps>
