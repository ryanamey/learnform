## Meter is layers of pulse

Krebs defines meter as the union of all regularly recurring layers of motion in a piece <Source book="krebs" where="Ch. 2" />. There's a fast **pulse layer** (in your music, usually sixteenths), occasional faster **micropulses** (a triplet figure against straight sixteenths), and slower **interpretive layers** that group the pulses. An interpretive layer is created by any regular accent: dynamic, durational (long notes), registral (high or low points), density (thick chords), a pitch dissonance, an ornament, or a change of chord or riff ("new-event" accents). Repetition creates layers too: a repeated pitch, and above all a repeated pattern. A layer that groups pulses in 4s is a "4-layer", one that groups them in 3s a "3-layer", and so on.

Not every layer is heard equally. Listeners focus on the layers marked by the most features at once, and two kinds of accent can carry a layer on their own: **chord changes** and **dynamic accents**. A layer made only by slurs or phrasing, without accents, is weak. For a band this is the practical rule: if you want an antimetrical layer to be heard, put a chord change or a hit on it; if you want it to be felt underneath, leave it to phrasing.

When the layers line up, the meter is **consonant**. When they don't, it's **dissonant**. Krebs names two kinds, and Biamonte adapts both to rock <Source book="biamonte" where="§1–2" />.

<KeyIdea>Grouping dissonance: layers of different lengths (3 against 4). Displacement dissonance: layers of the same length, out of phase (4 against 4, shifted).</KeyIdea>

## Grouping dissonance: G x/y

Two layers whose lengths aren't multiples of each other. Krebs labels it with the larger number first: a 4-layer against a 3-layer is **G4/3**. Three properties are worth knowing by heart:

- **They realign periodically.** The cycle is the product of the two lengths (divided by any common factor). G4/3 realigns every 12 pulses; G5/4 every 20.
- **Non-alignment dominates.** Between alignments there are many more clashing pulses than agreeing ones, which is why grouping dissonance feels unstable even though it's perfectly regular.
- **The bar line has its own cycle.** A 3-sixteenth riff realigns with the *beat* every 3 beats, but with the *bar* only every 3 bars. That longer cycle is where the large-scale tension comes from.

## Displacement dissonance: D x+y

Two layers of the *same* length, offset from each other. A riff that accents every 4 sixteenths but starts one sixteenth late is **D4+1**. Displacement never realigns by itself; every pulse of the meter is contradicted until you move the layer back. Ordinary syncopation is one form of it (displacement made by long notes), but Krebs counts any displaced accent.

Displacement is usually heard *forward* from the beat (D4+1: one pulse late). When the displaced accent sits near the end of the bar and belongs to the next chord, it's heard *backward*, as an early arrival, and Krebs writes it with a minus: D4−1. Harmony decides which: a displaced hit that continues the previous chord sounds late, one that already has the next chord sounds early.

## Patterns, families and strength

Three more tools from Krebs's taxonomy <Source book="krebs" where="Ch. 2" />:

- **Resultant rhythms.** Add up the attacks of both layers and you get a rhythm. For grouping dissonance it's a palindrome inside each cycle (G3/2 over six pulses: 2-1-1-2); for displacement it alternates two values (D4+3 gives 3-1-3-1, D5+2 gives 2-3-2-3). That resultant is often the riff you actually hear.
- **Families.** G3/2, G6/4 and G9/6 have the same resultant at different speeds, so they belong together; so do D2+1, D4+2 and D8+4. Related dissonances can link sections. Displacements with the same offset but different lengths are **tight** or **loose** relatives: D2+1 contradicts every beat, D8+1 only one beat in eight.
- **How strong a dissonance is** depends on the pattern itself (longer cycles are more intense; a displacement by one pulse either side of the beat is the most intense; tight is more intense than loose) and even more on presentation: dynamic or chord-change accents, grouping and accents together, antimetrical notes **held through** the beat rather than released, the displaced part in an outer voice or several parts, and a long continuous run of it. Strangely, accenting *every* possible displacement at once cancels the effect: when every pulse is accented, nothing is out of place.

Two more distinctions matter in practice. A dissonance is **simple** with two conflicting layers and **compound** with three or more, for example a 2-layer and a displaced 3-layer against the meter. And it can sit at the **low level** (inside the beat: a triplet against straight eighths), the **mid level** (beats against the bar, where most of the action is), or the **high level** (hypermeter: three-bar groups against four).

### When the meter itself goes silent

Krebs's most useful term for odd-meter writing is **subliminal dissonance**: everything the band plays expresses only the antimetrical layer, and the meter is implied only by context (what came before, the counting, the drummer's head). A guitar figure in groups of three with no backbeat underneath, inside a song in 4, is subliminal G4/3. It's fragile: after a few bars the listener simply hears the new grouping as the meter. To keep the conflict alive, establish the meter strongly first, keep reminding the listener of it (a kick on the real 1, one recurring passage that restates it), and bring it back clearly at the end.

The shorter cousin is **indirect dissonance**: when one grouping simply follows another, the listener carries the old one on for a few pulses, so even a plain switch from 4 to 7 produces a brief clash at the join. A direct dissonance therefore never has a hard edge either; it fades through an indirect stage on both sides.

## Rhythm, meter and hypermeter behave differently

Biamonte changes one thing in Krebs's model for rock <Source book="biamonte" where="§1–2" />. Krebs counts every regular layer as part of the meter. In rock, the drums and rhythm guitar spell out the bar so clearly that she keeps **rhythm** and **meter** apart, and distinguishes three levels:

- **Rhythmic dissonance** happens at the beat and below (a syncopated vocal, a 3-against-4 guitar figure over a steady kit). It adds tension, but the bar survives. This is everyday rock.
- **Metric dissonance** breaks the bar itself: an extra or missing beat, a change of meter, a downbeat that moves.
- **Hypermetric dissonance** breaks the habit of four-bar groups: a three-bar phrase, an eleven-bar bridge.

The higher the level, the rarer the dissonance. Listeners default to groups of two and four at large scales, so odd groupings are common inside the bar and increasingly rare above it. A strong dissonance at one level can also disturb the level above.

Three more distinctions sharpen the labels:

- **Direct or indirect.** Direct dissonance is simultaneous (drums in 4 against guitar in 6). Indirect dissonance is successive: a section in 7 next to a section in 4. In rock, most metric dissonance is **indirect** and **split** between instruments, not inside one part.
- **Measure-preserving or tactus-preserving** (Waters). In a grouping dissonance either the barlines still line up and the beats don't, or the beats line up and the barlines don't. Yes's "Roundabout" has drums in 4/4 against guitars in 6/4 with the bars preserved.
- **Odd meters are consonant; irregular changes aren't.** A steady 7/4 or a regular alternation of 3/4 and 4/4 has a larger cycle the body can learn (London calls these non-isochronous meters: uneven groups of 2 and 3 inside an even cycle). It is consonant, but less stable than 4/4. A meter that keeps changing with no larger period can't be entrained, and that is real instability.

For a band that lives in odd meters this last point matters most: 7/4 played steadily isn't tension by itself. Tension comes from **contrast** with something more regular next to it, or from changes the body can't predict.

## Making a changing meter legible

If bars keep changing length, the listener needs help to find them. Biamonte's analysis of the verse of Rush's "Free Will" shows how <Source book="biamonte" where="§4" />. The bars run 6, 7, 6, 7, 6, 8 beats, and the pattern repeats only at the middle of the verse, so neither the meter nor the hypermeter lines up with the form. It's still easy to follow, because both edges of every bar are marked:

- **the start**, by the same melodic motive (a rising fifth and a note anticipating beat 2);
- **the end**, by the drummer, who drops the backbeat for fills that stretch or shrink to fit each bar.

Use that as a rule for your own changing meters: something marks where each bar begins, and the drummer marks where it ends.

## Superimposed meters on records

Moore's survey of meter in recorded song has a short list of rock precedents for what this page describes <Source book="moore12" where="Ch. 3, pp. 66–68" />:

- **The commonest case** is a three-unit figure repeating over 4/4 (a 3/8 melodic pattern against the kit), often in improvisation: Led Zeppelin's "Whole Lotta Love" and "Rock and Roll". Moore treats it as a form of syncopation: the short repeating pattern draws attention to itself.
- **3+3+2** is the oldest grouping dissonance in popular music, from ragtime and swing ("In the Mood") through the Beatles ("Here Comes the Sun") and Porcupine Tree to metal (Opeth, "The Grand Conjuration"). Rotated, it starts elsewhere in the bar; reversed (2+3+3), it's in Jethro Tull's "Sweet Dream".
- **Two meters at once in the band**: in King Crimson's "Red", Fripp's guitar keeps an odd /8 figure going over Bruford's 4/4. In the later part of Tool's "Lateralus", the vocal line runs in one meter over a kit pattern in another (Moore's example; the time signatures in the preview scan aren't legible, so count them yourself).
- **Fused sections** create meter changes too: songs assembled from separately written parts change meter at the joins (Queen's "Bohemian Rhapsody").

He also names the suspicion every prog band meets: that meter changes are "virtuosity for its own sake". His answer is to ask what the change does in the track. Gorillaz's "5/4" lays a 5-pattern over a normal 4/4 kit for a poetic point; the Rolling Stones' "Paint It Black" switches to relentless fast triplets late in the song to make it sound more menacing. Make sure each of your superimpositions has a job like that.

One distinction from Pieslak's work on Meshuggah is worth keeping apart from Krebs's labels <Source book="pieslak" where="pp. 222–225" />. A riff of 25 sixteenths repeated against 4/4 is a G25/16 dissonance only if it keeps going unchanged. If its last statement is lengthened or shortened so the block fills exactly eight bars, it's **mixed meter fitted to a square hypermeter**, and the dissonance resolves on schedule at every frame. Most of Meshuggah's odd meters up to 2002 work this way (see *Frame vs. cycle*).

## How to use it

<Steps>

- **Pick the layer that stays put.** In rock, Biamonte treats the backbeat as the consonant reference, the timeline against which tension is measured (see *Time feel*). If everything moves, nothing is dissonant, only confusing. On records, she notes, the steady bass and drums are often mixed louder when the meter gets dissonant.
- **Displace the melody, group the band.** In her survey, displacement dissonance usually stays in the main melodic layer (voice or lead guitar), because displacing several parts at once threatens the downbeat. Grouping dissonances, which realign periodically, are more often played by several parts together.
- **Choose the cycle length on purpose.** G4/3 (with a sixteenth pulse, it realigns every 3 beats) feels like a hemiola; G7/4 (every 7 beats, almost two bars) feels like drifting. The longer the cycle, the more the realignment works as a *landmark*, so put it where you want an arrival.
- **Displace for pressure, group for motion.** Displacement adds steady tension without changing the riff's shape. Grouping changes the riff's shape against the beat and creates a sense of rotation.
- **Accent with more than volume.** Any regular accent creates a layer: a long note, a low note, a thick chord, a riff restart, a cymbal choice. Your drummer can make a 3-layer with the ride bell while the kick and snare hold the 4.

</Steps>

<ListenerSide grade="moderate">

London argues that listeners hold only one meter at a time and hear the other layer as tension against it, never as a second meter (see *Odd meters*). Vuust & Witek describe meter as the brain's *prediction model*. Rhythms that **contradict the meter without undermining it** create engaging tension. Rhythms that overwhelm it make listeners lose the beat. A 3-against-4 cross-rhythm can flip between two readings, like the vase-and-faces image. Trained musicians can hold the counter-meter, but with effort. Keep one layer clear, and the dissonance will feel intended rather than lost.

</ListenerSide>
